The short answer

Almost certainly yes, it’s a process, not a barrier

Reviewed July 2026 · checked against the current published guidance of each New Zealand registration board

New Zealand needs experienced healthcare professionals, and most internationally qualified clinicians who come to us are eligible to register. The question is rarely whether you can work here, but what the steps are and when to start them.

Every regulated health profession in New Zealand is overseen by a registration board, under the Health Practitioners Competence Assurance Act. Their job is to confirm you’re qualified, competent and safe to practise, the same assurance you’d want of any colleague treating your own family. Once you’re registered and hold a current practising certificate, you can work.

Here’s the part people are relieved to hear: you don’t navigate this alone. We’ve guided clinicians through registration for every profession we place, and we’ll tell you honestly what your pathway looks like before you commit to anything, including the parts that take time.

“Can I work there?” is four questions, and registration is one of them

The board decides whether you can practise. Whether you can live and work in New Zealand at all is decided elsewhere, and each part has its own owner. This page is the first.

  • Registration — the right to practise. This page, then your board.
  • The visa — the right to live and work here, decided by Immigration New Zealand, not the board, and usually needing a job offer first. Immigration & visas owns the routes.
  • English, health and character — asked by the board and by immigration in slightly different forms. English requirements.
  • Time and money — months rather than weeks, with fees at each stage. How long it takes; what it costs sits in Make the numbers work.

The quickest first pass is Check my pathway: your profession, where you trained and what you already hold, and the route most likely to apply to you.

How registration works

Three things every board is checking

The professions differ in detail, but underneath, each regulator is satisfying itself of the same three things before it lets you practise.

Qualifications

You’re properly trained

Your degree and clinical training are assessed against New Zealand standards. For many professions, qualifications from comparable countries are recognised relatively smoothly; others ask for extra evidence or an assessment.

Competence

You’re safe and current

Boards look at your recent experience, references and good standing with your current regulator, confirming you’ve been practising and there’s nothing on your record that would raise concern.

Communication

You can communicate clearly

Because clear communication keeps patients safe, most boards require evidence of English language ability, usually IELTS or OET, unless you trained and practised in English in a recognised country.

The registration journey

What the process usually looks like

No two professions are identical, but most registrations move through the same broad stages. Knowing the shape of it makes the whole thing feel far less mysterious.

1

Check your eligibility

We look at your qualifications, experience and country of training together, and tell you honestly which pathway fits and what the board will expect. This is where most uncertainty disappears.

2

Gather your documents

Degree certificates, transcripts, proof of identity, references and a certificate of good standing from your current regulator. Some need to be certified or sent directly between bodies, so this stage rewards being organised early.

3

Sit any required English test

If you need to evidence English, you’ll book IELTS or OET. Many clinicians who trained and worked in English are exempt, we’ll confirm whether this applies to you before you spend a penny.

4

Submit your application

Your application and supporting evidence go to the board, with the relevant fee. From here it’s an assessment process, and a job offer often runs in parallel rather than waiting until the end.

5

Registration & scope of practice

The board confirms your registration and the scope you can practise within. Some professions begin under a period of supervision; others move straight to general registration.

6

Annual practising certificate

Registration is ongoing: you hold a current practising certificate, renewed each year, and keep up your professional development, exactly as you would at home.

A common question

English language requirements

This is the part people worry about most, often needlessly. If you trained and have practised in English in a recognised country, you may not need a test at all; where evidence is required, two tests are widely accepted.

The tests boards accept

Each board sets its own required scores, and the healthcare versions of these tests are designed around real clinical communication.

IELTSThe Academic module is the one boards look for. A strong overall score with no weak individual band is the usual shape of the requirement.
OETBuilt specifically for healthcare, with reading, writing, listening and speaking set in clinical scenarios. Many clinicians find it the more natural fit.

We’ll always check whether you’re exempt before recommending a test, and which test plays to your strengths if you do need one.

The honest part

What’s worth knowing before you start

None of these should put you off, but knowing them now is how you keep the process smooth instead of stressful.

1

Start earlier than feels necessary

Certified documents and certificates of good standing can take weeks to arrive. The single biggest cause of delay is starting late, so begin gathering paperwork as soon as you’re serious.

2

There are fees along the way

Registration applications, English tests and document certification each carry a cost. They’re manageable and predictable, but worth budgeting for from the outset rather than meeting as surprises.

3

Some pathways include supervision

Depending on your profession and training, you may begin under a period of supervised practice. It’s a normal, supportive runway into the New Zealand system, not a mark against you.

4

Registration and the job run together

You don’t have to be fully registered before you start applying for roles. Registration, a job offer and your visa typically progress alongside one another, which is exactly where we help keep things moving.

How long does it take?

An honest sense of the timeline

Every case is different, and boards work to their own timeframes, but as a rough guide, this is the shape of it for most people we work with.

Treat these as orientation rather than promises. Your profession, country of training and how quickly documents arrive all move the numbers, and getting started early is what keeps them at the shorter end.

Common questions

The things people ask us first

Do I need a job offer before I can register?

Usually not to begin the process, but the two are closely linked. For some professions a position or supervisor is part of the registration pathway, while for others you can register independently. In practice, registration, a job offer and your visa tend to move together, and that’s exactly the choreography we help manage so nothing stalls waiting on something else.

Will my overseas qualification be recognised?

For most clinicians from comparable healthcare systems, yes, though the route varies. Some qualifications are recognised directly; others are confirmed through an assessment, and occasionally a period of supervised practice. The honest answer depends on your profession and where you trained, which is the first thing we’ll look at with you.

Do I definitely have to sit an English test?

Not necessarily. Many clinicians who trained and have practised in English in a recognised country are exempt. Where evidence is required, IELTS (Academic) and OET are the widely accepted tests. We’ll check whether an exemption applies to you before you book anything, and if you do need a test, our partners at Swoosh English will help you prepare.

How much does registration cost?

Each board sets its own application and annual practising certificate fees, and there can be additional costs for English tests and certifying documents. None of it is prohibitive, but it adds up, so it’s worth budgeting for early. We’ll give you a realistic picture of the costs for your specific profession.

What if my situation is unusual?

Then it’s exactly the kind of thing to talk through with us. Career breaks, training in more than one country, a niche specialty or a less common qualification are all things we’ve helped people navigate before. There’s almost always a sensible path, it just helps to map it out with someone who’s seen it.
