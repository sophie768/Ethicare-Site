Onboarding is where a move succeeds or fails

Sophie CareemFounder, Ethicare Resourcing · former NHS change leader
7 min read

A signed contract is not the finish line. How we welcome internationally trained clinicians, and help them settle into a new way of working, is what decides whether they stay.

Most international recruitment stops paying attention at the arrivals gate. Registration, the visa, the flights, a first-week induction: all of it points at getting someone here. Then the real work begins, and too often the support quietly falls away. That first year, in a new country and a new health system, is where a move is won or lost.

I’ve watched this from both sides. I spent years inside the NHS planning the teams that patients depend on, and I’ve since helped clinicians move to New Zealand and Australia. The pattern is the same wherever people come from, whether that’s the UK, South Africa, the Gulf or beyond. People rarely leave because the clinical work was too hard. They leave because they never felt they belonged.

The NHS learned this the hard way

The NHS depends on internationally trained staff, and for a long time it recruited them far better than it looked after them. That has changed. Over recent years the health service has built real structure around welcoming people: named pastoral leads, buddy schemes that pair a new arrival with someone who has already made the move, phased inductions, and exam and language support. NHS England now runs a Pastoral Care Quality Award that holds employers to a standard for how they onboard international recruits.

None of it is glamorous. All of it works. The lesson underneath it is simple: the effort you put in around someone’s arrival is what turns a new hire into a colleague who stays.

Cultural difference is not a soft issue

When people talk about "cultural fit" it can sound vague, even a little suspect. It isn’t. The differences that trip people up are concrete, and they show up on day one. How feedback is given. Whether a junior is expected to question a senior. How much autonomy you’re handed, and how fast. The rhythm of a ward round, the paperwork, the small unspoken rules everyone else already knows.

On top of that sits the ordinary weight of a new life. Finding a home. Enrolling children in a school. Being a long flight from family. Learning a whole city from scratch. Clinical skill was never the problem. It’s everything around the clinical work that decides whether someone copes or comes undone.

What good looks like, and why it pays off

Good onboarding isn’t complicated. It’s just deliberate. Pair every new arrival with a buddy who has walked the same path. Give the team around them a short, honest brief, so the welcome runs both ways. Phase the first months so people aren’t dropped straight into a full load. Check in at three, six and twelve months, when the early goodwill has worn off and the homesickness is loudest. And support the family, not only the clinician, because if the family doesn’t settle, the clinician won’t either.

For an employer, this is not a nicety. A clinician who arrives prepared and feels genuinely welcomed stays longer, works more safely and lifts the team around them. Someone left to sink costs far more, in agency backfill, lost continuity and the quiet damage to a stretched team, than the modest effort it would have taken to support them well.

You can hand someone the perfect job and still lose them, if nobody helps them feel at home in it.

How we think about it at Ethicare

We carry these lessons into every move we make. Before anyone flies, we give an honest picture of the role, the system and the place, so no one arrives on a set of assumptions that don’t hold. After they land, we stay in touch through the wobbles that never appear in a job description. We deliberately take on fewer people, so we can give each of them more. It is the kind of support I wish someone had offered me when I made the move myself. For the health services we work with, it shows up in the one number that matters most: the people we place are still there years later.

The bottom line

Recruiting a clinician from overseas is both a privilege and a responsibility. We are asking someone to uproot their life for our patients. The least we can do is help them belong once they’re here. Onboarding done well is where a move succeeds. Done badly, or not at all, it’s where a good hire quietly slips away.
