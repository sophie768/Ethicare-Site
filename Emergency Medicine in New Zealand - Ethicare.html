An honest guide for overseas-trained specialists

The honest opening

Emergency medicine is the same everywhere. Working in it is not

The resus is the resus. What changes when you move here is everything around it — the size of the system, how far the nearest tertiary centre is, and who you can get on the phone at three in the morning.

New Zealand is a country of five million people with a single national health service. That one fact shapes more of the job than any list of benefits could. Pathways are national rather than negotiated hospital by hospital. The person you are referring to has probably trained with someone you know. And the distances are real: on a bad night, definitive care can be a helicopter away rather than a lift ride.

For some clinicians that is precisely the appeal. A broader case mix, more procedural ownership, and decisions that stay yours rather than being deferred up a chain. For others it is the hardest adjustment of the move, and it is better to know which you are before you accept anything.

What consistently surprises people is the culture rather than the medicine. Departments here tend to be flatter. Consultants are on the floor. First names are normal, including yours. Nobody is impressed by working through a break, and being visibly exhausted is treated as a rota problem rather than a badge.

None of that makes the work easy. Winter is busy, crowding is real, and the same access-block conversations happen here as anywhere. But the scale means you are closer to the people who can change something about it.

The question is not whether you can do emergency medicine here. It is whether this particular version of it is the one you want for the next ten years.

Where the work actually is

Four quite different jobs, all called emergency medicine

The title travels. The day does not. It is worth being specific with yourself about which of these you are actually after.

  • 01

    A tertiary department in a main centre

    Full specialty support on site, high volumes, trauma, and the subspecialty interests and research time that come with a large department. Closest to what most overseas specialists are leaving, and the easiest transition.

  • 02

    A regional or base hospital

    Broader case mix, more procedural autonomy, and a genuine role in deciding what stays and what transfers. Fewer colleagues, more responsibility, and the single biggest jump in scope for most people.

  • 03

    Retrieval and pre-hospital

    Road and air, often alongside a departmental role rather than instead of one. Geography makes this a real part of the system here rather than a niche interest.

  • 04

    Teaching, simulation and clinical governance

    A smaller system means these roles are reachable earlier in a career than many people expect. If you want to shape how a department works, that is more available here than the size of the country suggests.

Registration, plainly

The route to practising as a specialist

Five steps, and the order matters more than the paperwork. Only the Medical Council can confirm your own route — nothing here is a decision.

  1. 01

    Your college assesses your training

    Specialist registration in New Zealand is vocational registration, and the specialist college compares your training against the New Zealand programme in your specialty. The college’s view is what the Council acts on.

  2. 02

    Check your qualification against the published list

    The Council publishes the qualifications it accepts for each specialty. UK, Irish and Australasian qualifications are commonly on those lists; others are looked at case by case. Confirm yours before you plan around it.

  3. 03

    Recent practice matters more than total years

    A significant recent gap usually means the college wants to see a supervised period first. That is a route, not a refusal.

  4. 04

    The job and the registration run together

    Most specialist registration is attached to a specific post, so you cannot complete it in the abstract. Everyone new to the country gets a period of oversight while they settle — it is not a judgement on your practice.

  5. 05

    Documents are the slow part, every time

    Verified qualifications, EPIC verification, and certificates of good standing from every regulator you have held. Most expire around three months after issue, so sequence them rather than collecting them early.

The part a recruiter usually leaves out

The trade-offs worth knowing now

None of these are reasons not to come. Knowing them before you accept is how you avoid discovering them in your second winter.

  • 01

    A smaller system means fewer posts

    Fewer departments, fewer employers, and a smaller professional circle. If your next decade depends on a particular subspecialty niche, check it exists here before anything else.

  • 02

    Pay is set by an agreement, not negotiated

    Senior medical pay in the public system sits on the collective agreement scale rather than being agreed case by case. That means transparency, and it also means less room to negotiate than you may be used to.

  • 03

    Distance is the thing families feel

    Flights home are long and expensive. This is consistently the hardest part of the move, and it is worth talking about honestly before you commit rather than after.

  • 04

    Crowding did not stay behind

    Winter is busy, access block is real, and the same pressures exist here. The difference is scale, not immunity.
