Practising in Australia

The system, the people and the philosophy of care you would be joining — and why the state and the funder matter as much as the job title.

The system at a glance

Four things about how Australian healthcare is funded and organised that change how your working week feels — whichever profession you hold.

Kettle on. A longer read — about 11 min

Medicare, the public and private split and the PBS are explained properly in how healthcare works in Australia. This is the clinician’s summary of it.

Who pays

Five funders, not one

Medicare, the state or territory health service, the disability scheme, private health insurance and the person themselves. Which one is paying shapes your caseload, your paperwork and how much intervention you have available.

The structural one

One registration, eight systems

Registration is national, so you can move states without starting again. Public hospitals are not: each state and territory runs its own services on its own enterprise agreements. The job travels; the terms and conditions do not.

The scale of private

Bigger than most systems

A very large share of care — particularly imaging, allied health, mental health and elective surgery — happens in private and community practice on Medicare-funded referrals rather than inside a hospital.

Distance

A clinical factor, not a lifestyle one

Australia is the size of Europe with a fraction of the population. Outreach, fly-in services, retrieval and telehealth are ordinary practice rather than specialist interests.

Worth understanding before you search

Ask which state, and ask who funds the role. Two jobs with the same title in Perth and in Hobart are genuinely different jobs on different agreements, and a Medicare-funded private caseload is a different working life from a state-run hospital one.

Nothing in a job advert makes either obvious. Ask us — we find out before you apply.

What matters here

Five things the system expects you to work to. None is unique to Australia, but they carry more weight here than most clinicians are used to — in position descriptions, interviews and appraisals.

Equity, not equality

Equality is giving everyone the same. Equity is giving people what they need to reach the same outcome — and here the distance between the two is measured, published and acted on, most visibly through Closing the Gap.

In practiceExtra time, an interpreter or outreach for the person who needs it is the standard here, not favouritism.

The person, and their family

Shared goals, real choice, informed decisions — and family in the room by default rather than by invitation.

In practiceWrite goals in the person’s own words — the plan that means something to them is the one that gets followed.

Multidisciplinary by default

Teams run flat. You are expected to know what the other professions contribute, and to ask when something sits outside your scope.

In practiceBeing new is not a reason to stay quiet in a case conference.

Prevention and keeping people out of hospital

Avoiding an admission counts as an outcome in its own right, and a great deal of funding follows care delivered in the community rather than on a ward.

In practiceHospital-avoidance targets and community contracts will shape your caseload from week one.

Ageing, disability and long-term conditions

Demand is shifting towards rehabilitation, chronic conditions, aged care and disability support. That is where the growth in most professions sits, and where a large share of the funding now goes.

In practiceMore of your week is about function and independence than cure — and the funding follows it.

Culture and care

Australia holds the oldest continuing cultures on earth, and is also one of the most multicultural countries in the world. Both facts land on your caseload, and nobody expects you to arrive expert.

The history and the culture are covered properly in community and culture in Australia. Here is what they mean in a clinic by your second week.

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have cared for this continent for more than 60,000 years. Colonisation and its continuing effects are part of the clinical picture, not a historical aside — and health outcomes remain measurably unequal.

Connection to family, community, culture and Country is understood here as part of health rather than context around it.

The culture you would be joining

Closing the Gap

The national agreement to close the gaps in life expectancy, health and wellbeing outcomes. You will hear the term constantly, in service plans, funding and appraisals.

At a caseload level it asks a practical question: what would equitable access to your profession actually require for someone living hundreds of kilometres from the nearest service?

What that looks like in remote practice

Community-controlled services

Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations deliver care designed and governed by the communities they serve, usually with family and Country at the centre of the model rather than at its edge.

You will refer to them, work alongside them, and in some roles be employed by them.

Where they sit, by state
Cultural safety, plainly

Clinical competence is performing the technique correctly. Cultural safety is the patient’s judgement of whether your care was safe for them — which is why you cannot self-certify it, and why it sits in the codes of conduct rather than in an induction folder.

Day to day it is unremarkable good practice: learn whose Country your service stands on, take the cultural induction seriously, ask who should be part of the conversation, explain before you touch, notice when someone has gone quiet, and accept correction without defending yourself. Nobody expects fluency on arrival. People do notice effort.

Acknowledgement of Country opens the meeting. Take it as clinical business, not ceremony.

Where you practise changes the job

More than the job title does, and more than in almost any other health system. Metropolitan Melbourne and a remote community in the Northern Territory are the same registration and not remotely the same work.

Regional, rural and remote Australia

Workforce shortages, very long distances, outreach and fly-in services, and a scope of practice far broader than the metropolitan version of your job. This is where the most autonomy, often the best pay, and the country’s most persistent workforce need all sit — and we would rather be honest with you about the professional isolation and the distance from family than have you discover both in month three.

Often true: one contact instead of six · a broader caseload · decisions you make rather than escalate · telehealth as core practice · a community that knows exactly who you are

Metropolitan health services

Subspecialty depth, teaching, research and the largest teams, concentrated in the capitals and carrying the state’s tertiary and quaternary work.

Private and community practice

Medicare-funded referrals, booked lists, commercial throughput and often the newest equipment. A genuine career route here rather than a fallback, and a large employer in several professions.

Disability and aged care

Work funded through the disability scheme or aged care rather than through health, focused on function, capacity and daily living rather than episodes of treatment.

Community-controlled and outreach services

Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations and regional outreach programmes, where your profession sits inside a wider model of care and cultural safety is the clinical standard.

The state you land in

Enterprise agreements, progression, rostering and how services are structured are all set state by state. Your registration moves with you; your pay and conditions are renegotiated by geography.

The funder behind the role

Medicare, state health, the disability scheme, private insurance or an employer. Each buys a different job under the same professional title — different caseload, different documentation, different amount of time per person.

What might feel different

What catches experienced clinicians out here is rarely clinical. It is the structure around the work.

These are the differences every profession meets. The version specific to your discipline sits on your own profession’s page.

Often here

Often here

Decisions are escalated for approval.

Often here

You make the call and justify it afterwards, and the further from a capital city you work, the more true that becomes. Most clinicians name this as the best part of the job and the hardest month of the first year.

Often here

None of this is expected of you on day one.

But the clinicians who settle fastest understood the system before they signed — exactly the conversation we would rather have with you first.

The first months

Australian services are used to internationally qualified clinicians arriving, and most describe the start as a runway rather than a cold plunge. This is what the runway usually contains.

A proper induction

A structured orientation to the service, its systems and its documentation before you carry a full load. In public services it is formal and measured in days, not an hour, and the funder-specific paperwork from the previous section is learned here rather than before you arrive.

Supervision, where your registration carries it

Depending on your profession and route, registration can come with a period of supervised practice attached. It is a registration condition, not a judgement on your experience. Whether it applies to you, and for how long, is on your profession page and in the registration guide.

The culture, close up

First names, open communication and multidisciplinary teamwork are the norm, and clinicians arriving from more hierarchical systems often describe care as a touch less rushed. Not universal — healthcare is healthcare — but it is the common experience.

One registration, wherever you go next

Registration is national. Once you are settled you can develop, specialise or move between states without starting the paperwork again — though pay and terms change at the border, as the previous section says.

Three to six months is the usual figure before the systems, the shorthand and the referral pathways stop taking conscious effort.

The wobble that tends to arrive around the third month is rarely about the work. What we do in those months is set out once, on the About page; the life around the job has its own guides, community and culture and moving with your family.

Before you arrive

Five things worth doing while your registration and visa are in progress. None takes more than an evening, and all of them get noticed.

Learn whose Country you are moving to

The traditional owners of the place you will work, and how to say the name. It takes an evening, it will come up in your first week, and getting it wrong without having tried is what people notice.

Read the community and culture guide

Once before you fly and once after your first fortnight — it reads differently the second time. Community and culture in Australia

Understand Medicare before your interview

How it funds care, what bulk billing means and where the private system sits. Knowing it signals you have done more than skim a job advert. How healthcare works here

Ask which state agreement covers the role

And what step you would start on. Two offers with the same headline title can be materially different once the agreement is on the table, and this is the question candidates most often forget to ask.

Decide what the move is for

Adventure, scope, pay, family — the honest answer changes which state, which setting and which roles we should be showing you.

One honest boundary. We are not migration agents and we are not your regulator — those decisions sit with the board and with the Department of Home Affairs. What we do is explain, sequence and keep your file moving, and tell you plainly what we do not know.

And make sure the system holds no surprises before your family is committed to it.
