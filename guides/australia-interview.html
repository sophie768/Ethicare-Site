Start here

A clear, evidence-based CV

Australian healthcare CVs are clean and factual: what you can do and what you have done, backed by evidence, rather than a long personal statement. A busy manager should see your fit in under a minute.

A few things employers here look for that may differ from where you trained:

  • Your registration or accreditation status up front, registered, in progress, or eligible.
  • Clinical competencies and modalities stated plainly, with caseload or volume where it helps.
  • Reverse-chronological roles with dates, settings and responsibilities, with gaps explained briefly.
  • No photo, date of birth or marital status, they’re not expected and best left off.

We help every candidate shape a CV that reads naturally to Australian employers, not by changing your experience, but by framing it the way the people hiring expect to see it.

Practical

What goes in, section by section

There’s no official format, but there is a shape that Australian hiring managers are used to reading. Follow it and your experience does the talking instead of your layout.

1

Name, contact, registration status

Top of page one. Whether you’re registered, in progress or eligible is often the first thing a manager looks for, so don’t bury it on page three.

2

A short professional summary

Three or four lines: your profession, years of experience, the settings you’ve worked in and what you’re looking for. Not a personal statement, a précis.

3

Skills and modalities

Stated plainly, ideally with an indication of volume or independence. “Independent reporting across x and y” tells a manager more than a list of nouns.

4

Roles, most recent first

Employer type rather than brand (many managers won’t know the hospital), setting, dates, and what you were actually responsible for. Brief explanations for any gaps.

5

Qualifications and CPD

Your qualifying award, where and when, then relevant post-qualification training. Recent CPD signals someone who keeps current.

6

References, on request

Two is standard, ideally including a recent clinical supervisor. Ask them before you list them, and warn them about the time difference.

Learned the hard way

The mistakes we see most

None of these are about competence. They’re about a CV or an interview not showing the competence that’s already there.

What helps

  • Naming your registration status in the first few lines
  • Describing settings and systems, not just job titles
  • Two or three specific stories you can tell naturally
  • Being clear about what you want next, and why Australia
  • Explaining a career gap briefly and without apology
  • Asking your own questions at the end

What quietly costs you

  • A photo, date of birth or marital status, leave them off
  • Assuming a manager knows your hospital, region or grading system
  • Untranslated local acronyms and banding shorthand
  • Ten pages when four would do, or one page when four are needed
  • Rehearsed answers that don’t quite answer the question asked
  • Treating “why Australia?” as small talk, it’s a real question
What to expect

How interviews tend to run

Interviews for overseas candidates are usually done by video, and are often more conversational than formal. Knowing the shape of it takes most of the nerves away.

Format

Often a video call

A panel of one to three people, typically a manager and a senior clinician, by Teams or Zoom, allowing for the time difference. Usually one or two stages.

Tone

Conversational

Friendly and on first-name terms. They’re assessing competence, but also whether you’ll be a good colleague, so it’s as much a two-way conversation as a test.

Focus

Real situations

Expect questions grounded in practice: how you handled something, how you work in a team, how you keep patients safe, rather than abstract puzzles.

The week before

Preparing for the call

A video interview across time zones has a few practical failure modes that have nothing to do with your clinical ability. Close those off and you can concentrate on the conversation.

1

Confirm the time twice

In their time zone and yours, in writing. Australia spans several zones and only some observe daylight saving, which catches people out every year.

2

Test the link, not just the laptop

Open the actual meeting link a day early. Check camera, microphone, and that whatever’s behind you is something you’re happy for a panel to look at.

3

Prepare three stories, not thirty answers

One about a difficult clinical situation, one about a team or a disagreement, one about improving something. Most behavioural questions can be answered from those three.

4

Know the service you’re applying to

Not trivia, just context: what kind of service it is, the population it serves, and where your role sits in it. We’ll brief you on this properly beforehand.

5

Have your own questions written down

Nerves erase them. A short list on paper, next to you, is completely acceptable and makes you look considered rather than unprepared.

6

Plan for the connection dropping

Agree a fallback in advance: a phone number to call, or who reconnects first. It happens often enough to be worth a sentence beforehand.

Be ready for these

Questions to expect

You won’t be able to predict every question, but most fall into a few families. Have a couple of real examples ready for each, the "tell me about a time…" style rewards specifics.

Clinical & technical

Your scope, the modalities or cases you’re confident with, how you stay current, and how you’d handle an unfamiliar situation safely.

Teamwork & communication

Working in a multidisciplinary team, raising a concern, handling a difficult interaction with a colleague or patient.

You & the move

Why Australia, why this role, and whether you’ve thought the relocation through, they want to know you’ll stay and settle.

Worth rehearsing out loud: “Tell me about a time something went wrong and what you did.” “How do you raise a concern about a colleague’s practice?” “What would you do on a shift where you were asked to work outside your scope?” “Why Australia, and why now?” Four questions, in some form, come up in most interviews we sit in on.

It goes both ways

Questions worth asking them

You’re deciding too, and an interview where you ask nothing reads as an interview where you didn’t care. These also happen to be the questions whose answers matter most once you’ve arrived.

About the work

What does a typical week look like? What’s the case mix? How is the team staffed at the moment, and what’s the plan? What does support look like in the first few months?

About progression

How do people develop here? Is there protected time for CPD? What have previous international clinicians in this team gone on to do?

About arriving

What relocation support exists? Who helps with the practical side of landing? Is there anyone in the team who’s made the same move?

The thing that sets candidates apart

Showing you’ll fit the team

Australian healthcare is collaborative and patient-centred, with a genuine emphasis on respectful, culturally safe care. Employers hire people who’ll add to that, not just tick the clinical boxes.

You don’t need to perform, just be ready to show, with examples, that you communicate openly, work flatly within a team, ask when you’re unsure, and treat patients and colleagues with respect. A little awareness of the local context (that you’ve thought about practising in a new system and culture) goes a long way.

And then?

What happens after the interview

The waiting is the part nobody prepares you for. Here’s the honest shape of it, so silence doesn’t get read as bad news.

Decisions rarely come the same day. A panel usually needs to reconvene, and public services often have an approval step after that, so a few days to a couple of weeks is normal rather than ominous. We chase on your behalf, and we’ll tell you what we’re hearing even when what we’re hearing is “not yet”.

If an offer comes, read it properly before you celebrate: the role and its level, hours, on-call expectations, start date, and what relocation support is included. Ask about anything unclear now, because it’s far harder to raise once you’ve accepted. And if the answer is no, ask for feedback. It’s usually given generously here, and it’s often about timing or scope rather than you.

One thing we’d say plainly: don’t resign anything, or give notice on a home, until your offer and your visa are both real. We’ll tell you when that point has arrived.

Sophie’s insight

The clinicians who interview well are rarely the ones with the slickest answers, they’re the ones who’ve prepared two or three honest, specific stories and can talk about them naturally. We’ll do a proper prep call before any interview: the likely questions, the panel, the role, and the small cultural things that make a difference. You’ll walk in knowing what to expect, which is most of the battle.

Common questions

The things people ask us first

How long should my CV be?

For most clinical roles, two to four pages is right: long enough to evidence your experience and modalities, short enough to stay readable. We’ll help you trim or expand it to suit the role and your seniority.

Will the interview be in person?

Almost never for an overseas candidate, they’re done by video, scheduled around the time difference. We’ll make sure you’re set up and comfortable with the format beforehand.

What if I’m asked something I don’t know?

Say so, and say how you’d find out or who you’d ask. In Australian healthcare, knowing your limits and seeking help is seen as a strength, not a weakness, it’s exactly the safe behaviour they’re looking for.

Do you help with references?

Yes. We’ll guide you on who to ask and when, since references and certificates of good standing are often on the critical path for both the job and your registration.

Should I explain a gap in my CV?

Yes, briefly and without apology. Parental leave, caring, study, illness, a move, all completely normal. A one-line explanation stops a reader guessing, and guessing is worse than the truth in almost every case.

Can I ask about pay in the interview?

You can, though for public roles the answer is usually set by an agreement rather than negotiable, so there’s often less to discuss than you’d expect. It’s generally easier to let us handle the pay conversation while you concentrate on the role.

What if I interview badly on the day?

Tell us honestly afterwards. Sometimes there’s a second stage where it can be put right, sometimes there’s another role that suits better. What doesn’t help is pretending it went well, we can only advocate for you properly if we know what actually happened.

How many roles should I apply for at once?

Fewer, better-matched applications beat a scattergun. Two or three genuine possibilities is plenty to manage alongside registration and a visa, and it means every interview gets proper preparation.
