The practical part

A big move is just a lot of small, doable things

This is the stage where it all becomes real, and the one people describe as most overwhelming. A move is never one enormous task — it is a long list of small ones.

So this step lays it out as a countdown: what to think about months ahead, what to do in the final weeks, and what waits for your first days on the ground. Work through it at your own pace, tick things off as you go, and let the list carry the worry so you don’t have to.

And you’re not project-managing this alone. We’ve helped many families make exactly this move, and we’re on hand for the questions the checklists don’t answer, plus introductions to the relocation and shipping partners we trust.

Start with the big rocks

Three things to sort first

If you get these three moving early, everything else fits around them. They take the longest and matter the most, so they’re worth your attention before the smaller details.

A roof

Somewhere to land

You don’t need your forever home before you fly, just a sorted first few weeks. Temporary or short-term rental accommodation buys you time to find the right place once you’re here.

Money

Banking & funds

Opening a New Zealand bank account and planning how you’ll move your money across are jobs best started early: some banks let you begin the process before you arrive.

Children

Schools & childcare

If you’re moving with children, school enrolment and zones shape where you’ll want to live. Researching this early often decides the neighbourhood for you.

A countdown to departure

What to do, and roughly when

Timeframes shift with your circumstances, but this is the broad rhythm most moves follow. Treat it as a guide to keep things from piling up at the end, not a set of hard deadlines.

3+ months out

Lay the groundwork

  • Research regions and neighbourhoods, guided by work and schools.
  • Begin school enrolment enquiries and check zones.
  • Get quotes from international shipping and relocation companies.
  • Start gathering documents: passports, certificates, references, medical and school records.
  • Plan the money side: how and when you’ll transfer funds.
1–2 months out

Book & arrange

  • Book flights and your first accommodation for arrival.
  • Confirm your shipping or air-freight, and decide what comes and what goes.
  • Arrange travel and contents insurance for the move.
  • Notify schools, landlords, banks and utilities at home of your leaving date.
  • Sort pets early if they’re coming, it has its own long timeline.
Final weeks

Tie off loose ends

  • Close or redirect accounts, subscriptions and mail.
  • Pack a "first fortnight" bag: essentials you’ll want before shipping arrives.
  • Keep all key documents together in your hand luggage.
  • Confirm someone can meet you or your accommodation is ready on arrival.
  • Say your goodbyes, and let yourself feel them.
First days here

Get set up

  • Open or activate your bank account and get an IRD number for tax.
  • Get a local SIM and sort your phone and internet.
  • Enrol the children and register with a local doctor (GP).
  • Look into transport, a car, or public transport options.
  • Breathe. The hardest part is now behind you.

Setting up home

Furnishing your home affordably

There is a common worry that you need to arrive with a fully furnished home in mind, or kit one out from scratch. Most healthcare professionals build theirs gradually: a few essentials first, the rest as they settle.

New Zealand has a well-established second-hand market, and it’s often possible to furnish an entire home for a fraction of the cost of buying new. Online marketplaces, charity stores and used-furniture retailers are especially popular in the first few months, while you find your feet and work out what you actually need.

Buying second-hand

Where to look

  • Facebook Marketplace, the busiest place for local, near-new pieces
  • Trade Me — New Zealand’s big online marketplace
  • Habitat for Humanity ReStores
  • City Mission stores
  • Salvation Army Family Stores
  • Hospice shops

Most regions also have well-loved used-furniture retailers, ask a local or your new colleagues for the nearest one.

Buying new on a budget

Value-for-money retailers

  • Mocka
  • Early Settler
  • Furniture Clearance Centre

These stores often offer starter furniture packages at gentler prices than premium retailers, handy for the few pieces you’d rather buy new.

Ethicare insight

One of the biggest misconceptions we hear is that you need a fully furnished home ready on day one. In reality, building it gradually tends to save money and gives you the chance to create a home that reflects your own style. Rather than rushing into expensive purchases before you even know which neighbourhood you’ll stay in.

Moving with children

Settling the family in

If you’re moving as a family, the children’s transition matters as much as any logistics. New Zealand schools are welcoming and used to new arrivals, and children tend to find their feet quickly.

Schools & zones

State schools are generally free for residents, and enrolment is often tied to where you live, so school choice and housing go hand in hand. Start enquiries early and check zones before you commit to an address.

The records you’ll need

Bring school reports, immunisation records and any learning-support paperwork. Having your children’s history to hand makes enrolment and the right placement much smoother on arrival.

Easing the change

Involve children in the move, talk openly about what’s changing, and expect a settling-in period. An outdoor, friendly school culture usually does the rest faster than parents dare to hope.

Sophie’s insight

The move itself is the part people lose the most sleep over, and almost always it goes more smoothly than the dread suggests. My honest advice: don’t ship more than you need, and don’t try to arrive with everything perfect. Sort a soft landing, a place to stay, a bank account, a SIM card, and let the rest come together in the first few weeks. The families who travel light, in their heads as much as their boxes, are the ones who arrive ready to enjoy it.

SophieCEO & Founder, Ethicare Resourcing

The honest part

What’s worth knowing before you pack

A few realities that catch people out: none of them dealbreakers, all of them easier when you see them coming.

1

Shipping takes longer than you think

Sea freight can take many weeks to arrive. Pack a generous "first weeks" bag and plan to live for a while. Your container won’t be there to greet you.

2

Biosecurity is taken seriously

New Zealand has strict rules on bringing in food, plants, wooden items and used outdoor equipment. Declare everything and clean your gear: fines for getting it wrong are real.

3

The first weeks cost more

Bonds, rent in advance, a car, setting up a home, the early outlay adds up before your first pay lands. A cash buffer takes a lot of pressure off those opening weeks.

4

Goodbyes hit harder than logistics

Amid the admin, the emotional side of leaving can ambush you. Give it room, plan how you’ll stay in touch, and be gentle with yourself and the family.

Common questions

The things people ask us first

Should I find a permanent home before I arrive?

Usually not: most people rent somewhere short-term to begin with and choose their longer-term home once they’re on the ground. It’s hard to judge a neighbourhood from the other side of the world, and arriving with a soft landing booked takes the pressure off making a big decision sight-unseen. Sort the first few weeks, then take your time.

Do most rental properties come with appliances?

An oven and cooktop are almost always included, and a rangehood is common. Beyond that, don’t assume: a fridge, washing machine and dishwasher are often not provided, which surprises people coming from places where white goods are standard. Many rentals are let unfurnished, with appliances varying property to property, so always check the listing or ask the landlord exactly what’s included. If you need to buy, the second-hand options in our furnishing guide above make it quick and affordable to sort.

What is the quality of rental houses like in New Zealand?

Honestly, it varies. Newer builds are warm, dry and well insulated, but some older homes can feel colder and damper than newcomers expect. Central heating is far less common than in the UK, and many homes rely on a heat pump and good sun instead. The good news is that all rentals must now meet the government’s Healthy Homes Standards, which set legal minimums for heating, insulation, ventilation, moisture control and draught-stopping.

When you’re viewing, look for insulation, a heat pump or other fixed heating, double glazing where possible, and which way the home faces, a north-facing home gets far more winter sun here. It’s perfectly reasonable to ask for the property’s Healthy Homes compliance statement before you sign.

Can I get a mobile phone plan straight away?

Yes, a prepaid SIM is the quickest route, available the day you arrive from any provider (One NZ, Spark, 2degrees or the budget brand Skinny) with just your passport. A pay-monthly plan can require a New Zealand bank account and a little local credit history, so many people start on prepaid and switch to a contract once their banking is set up. Coverage is strong in the cities and main towns; if you’re heading somewhere rural, it’s worth checking the provider’s coverage map.

How much should I ship?

Less than you’d instinctively pack. Furniture and bulky items are often not worth the freight, while sentimental and hard-to-replace things usually are. Many people send a modest sea-freight shipment and bring essentials by air, then buy the rest here. Get a few quotes early and let the cost guide your cull.

Can I sort banking and tax before I land?

Partly. Some banks let you begin opening an account from overseas, which is worth doing. Your IRD number for tax is generally sorted once you’ve arrived and have a bank account and visa in place. Getting both done early in your first days matters, without an IRD number you’ll be taxed at a higher rate until it’s resolved.

What about bringing pets?

It’s very doable, but it has one of the longest lead times of anything in the move, with import rules, vaccinations and quarantine to arrange. If a pet is coming, start that process as early as you possibly can and consider a specialist pet-relocation company, it’s not something to leave to the final weeks.

Can you help with the move itself?

We’re not a removals firm, but we’ve helped enough families through this to know who is worth trusting. We’re happy to introduce you to relocation, shipping and settling-in partners we rate, and to answer the in-between questions that no checklist quite covers. You won’t be left to work it all out alone.
