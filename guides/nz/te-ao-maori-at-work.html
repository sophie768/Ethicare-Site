The Auckland guide

Te reo Māori is woven through everyday New Zealand life, and in healthcare it arrives with something more: a Treaty relationship that shapes how care is designed, measured and delivered. None of this asks you to arrive expert. It does ask you to arrive willing.

Kettle on. A longer read — about 6 min

Te Tiriti o Waitangi, in practice

Signed in 1840 between Māori and the Crown, Te Tiriti is the founding agreement of the country. The history sits in About New Zealand — what matters on your first shift is what the Treaty relationship asks of you as a clinician, because your professional body will assess you against it.

Partnership

Working with Māori patients, whānau and communities rather than delivering to them. Decisions made together, and plans the whānau has genuinely agreed to.

In practiceThe person answering your questions may not be the person who decides. Ask who should be part of the conversation before you start, not after the plan is written.

Participation

Māori involvement at every level — in their own care, and in how services are designed and run. Kaupapa Māori providers deliver care by Māori, for Māori, and you will refer to them and work alongside them.

In practiceIf a service is not reaching a group, that is treated as a service problem to solve — not a patient problem to record as non-attendance.

Protection

Actively protecting Māori health and wellbeing, and closing outcome gaps that are well documented and long-standing.

In practiceEquity is not equality. Two people with the same diagnosis may need different amounts of your time, and giving it to them is the standard rather than favouritism.

Te Whare Tapa Whā

A Māori model of health you are likely to meet in your first week: wellbeing as a house standing on four walls, on the whenua beneath it. If one wall is weak the house is unstable — which is a more useful way to think about the person in front of you than a problem list is.

PhysicalTaha tinana

The body — pain, function, mobility, strength, nutrition. What most of us were trained to assess first.

Mind & emotionsTaha hinengaro

Thoughts, feelings and mood. Rarely separable from a clinical outcome, and increasingly assessed alongside it.

Family & socialTaha whānau

Family and belonging. Who someone is caring for, and who is caring for them, is clinical information.

Spirit & meaningTaha wairua

Purpose, identity and meaning — what the person is actually trying to get back to.

Land & rootsWhenua

Connection to place and to whakapapa: the ground the four walls stand on. A person’s home, land and community are part of their health rather than the backdrop to it.

Used well this is not an extra form to complete. It is a prompt that a goal recorded as “increase shoulder range to 140°” may matter far less to the person than being able to lift a grandchild, or to stand and speak at a family gathering.

Cultural safety, plainly

Clinical competence is performing the technique correctly. Cultural safety is the patient’s judgement of whether your care was safe for them — which is why you cannot self-certify it, and why it is assessed as an ongoing competency rather than completed as an induction module.

In day-to-day terms it is unremarkable good practice. Nobody expects fluency on arrival. People do notice effort.

  • Learn to pronounce names, place names and the local iwi correctly — and ask if you are unsure.
  • Ask who should be part of the conversation before you begin, rather than after the plan is written.
  • Offer whānau a seat. Assume they are staying unless told otherwise.
  • Explain what you are about to do to someone’s body before you do it.
  • Notice when someone has gone quiet, and do not read silence as agreement.
  • Do not assume the patient is the decision-maker, or that they are not.
  • Record goals in the person’s own words, not translated into clinical language.
  • Accept correction without defending yourself. It is offered as help.

On a marae

You may be welcomed onto a marae for a service opening, a team day or a pōwhiri. You will be guided throughout and nothing is expected of you beyond following your host’s lead.

The short version

  • Wait to be called on. Do not walk ahead of the group.
  • Remove your shoes before entering the wharenui.
  • No food or drink inside the wharenui, and do not sit on tables or pillows.
  • Dress tidily and modestly; keep phones away and silent.
  • A hongi — the pressing of noses and foreheads — is a greeting, not a kiss. Follow the lead offered to you.
  • Koha is a contribution, usually collected by the group rather than by individuals.
  • Speeches take the time they take. That is the point of them.

Saying it right

A quick guide to pronunciation
  • Vowels are pure and consistent: a = "ah", e = "eh", i = "ee", o = "aw", u = "oo" — every time.
  • "Wh" sounds like "f" — so whānau is "fah-now".
  • "R" is lightly rolled, closer to a soft "d" than an English r.
  • Macrons (ā ē ī ō ū) simply lengthen the vowel — Māori is "Maa-ori".
  • Place names reward a go: Taupō is "Toe-paw", Whangārei is "Fong-ah-ray". Locals value the effort far more than perfection.
