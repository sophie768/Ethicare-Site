Welcome to Wellington

Kia ora, and welcome to the capital

Wellington — Te Whanganui-a-Tara — is New Zealand’s capital, and the city people tend to fall for fastest. Wrapped around a dramatic harbour and built between the hills and the sea, it’s famously compact, walkable and creative: a place of cafés, culture and government that locals like to call the coolest little capital in the world.

What makes it easy to settle here is the scale. You can walk from Parliament to the waterfront to a hillside café in twenty minutes, so a new city stops feeling foreign within weeks. New Zealanders are known for their warmth and manaakitanga — hospitality and care for others — and Wellington folds newcomers into its arts, food and neighbourhood life quickly.

This guide is written to make your move smooth and unhurried — from the health system, IRD numbers and KiwiSaver to the suburbs, the school zones and where to spend a clear, calm weekend. Think of it as the conversation we’d have over a flat white, in your pocket for whenever you need it.

Nau mai, haere mai ki Aotearoa — welcome to New Zealand. Pack a good jacket for the wind; the rest, we’ll help you with.

Overview · Culture

Capital of culture

As the seat of government and home to the national museum Te Papa, Wellington carries an outsized cultural life — film, theatre, festivals and a café culture the locals take seriously.

The city is the centre of New Zealand’s film industry, with Weta Workshop and Wellington’s studios behind some of cinema’s best-known work. Te Whanganui-a-Tara has a deep Māori history and a creative, progressive identity reflected in its festivals, galleries and laneway bars.

The magic is the compactness: you can walk from Parliament to the waterfront to a hillside café in twenty minutes, and stumble into a gig, a gallery or the country’s best coffee on the way.

Overview · Transport

Getting around

Here’s how people get around Wellington day to day — and what to know about driving in New Zealand when you first arrive.

Walking

The compact CBD means many residents walk to work — one of the city’s great everyday advantages.

Trains & buses

The Metlink network of trains and buses connects the city with the Hutt Valley and Kāpiti — tap on with a Snapper card.

Ferries

Harbour ferries and the Cook Strait ferry to the South Island depart from the city — the classic way south.

Air

Wellington Airport links the capital to domestic and trans-Tasman destinations, a short hop from the CBD.

Section two · Healthcare

Healthcare in Wellington

Health New Zealand | Te Whatu Ora delivers care across the capital region, anchored by Wellington Regional Hospital — a major tertiary centre for the lower North Island and upper South Island, and a deep base of clinical work.

Wellington Regional Hospital (Newtown)

A major tertiary teaching hospital with acute, surgical and specialist services, serving the lower North Island and beyond.

Hutt Hospital (Lower Hutt)

Serves the Hutt Valley with acute, medical, surgical and maternity services.

Kenepuru (Porirua)

Provides community, elective and outpatient services north of the city.

Private & ACC

Private hospital options such as Wakefield and Bowen sit alongside the public system; ACC covers accidents for everyone.

Public & private
·Public healthcare is funded through general taxation
·ACC provides no-fault accident cover for everyone
·Private hospitals offer elective procedures and shorter waits
·Many residents hold private insurance alongside the public system
Healthcare · Everyday care

GPs, pharmacies & urgent care

Registering with a GP & dentist

Once you settle in, enrol with a local GP. Enrolment means subsidised visits — typically around NZ$50–$70, more as a casual patient. Many practices allow online enrolment once you have an address. Dental care is private (roughly NZ$90–$150 for a check-up).

Pharmacies

Pharmacies are found throughout the region. Prescriptions are partly subsidised once you’re enrolled with a GP — commonly a small standard charge per item.

Emergency & after-hours
·Emergency Departments — for urgent or critical care; in an emergency, dial 111.
·Urgent care clinics — accident & medical clinics offer extended-hours care.
·Healthline 0800 611 116 — free 24/7 advice from registered nurses.
Section three · Banking & Finance

Setting up your finances

Getting your finances in order early makes day-to-day life much easier. Most of this can be started before you arrive.

Go deeperSalary & pay in New Zealand healthcare→

Opening a bank account

The major banks are ANZ, ASB, BNZ, Kiwibank and Westpac. You’ll usually need your passport, work or resident visa, and proof of a NZ address. Many banks let you start online before arrival, then activate in person — ask for online banking and a debit card at the same time.

Tax — your IRD number

Every worker needs an IRD number from Inland Revenue. Apply online once you have a NZ address and bank account; it usually takes around 7–10 working days. Give the number to payroll as soon as it arrives so the correct tax is applied. Wages are commonly paid fortnightly.

Sophie’s tip

Start your bank account application before you fly. Having an account number ready makes your IRD application and payroll setup far smoother in those hectic first weeks.

KiwiSaver & sending money home

KiwiSaver is a government-supported retirement savings scheme. On a work visa you can usually opt in or out. Contributions are a percentage of your pay (commonly 3%, 4%, 6%, 8% or 10%), with employers contributing at least 3%. You can withdraw your funds if you permanently leave New Zealand.

Sending money overseas: banks can transfer internationally, but specialist services such as Wise or OFX are often cheaper and faster. Always compare the exchange rate and the fees before moving larger amounts.

Section five · Housing & Areas

Finding a place to live

Wellington offers harbourside apartments, characterful hillside villas and suburban family homes — with the Hutt Valley and Kāpiti adding more space within commuting distance. Where you live shapes your commute, so think about transport links and, if you have children, the school zone before you sign.

Most rentals are advertised online and move quickly in popular areas. The main sites are trademe.co.nz/property (NZ’s largest rental marketplace) and realestate.co.nz (nationwide rentals and homes for sale). Many people rent for a few months first to get to know the areas before committing.

A guide to the areas

Te Aro & the CBD

Apartments in the heart of the city, walkable to work, dining and the waterfront — ideal if you don’t want a car.

Mount Victoria & Thorndon

Characterful inner suburbs of heritage villas, close to town and the town belt.

Newtown & Berhampore

Diverse, lively suburbs right by the regional hospital — handy for shift work.

Hutt Valley & Kāpiti

More space and family homes a train ride from the city, with good schools and beaches.

A tip on choosing

Where you live can decide which state school your children attend — boundaries can run down the middle of a street, so check the exact address is in-zone before you sign. Many people rent short-term first to get to know the areas.

Housing · Everyday services

Utilities, rates & rubbish

Power & gas

Choose from providers like Genesis, Mercury, Meridian and Contact. Compare rates on Powerswitch (powerswitch.org.nz).

Water & rates

Council rates fund local services; if you rent, rates are usually included in your rent.

Rubbish & recycling

Kerbside collection is run by the council, with separate bins for rubbish, recycling and food scraps.

Internet

Most homes have fibre broadband; Spark, One NZ and 2degrees are the main providers.

Section six · Shopping & Food

Where to shop

Wellington offers a compact, walkable retail heart, characterful strips and weekend markets — plus a mall across the harbour for everything under one roof. A few of the main ones:

Lambton Quay & Willis St

The 'Golden Mile’ — the city’s main retail strip, running through the CBD.

Cuba Street

Bohemian boutiques, vintage and independent shops in the city’s creative heart.

Queensgate (Lower Hutt)

The region’s largest mall, across the harbour in the Hutt Valley.

Markets

The Harbourside Market and weekend underground markets for fresh food and craft.

Supermarkets & groceries

New Zealand’s supermarkets fall into a few main groups — knowing the difference helps you balance budget and convenience.

Pak’nSaveThe cheapest of the big chains — a no-frills, bulk-buy warehouse format. Best for budget shopping.
WoolworthsMid-range and widespread, with strong online shopping and delivery (formerly Countdown).
New WorldMore upmarket, with a wider fresh and specialty range.
Four Square & FreshChoiceSmaller neighbourhood stores for convenience and top-ups.

Wellington has Pak’nSave, Woolworths and New World across the city and suburbs, plus Moore Wilson’s for fresh and bulk. Bring your own bags — single-use plastic bags are banned.

A taste of home

Halal & South Asian. Halal and Indian grocers serve the central suburbs; many supermarket lines are halal-certified — check the label.

Asian groceries. Excellent Asian supermarkets and restaurants, especially around the CBD and Newtown.

World food. Wellington’s celebrated food scene spans every cuisine — the city is often said to have more eateries per capita than New York.

Faith & community · Places of worship

Wellington reflects New Zealand’s spiritual diversity, with welcoming communities across many faiths — churches of many denominations, mosques, Hindu and Buddhist temples, and an established Jewish community. Most list service times online, and many are among the quickest ways to build a support network when you arrive.

Section eight · Education & Family

Schooling & study

Wellington offers a full range of early childhood education, schools and tertiary study. The school year runs late January to mid-December, in four terms with two-week breaks in April, July and October.

Go deeperEducation & schools in New Zealand→

Early Childhood (ECE)

Kindergartens, daycare, Montessori, kōhanga reo and home-based care for ages 0–5. Government 20-hours funding applies for ages 3–5.

Primary & intermediate

State, state-integrated and private schools for Years 1–8.

Secondary

Years 9–13, working towards NCEA, the national qualification.

Tertiary

Victoria University of Wellington and Te Pūkenga institutes across the region.

School zones come first

In New Zealand, where you live usually determines which state school your children can attend. For many families, the school decision leads the choice of where to rent or buy.

In-zone = guaranteed

Children living inside a school’s enrolment zone are guaranteed a place.

Out-of-zone = ballot

You can still apply, but popular schools run a ballot for limited spots.

Street-by-street

Boundaries can run down the middle of a street — check the exact address.

The order that works best: shortlist schools you like → check each school’s zone map → search for rentals inside that zone → confirm the address is in-zone before you sign.

Wellington for families

Beyond school and work, Wellington is an easy, walkable place to raise a family — Te Papa, the waterfront and the Botanic Garden (much of the best fun is free), rainy-day options like Te Papa, Capital E, the zoo and Zealandia, free library programmes and Plunket support, and frequent trains and buses. Children often settle fastest through school, sport and local clubs.
