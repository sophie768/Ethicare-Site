What you earn as a healthcare professional in New Zealand isn’t negotiated case by case, it’s set by national agreements that everyone can read.

The honest starting point

Pay here is transparent, and that’s a good thing

The first thing most people want to know is what they’ll actually earn. In New Zealand’s public health system pay is openly structured rather than privately bargained, so you can read it.

Most healthcare roles are covered by national collective employment agreements: negotiated between Health New Zealand / Te Whatu Ora and the relevant union or professional association (for many allied health and medical-imaging roles, that’s the agreements negotiated through APEX). Your pay sits on a published step or grade within that agreement, based on your experience and qualifications. There’s no haggling, and no wondering whether the person beside you is on more for the same work.

Think of it as a ladder, not a negotiation. The rungs are public, the same for everyone, and reviewed regularly: your job is to land on the right one for your experience. That’s the part we help you get right.

A note on figures

We deliberately don’t publish specific salary numbers on this page. Pay rates change as agreements are renegotiated, and the right figure depends on your exact role, step and hours. Rather than risk an out-of-date number, we’ll confirm the current, correct rate for your role directly against the relevant collective agreement before you make any decision.

The system behind the number

How your pay is set

Three things shape what you’re paid in the public system, and all of them are written down. Understanding them means you’ll always know where your number comes from.

The framework

National agreements

Most roles are covered by a national collective agreement negotiated between Health New Zealand / Te Whatu Ora and the relevant union or professional body. It sets the pay scale, hours, leave and conditions for everyone in that profession.

The scale

Steps & grades

Within each agreement is a series of steps. You’re placed on a step that reflects your years of relevant experience and your qualifications, and you typically progress up a step at a time as you accrue service.

The review

Regular updates

Agreements are renegotiated periodically, which is when the scales are updated. Because it’s collective, increases apply across the board rather than depending on an individual conversation with a manager.

Outside the public system

Some private and community providers set pay differently, and senior or specialist roles can have their own arrangements. The same principle still applies: we’ll always tell you exactly how a particular role’s pay is structured before you consider it.

Landing on the right rung

What decides your step

Two clinicians doing the same role can sit on different steps, by design. These are the things that decide where you start — and the part we make sure is properly recognised.

A

Relevant experience

Years spent working in your profession, including experience earned overseas, are recognised and usually count towards your starting step. Getting your full history credited matters.

B

Qualifications & scope

Your registration, post-graduate qualifications and any specialist scope of practice can affect where you sit and which roles you’re eligible for.

C

Role & responsibility

Senior, charge or team-lead positions sit higher on the scale, with added responsibility allowances in many agreements.

D

Progression over time

You move up steps as you accumulate service, so your pay grows steadily without you having to renegotiate each year.

Document your experience

Keep clear evidence of your years of practice and prior roles, it’s the basis for the step you’re placed on, so gaps or vagueness can cost you.

Get your qualifications recognised

Make sure post-graduate study and specialist training are reflected in your application, they can lift both your step and your options.

Understand the hours

Whether a role is full-time, part-time or includes on-call changes your total earnings, not just the headline rate. We’ll spell this out for any role.

Ask about starting placement

It’s reasonable to ask exactly which step an offer places you on and why. A good employer, and a good recruiter, will explain it plainly.

The whole picture

Beyond the base salary

The number at the top of an offer is rarely the whole story. A fair amount sits on top of base pay, and it changes what actually lands in your account.

Penal & overtime rates

Many agreements pay enhanced rates for weekends, nights, public holidays and overtime, so shift patterns can lift earnings well above base.

Allowances & on-call

Depending on the role, there may be allowances for on-call, recall, qualifications or specific responsibilities, all defined in the agreement.

KiwiSaver

New Zealand’s workplace savings scheme includes employer contributions on top of your pay, a quiet but real part of the package.

Generous leave

Annual leave, public holidays, sick leave and, in many agreements, extra service-related leave: time that’s yours.

Professional development

Funded training days, CPD support and continuing-education allowances feature in many agreements, helping your career keep growing.

Relocation support

Some roles include relocation assistance for international hires. Where it exists, we’ll make sure you know what’s on offer.

Who has your back

Unions & professional associations

Because pay is set collectively, unions and professional associations negotiate the agreements your pay sits within. Joining is your choice, and most clinicians do.

Who negotiates your pay

The right body for your role

Which union covers you depends on your profession. For example, APEX represents many allied health, scientific and medical-imaging roles; the NZ Nurses Organisation covers nursing; and senior and resident doctors have their own associations. We’ll tell you which agreement and body apply to your role.

What they do

More than pay talks

They negotiate the national pay scales and conditions, but also support members with workplace questions, professional development and representation if an issue ever arises, a reassuring backstop when you’re new to a system.

Your choice

Joining is optional

Membership is voluntary, and you’re generally covered by your agreement’s terms either way. Many people join for the representation and support, particularly in their first years finding their feet here.

Good to know

You don’t need to work any of this out alone. When we talk through a role, we’ll tell you exactly which collective agreement covers it and which union or association represents your profession, so you know who to turn to before you even arrive.

What actually lands in your account

Tax & take-home pay

Headline salaries are quoted before tax, so the figure you live on is your take-home, and New Zealand’s system is refreshingly simple compared with many places clinicians move from.

Income tax (PAYE) is deducted automatically from your pay by your employer, so most people never file a complicated return. You’ll need an IRD number (your tax identification number) soon after you arrive, and giving it to your employer ensures you’re taxed at the right rate from the start rather than an emergency one. There’s also a small ACC levy that funds New Zealand’s no-fault accident cover, which means you and your family are covered for injury from day one.

Crucially, there’s no separate National Insurance-style contribution as in the UK, and GST (the sales tax) is already included in displayed prices, so what you see on a shelf is what you pay. We’ll always talk in terms that help you picture your real monthly take-home, not just the gross figure.

The practical upshot: once your IRD number and KiwiSaver choice are sorted in your first weeks, your pay runs smoothly and predictably. Our living & thriving guide covers setting up the everyday money side, bank account, IRD, KiwiSaver, step by step.

Your safety net

Insurance, ACC & protection

New Zealand’s safety net works a little differently from what you may be used to. Some things are covered for everyone automatically; others are worth adding for peace of mind, especially with a mortgage or a family.

ACC, accident cover for all

New Zealand’s no-fault accident scheme covers treatment and a portion of lost income if you or your family are injured in an accident, from your first day, funded through levies. It’s one of the reassuring things about life here.

Public healthcare

Residents and many work-visa holders can access the public health system. Our living & thriving guide covers who’s eligible and how it works.

Private health insurance

Optional, but popular. Many take it out for faster elective treatment and more choice of specialist. Some roles include it as a benefit, which we’ll always flag.

Income & life protection

Income protection, life and trauma cover are worth considering, particularly once you’ve a mortgage or dependents. A local adviser can tailor this to your situation.

Home, contents & car

The everyday insurances. Car insurance isn’t legally compulsory here, but it’s strongly recommended, and contents cover is well worth it while you’re renting.

Professional indemnity

In the public system you’re generally covered by your employer, and ACC changes how treatment-injury claims work compared with many countries. Some scopes or private work may need your own cover, confirm what’s required with your regulator and employer.

A note on advice

Insurance and ACC rules depend on your circumstances and visa, and they change, so always confirm the current position at the source. Ethicare are recruitment specialists, not financial advisers, but we’re glad to introduce you to advisers we trust.

Earning, against living

Pay in the context of life here

A salary only means something against what it costs to live. New Zealand pay is structured to support a comfortable, balanced life, and the lifestyle is a real part of the return.

Cost of living, told straight

Housing and groceries are the costs that surprise most newcomers, and they vary a lot by region, a smaller city or town can stretch the same salary much further than a major centre. We’ll talk through the real numbers for where you’re considering.

The lifestyle dividend

Shorter commutes, the outdoors on your doorstep and a genuine respect for time off mean many clinicians feel better off here even where the headline figure is comparable to home.

Region matters

Because base pay is national, the same role pays the same across the country, so your money goes noticeably further in more affordable regions. Our destination guides help you weigh this up.

No surprises

Knowing your exact rate, before you decide

You should never have to accept a role on a vague sense of the pay. By the time you’re weighing an offer, you’ll know precisely what you’ll earn and why.

For any role we discuss, we’ll confirm the current rate against the relevant collective agreement, tell you the step you’d be placed on and the reasoning, and walk through the full package: base, penal rates, allowances, KiwiSaver and leave, so the picture is complete. Where pay sits outside a standard agreement, we’ll say so plainly and explain how it’s set instead.

This is the heart of how we work: calm, honest, no pressure. A move this big deserves real numbers and straight answers, and the time to think them over.

Common questions

The questions we’re asked about pay

Why don’t you list salary figures on this page?

Because the honest figure depends on your exact role, step and hours, and because the agreements are renegotiated over time. Rather than publish a number that could be out of date or misleading for your situation, we confirm the current, correct rate for your specific role against the relevant collective agreement when we talk.

Can I negotiate my salary?

In the public system, base pay follows a published scale rather than individual negotiation, which protects you as much as anything. What does matter, and where good representation pays off, is making sure you’re placed on the correct step for your experience and qualifications. That’s something we actively help you get right.

Will my overseas experience count?

Generally yes. Relevant experience earned abroad is usually recognised towards your step. The key is documenting it clearly so it’s properly credited. We’ll help you present your history so nothing is overlooked.

How does take-home compare to where I am now?

It depends on your current country’s tax and cost of living, so a raw comparison can mislead. We’ll help you think in terms of monthly take-home against local costs, and factor in the things that don’t show on a payslip, like shorter commutes, strong leave and the lifestyle on offer.

What’s KiwiSaver, and do I have to join?

KiwiSaver is New Zealand’s workplace savings scheme, with contributions from you and your employer. If you’re eligible, new employees are usually enrolled automatically with the option to opt out, and you can choose your contribution level. We’ll point you to the basics so you can make an informed choice in your first weeks.

Can I pay into KiwiSaver or a pension on the AEWV?

Usually not straight away. Joining KiwiSaver generally requires you to be entitled to live in New Zealand indefinitely, so on a temporary work visa such as the Accredited Employer Work Visa (AEWV) you typically can’t join yet, and you wouldn’t receive the employer or government contributions until you gain residence. Many people save another way in the meantime and join KiwiSaver once they’re residents. The rules matter here, so confirm your eligibility with a KiwiSaver provider or Inland Revenue.
