Moving to New Zealand

This is the chapter that separates Southland from the other far-south options. Unlike Central Otago, this region has a base hospital — and that single fact changes what a career here can look like.

The shape of the service

Public hospital and specialist services are delivered by Health New Zealand | Te Whatu Ora. The base hospital in Invercargill serves the whole region — roughly a hundred thousand people spread over an area larger than Wales — carrying acute medicine and surgery, an emergency department, intensive care, maternity, paediatrics, imaging, and a full outpatient and day-procedure service.

Beyond the city, care runs through a rural network: community and district services across the plains, and rural facilities in the northern and western districts serving Gore, the Te Anau basin, Fiordland and Rakiura. Distance is a live clinical factor. Retrieval and transfer — by road, and by air from Te Anau and the island — are a normal part of the working year.

Complex and highly specialised work is referred to Dunedin, two and a quarter hours away, and to Christchurch for some tertiary services. That relationship is close and well established.

What the work is like

Real hospital breadth
A base hospital in a region with no competing centre means you see everything the region produces. Scope is wide and referral options are further away, so you make more of the decisions.
Rural and remote reach
Fiordland, Rakiura and the western districts. Genuinely remote medicine, search-and-rescue interaction, and a tourism injury load from the Great Walks and the sounds.
A strong agricultural caseload
Farming is the region’s economy, and farm injury, occupational health and rural mental health are a real and distinctive part of the work.
An ageing, dispersed population
Age-related medicine, orthopaedics and chronic disease weigh heavily, with access and distance compounding them across the plains.
Progression, sooner
A mid-sized service a long way from a tertiary centre gives you visibility and responsibility earlier than a large hospital would. Several clinicians we’ve placed here have stepped up faster than they expected.
Recruitment is the standing challenge
Southland has worked hard to attract and keep clinicians for years. That means genuine openness to international staff — and, honestly, some gaps and pressure alongside it.

Working with te ao Māori

This is the rohe of Ngāi Tahu, and Murihiku has a long history of settlement, harvest and trade along the southern coast and the islands. Iwi-led health providers are an established part of care delivery, and the region’s health equity picture — rural, dispersed, with real access barriers and a significant Māori and Pacific population in the city — is central to the work rather than adjacent to it.

Practically: karakia at the start of meetings, te reo used on the ward, whānau involved in decisions. You are not expected to arrive fluent — you are expected to try. The national chapter Te ao Māori at work covers pronunciation, the words you will hear and marae etiquette.

What to ask at interview
  • What is the catchment for my service, and how much rural, outreach or island work is involved?
  • What is the on-call or rostered-hours pattern, and how is it paid under the relevant collective agreement?
  • What gets referred to Dunedin or Christchurch in my specialty, and what is my role in stabilising and transferring?
  • What orientation, supervision and cultural induction is there for clinicians new to New Zealand?
  • What professional development and study support exists, given the distance from a tertiary centre?
  • Is there relocation support, and what help is there finding housing and schools?

Sophie’s insight

Nobody asks me about Southland, and it is the region where I have seen people build the most life the fastest. A base hospital, a house bought outright within a few years, and Fiordland two hours away. The weather is the honest cost — everything else is in your favour.

Before you can start
