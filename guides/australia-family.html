The reassuring part

Children travel further than we give them credit for

The flight to Australia from the UK, South Africa, the Gulf or India is among the longest journeys you’ll ever make, and doing it with children sounds daunting. In practice, it’s the bit you’ll have forgotten within a fortnight. It’s the settling afterwards, not the travelling, that deserves your real attention.

Children take their cue from you. If the move is framed as an adventure and the practicalities are quietly handled in the background, most children adapt remarkably quickly, often faster than the adults. Australia’s outdoor, friendly, sport-loving culture is good for kids, and schools are well used to welcoming children from all over the world. This guide walks through both halves: getting there in one piece, and helping everyone feel at home once you’ve arrived.

If there’s one thing to hold onto, it’s this: a few unsettled weeks are completely normal and almost always temporary. Wobbles, clinginess and the odd tearful evening don’t mean you’ve made the wrong decision, they mean a small person is doing something brave.

The question the move turns on

Can my partner work?

For most families this decides it. Two salaries or one changes what you can afford, where you can live and how long you can give the move to work — and it changes how the person coming with you feels about going at all.

The short version: your partner’s right to work comes from your visa, not from a job offer of their own. Partners are usually included in your application, and what they are permitted to do follows from the visa you are granted and the conditions attached to it. That is why the honest answer is never a flat yes or no, but check it against the route you are actually on — and check it before you accept, not after.

Whose visa decides it

Yours, not theirs

Work rights attach to being included in your application. Change route — temporary to permanent — and theirs changes with it.

Read the conditions on the visa you are granted, not the one you applied for.

Will the qualification count?

A separate question entirely

Permission to work and recognition to practise are two different processes. Regulated professions run through the Australian regulator or assessing authority for that field; everything else is judged by employers.

Start it early. Assessments take longer than anyone expects.

How long until they earn?

Budget the first months on one income

Even with full work rights, job-hunting while unpacking, enrolling children and learning a new city takes time. Families who plan for one income and are pleasantly surprised do far better than families who plan for two.

If they are a clinician too

Two registrations, two timelines

Theirs may well be the longer one. Run both from the start rather than in sequence — a second timeline discovered late is the most common reason a family ends up arriving months apart.

Check their pathway alongside yours.

What to settle before you accept

Ask the employer which visa they intend to sponsor and what conditions come with it. Ask what support, if any, extends to a partner. Then have the honest conversation at home about what six months on one income looks like. None of this is awkward at the offer stage; all of it is expensive later.

Visa conditions are set and changed by government, so we point you at the source rather than paraphrasing it: Department of Home Affairs. We will talk through what your route means in practice, and we will tell you when a question needs a registered migration agent rather than us. See also visas and immigration.

Under five

Childcare, honestly

Two things surprise people: the vocabulary is different, and the binding constraint is places rather than price. In popular suburbs the waiting list is the problem, and it starts long before you land.

1

Learn the names

Long day care covers the full working day. Family day care is a smaller setting in an educator’s own home, and is often the more flexible option. Preschool and kindergarten are the year or two before school, run by each state under its own name and its own rules — which is why advice from a friend in another state may not apply to you.

2

Join waiting lists before you fly

You can enquire and be listed from overseas. Do it as soon as you have a suburb in mind, and list at more than one centre. A place you do not need is easy to decline; a place that does not exist is not.

3

Check the subsidy against your own visa

Fees are quoted per day, and the Child Care Subsidy reduces what families actually pay — but eligibility depends on your residency status, so a temporary-visa family should confirm their position before budgeting on the subsidised figure. This is the single most expensive assumption we see families make.

4

Test it against a clinical roster

Standard centre hours were not designed around a 7am start or a late finish. Ask about the earliest drop-off and the latest pick-up, ask what happens when you are held past handover, and plan for the gap — a nanny share or family day care often covers what a centre cannot.

Official sources: Child Care Subsidy (Services Australia) for eligibility and rates, and your state education department for preschool. For school-age children, schools and education covers enrolment, catchments and fees.

Doing it on your own

Moving as a single parent

This is a different move, not a smaller one, and almost everything written for families quietly assumes a second adult. Plenty of single parents make this move well. What separates the ones who find it manageable is that none of it came as a surprise.

Two things need handling early, and both are easier to do at home than from the other side of the world.

Consent to take a child abroad

Where someone else holds parental responsibility, you will generally need their written consent or a court order — and it can be asked for twice: once by the visa application, and again by an airline or a border officer. Get it in writing, get copies certified, and carry them in your hand luggage rather than the shipping container.

  • Full birth certificates showing both parents, not the short form.
  • Any parenting or custody orders, certified.
  • A signed consent letter with the other parent’s contact details, or evidence of sole parental responsibility.
  • Where a parent has died, the death certificate.
  • Immunisation records and medical notes, which schools and clinics will ask for.
One income, two costs

Childcare is the line that decides it

Work the numbers on your own salary with care included at full price, and check the subsidy question above before you rely on it. The first three months are the most expensive of the whole move.

No second adult

Build the network before you need it

Before and after-school care, school-holiday programmes, a neighbour, and one colleague who will swap a shift. Set these up in the first fortnight, not the first emergency.

The roster

Negotiate it before you accept

Nights, weekends and on-call are a different proposition without another adult at home. Say so at offer stage — it is a normal conversation, and a far harder one to start once you have arrived.

School holidays

Longer than you are used to

The summer break is substantial and falls over Christmas. Holiday programmes book out early. Check the term dates for your state before you plan your leave for the year.

Tell us your situation early and we will sequence it with you — what to gather, in what order, and who actually decides. Consent and documentation requirements are set by government: see Department of Home Affairs, and talk to us if you would rather work through it with someone.

Before you fly

Preparing the children for the move

The groundwork you lay in the weeks before you leave does more for a smooth landing than anything you can do on the day. The goal is simple: make the unfamiliar feel familiar before you ever get on the plane.

Tell them early

Talk it through, often

Share the news in good time and keep the conversation going. Look at photos and maps of where you’re heading, watch videos together, and let them ask the same questions as many times as they need to.

Make it real

Picture the new life

Explore the new town online, find the local beach, park or pool, and if you can, look at their school’s website together. The more they can imagine the everyday, the less frightening it feels.

Honour the goodbye

Let them say farewell

A proper goodbye matters. Help them mark leaving friends, family and a familiar home, a small party, a memory book, swapping addresses, so the move feels like a beginning, not a loss.

Keep one comfort close

A favourite toy, blanket or pillow should travel in the hand luggage, never the hold. Familiar things are anchors when everything else is new.

Plan to stay connected

Reassure them that grandparents and friends are a video call away. The time difference takes some juggling, but knowing loved ones haven’t disappeared makes the distance easier.

Involve them in packing

Let older children choose a few of their own things to bring. A little ownership over the move turns something happening to them into something they’re part of.

Sort documents & health

Carry passports, visas, and copies of immunisation records, medical notes and any prescriptions in your hand luggage. Australia’s biosecurity rules are strict, so check what food and items you can bring before you pack.

The big one

Surviving the long flight

Getting to Australia usually means around 20 to 24 hours of travel, often with at least one stop. It’s long, but airlines see families every day and are helpful, and a little planning turns an ordeal into a story you’ll laugh about later.

Break the journey

Consider a stopover

A night’s stopover en route (common hubs include the Gulf, Singapore, Hong Kong or Kuala Lumpur) can split an overwhelming trip into two manageable ones and let small bodies stretch, sleep and reset.

Ask for help

Tell the airline early

Request bassinets, children’s meals and seats together when you book, and use family boarding. Cabin crew are well used to travelling children and will lend a hand if you ask.

Beat the boredom

Pack a bag of surprises

Snacks, headphones, downloaded shows, and a few small wrapped activities handed out across the hours work wonders. Novelty buys you precious quiet stretches.

Dress for sleep

Comfy layers and familiar pyjamas signal bedtime even at 35,000 feet, and layers cope with cabins that swing from warm to chilly.

Ease little ears

Feeding, a drink or a chew during take-off and landing helps with pressure. Pack more nappies, wipes and spare clothes than feels sensible.

Go gently on jet lag

The time difference is large, so nudge towards local time over the first few days with daylight, fresh air and patience. Expect early starts and odd-hour wakings for a week or so.

Mind the biosecurity gate

Declare any food, and don’t pack fresh produce. Australia’s arrival biosecurity is strict. Knowing this in advance saves a stressful first hour at the airport with tired children.

When you land

The first 48 hours

You’ll arrive tired, possibly at a strange hour, into a brand-new place, and quite likely into bright sun and serious heat if you’ve landed in summer. Keep the first couple of days deliberately small. There’s nothing to achieve yet except rest, food and a gentle first look around.

Rest & hydrate

Don’t fight the jet lag with a packed schedule. Let everyone sleep, eat simply, and surface slowly, and in the heat, get the children drinking water, slip-slop-slapping on sunscreen and out of the midday sun.

Find the everyday

A trip to the local supermarket, park or beach in the first day or two makes the new place feel ordinary and knowable, fast, for children especially.

Recreate small routines

The familiar bedtime story, the same breakfast, the favourite cup. Tiny rituals carried from home are how children feel safe somewhere new.

Finding your feet

The first few weeks

Once the worst of the jet lag lifts, the work becomes gentle and steady: building a rhythm, getting children into school or care, and letting them start to make this place their own. Don’t rush it, and don’t expect a straight line.

Get into school soon

Routine and friends are the fastest cure for homesickness, so enrol and start school or early learning sooner rather than later. Our education guide walks through how it works, including the bits that depend on your visa.

Say yes to invitations

Playdates, birthday parties, Nippers at the surf club, weekend sport: these are where Australian childhood friendships form. Accepting invitations, for parents too, is how a family puts down roots.

Expect the wobble

A few weeks in, the novelty fades and the missing-home feelings can arrive, for children and adults alike. It’s a normal stage, not a setback. Keep routines steady and it passes.

The decision that shapes the rest

Choosing where to live, with the family in mind

For a family, where you settle matters as much as the role, sometimes more. Australia is vast, and life in a diverse city suburb is a world away from life in a small regional or remote town. Both can be wonderful; they’re just very different, and children feel the difference too.

In the cities, you’ll find the widest choice of schools, the most established communities, and classmates from every background, so a child who’s a visible minority is one of many. In small country towns and the remote outback, the pace is gentler and the welcome often warm, but there’s less diversity, fewer services and a child may be one of very few from their background. For some families that closeness is a gift; for others it can feel isolating. It’s worth thinking about honestly, with your children’s needs in the picture.

There’s a practical layer too: in most states, a government school place is tied to your home address through a catchment zone, so the suburb and the school decision go hand in hand. It’s well worth researching schools before you choose a rental. Our community guide goes deeper on how diversity changes by place.

This is exactly the kind of thing we’ll talk through with you: your children’s ages, what matters to your family, and the honest character of the places you’re considering. We’d far rather help you choose well up front than watch a family settle somewhere that was never going to suit them.

Different ages, different needs

Settling, age by age

A toddler, a school-age child and a teenager experience the same move in completely different ways. Knowing what to expect, and what helps, at each stage takes a lot of the worry out of it.

Babies & toddlers

The portable ones

0–4

The easiest to relocate and the quickest to settle: their world is you. Protect sleep and feeding routines, keep their comfort items close, and they’ll be at home wherever you are within days.

Primary age

The fast adapters

5–11

Usually settle within weeks. Friendly, outdoor, sporty Australian classrooms make friends easy to come by. Children this age often adapt before their parents have unpacked the last box.

Tweens

The ones to watch

12–13

Old enough to feel the loss of friends keenly, young enough to make new ones readily. Help them stay in touch with old friends while gently encouraging clubs and activities that build new circles.

Teenagers

The ones who need a say

14+

Moving at a decisive age is hard, and they need more time and more involvement. Bring them into decisions, take homesickness seriously, mind how the senior-school years (and the ATAR) line up, and give the first term or two before judging how it’s going.

The decision checklist

Moving with your family: the questions to settle first

The career can be right and the move still wrong for the people coming with you. These are the questions that decide whether it adds up, settle them before you fall for a suburb.

What will my partner do?
Two careers move, not one, and their right to work comes from your visa rather than a job offer of their own. Can my partner work? on this page sets out what decides it, what to ask before you accept, and what to do if they are a clinician too. Immigration & visas explains how a partner fits the main application.
Will we pay school fees?
It depends on your state and your visa: public schooling is not automatically free for every temporary-visa family, and the rules differ meaningfully between states. Will I have to pay school fees? answers it properly, read it before you choose a state, not after.
What will childcare cost?
Under-fives are a different budget line entirely, and in popular suburbs the constraint is places rather than price. Childcare, honestly covers the options, the waiting lists, the subsidy question and what a clinical roster does to all three.
Can we manage on one income at first?
Don’t guess. Do the subtraction: net salary, minus rent, childcare, school costs, insurance, a car and the weekly shop. If the result works before a second income arrives, everything that follows is easier. Salary & pay and the cost of relocating give you the pieces.
Will my family have healthcare cover?
Medicare is not automatic on every temporary visa, and private health cover can be a visa condition in its own right. Check your family’s position early, bring enough regular medication for the first weeks, and carry copies of medical records. Understanding healthcare covers Medicare and the private system.
When in the school year should we move?
Aim for the long summer break and children get a clean start, though schools handle mid-year joins well too. Teenagers approaching their senior assessment years need the most care, plan around exam cycles, not flight prices.
Does my child need extra learning support?
Support does not transfer automatically with overseas paperwork. Get current reports and assessments before you leave, contact shortlisted schools before you commit to an address, and ask each one directly what support it can offer and how long assessments take to arrange.
Can our parents and grandparents join us?
In stages, yes, the tiers mirror New Zealand’s. Family-stream visitor visas (subclass 600) cover regular visits; the Sponsored Parent (Temporary) visa (subclass 870) allows stays of three or five years at a time, up to ten years in total, with sponsorship and health-insurance conditions, an annual cap and no work rights, and no pathway to residence. Permanent routes exist too: the Parent visa (subclass 103) carries a queue measured in decades, while the Contributory Parent visa (subclass 143) moves far faster in exchange for substantially higher contribution charges. Caps, charges and processing change, check the official Home Affairs parent visa pages before promising anything to the grandparents.
Temporary first, or permanent residence?
For families this is the quiet big one, because residence status, not the job, drives the school-fee, Medicare and subsidy answers above. Most clinicians arrive on the employer-sponsored Skills in Demand visa (subclass 482) with the family as secondary applicants; some roles support the permanent Employer Nomination Scheme (subclass 186) sooner, and regional classifications open further routes. Reaching permanence flips several of the costs on this page. Immigration & visas covers the pathways.
What if someone in the family has a health condition or disability?
This is the hardest question on the page, and the one to face earliest. Every applicant. Dependants included, must meet Australia’s migration health requirement, and the test is projected cost, not diagnosis: a Medical Officer of the Commonwealth assesses whether a condition is likely to cost more than the Significant Cost Threshold, currently A$86,000, over ten years (or the visa length). Regardless of your ability to pay privately. Classic triggers include high-cost PBS medicines (biologics such as adalimumab), dialysis- or transplant-level care, and autism-related support needs in a child, which is why autism can complicate an application even when the child is thriving. If one family member fails, the whole application can be refused; health waivers exist on some visa subclasses only, and are discretionary. Declare everything honestly, gather specialist reports and costed treatment plans before you apply, and for anything borderline take advice from a registered migration agent. This is the one part of a move where that professional advice earns its fee. The official Home Affairs health requirement pages carry the current rules; whether a particular medicine is PBS-funded is a separate question again, see Understanding healthcare.

We’re recruitment specialists, not registered migration agents, and none of this is immigration, tax or financial advice. Visa conditions and eligibility rules change, so confirm the current position with the Department of Home Affairs and the schools themselves before committing.

Told straight

Looking after yourself, too

It’s a quiet truth of moving with children: you’ll pour everything into settling them and forget to settle yourself. But a calm, supported parent is the single biggest thing a child needs to feel safe somewhere new.

Give yourself permission to find it hard. You’re holding down a new job, running an unfamiliar household and being everyone’s reassurance, often without your usual support network nearby, and the distance from family back home can feel further from Australia than almost anywhere. Build your own footholds early: say yes to the school-gate chat, find one or two other parents, and lean on the communities who’ve made the same move. You don’t have to do it perfectly, and you certainly don’t have to do it alone.

If you ever feel you’re struggling more than seems normal, reach out, to your GP, to a helpline, or to us. Asking for support is part of doing this well, not a sign you’ve got it wrong.

Sophie’s insight

I’ve sat with so many parents who lay awake over this exact question, the flight, the schools, whether they’re doing the right thing by their children. Here’s what I’ve seen, again and again: the kids are usually fine, and often more than fine. They take to the beach and the outdoor life, they make friends at the park, and within a term this is home. My honest advice is to look after yourselves as carefully as you look after them, and to choose where you live with the whole family in mind, not just the commute. Pointing families towards the right town, the right school and the right people is one of the parts of this work I love most.

Common questions

The questions parents ask us

When’s the best time of year to move with school-age children?

The Australian school year runs from late January to mid-December, in four terms, so arriving around late January lets children begin with everyone else. That said, schools take new arrivals throughout the year and are well practised at it, the right time is mostly the one that fits your job and visa, so don’t force it to line up perfectly.

How long until the children really settle?

Every child is different, but a common pattern is an exciting first few weeks, a dip as the novelty fades and home is missed, and a gradual settling over the following months. Younger children often feel at home within weeks; teenagers may need a term or two. Steady routines and getting into school quickly are the things that help most.

What about childcare while we’re both finding our feet?

Australia has a well-established early childhood system: long day care, family day care and preschool or kindergarten. The federal Child Care Subsidy lowers fees for eligible families, but eligibility depends on your residency and visa: many families on a temporary visa (including the 482) don’t qualify and pay full fees. Popular centres can have waitlists, so it’s worth enquiring before you arrive. Our education guide covers this in detail.

Will being on a temporary visa affect my child’s schooling?

It can, and it’s worth checking early. Government schooling is free or low-cost for citizens and permanent residents, but several states charge an annual tuition contribution for temporary residents’ children at public schools, while others exempt skilled or sponsored visas. It varies a lot by state and changes over time, so we’ll help you confirm the current policy where you’re heading. The education guide has an indicative state-by-state breakdown.

Can you help us choose where to live with the family in mind?

Absolutely, it’s one of the most useful things we do. Tell us your children’s ages, what matters to your family and any schools you’ve got your eye on, and we’ll help you weigh up suburbs, school zones, commutes, community and the everyday feel of an area, honestly, before you commit to anywhere.
