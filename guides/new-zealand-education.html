Education & schools in New Zealand

For many families, the children’s schooling is the question that matters most.

The reassuring part

A welcoming system where children settle fast

New Zealand has a well-regarded, inclusive education system, and schools here are used to welcoming children from all over the world. Time and again, families tell us their children settled faster than they did, helped by friendly classrooms and an outdoor, sporty school culture.

The system will feel broadly familiar to anyone from a comparable country, with a few differences in structure, qualifications and the way you enrol. This guide walks through all of it, so you can picture your children’s school days clearly and choose the right place with confidence.

If there’s one practical thing to take away, it’s this: school enrolment and where you live are closely linked. Many good schools have enrolment zones, so it pays to research schools before you settle on a neighbourhood, often the school decides the postcode.

From toddlers to teenagers

The stages of education

Schooling runs from age 5 through to around 18, divided into clear stages. Most children start school on or around their fifth birthday, and education is compulsory from 6 to 16.

Under 5

Early childhood (ECE)

birth–5

Optional but popular early learning: kindergartens, daycare, playcentres and kōhanga reo (Māori-language). Government funding subsidises a set number of hours a week for children aged 3 to 5, so it’s more affordable than many expect.

Years 1–6

Primary school

ages 5–10

The foundation years, starting when your child turns five. Warm, play-informed and broad, with a strong focus on literacy, numeracy and learning to belong in a classroom community.

Years 7–8

Intermediate

ages 11–12

A two-year bridge between primary and secondary in many areas, easing the step up. In some places these years sit within a full primary or a middle school instead, it varies by region.

Years 9–13

Secondary (college / high school)

ages 13–17

Where students work towards their national qualifications in the senior years, with a widening choice of subjects and pathways into work, training or university.

How the year is shaped

The school year & holidays

The New Zealand school year runs from late January to mid-December, divided into four terms of roughly ten weeks each. Between terms are two-week breaks, with a long summer holiday over Christmas and New Year.

Term 1

Late Jan – mid-April

The year begins, usually in the first week or two of February, followed by a two-week autumn break.

Term 2

Early May – early July

The longest stretch of settled learning, rounded off by the two-week winter holidays.

Term 3

Mid-July – late Sept

Through the heart of winter into early spring, then a two-week spring break.

Term 4

Mid-Oct – mid-Dec

The final term, ending before Christmas and the long summer holiday of around six weeks.

A few things worth knowing: exact dates are set each year by the Ministry of Education and vary slightly between schools (start days, the odd teacher-only day). Public holidays and your region’s anniversary day also fall within term time. For care over the breaks, holiday programmes are widely available, see childcare & out-of-school care above.

Your choices

The types of school

Most children attend free, government-funded state schools, but there are three main types to know about: each with a different character and cost.

Most common

State schools

Government-funded and free for domestic students, these educate the large majority of children. Quality is generally high and consistent, and they follow the national curriculum.

Special character

State-integrated

State-funded schools with a particular character, often faith-based (such as Catholic) or a specific philosophy. They follow the national curriculum but usually charge modest attendance dues.

Fee-paying

Private / independent

Independently run and funded mainly through fees. A smaller part of the system, chosen by some families for particular programmes, facilities or smaller classes.

The thing to get right early

Enrolment zones, why where you live matters

Many popular schools manage demand with an enrolment zone (a home zone). If you live within the zone, your child is generally guaranteed a place; if you live outside it, places are usually limited and offered by ballot. It’s the single biggest reason to research schools before you choose where to rent or buy.

In-zone

Living within a school’s zone usually guarantees enrolment, which is why families often choose the neighbourhood for the school.

Out-of-zone

Out-of-zone places at popular schools are limited and typically allocated by a ballot, so they’re never guaranteed.

Check before you commit

You can look up any school’s zone online before signing a lease. A few minutes here saves a lot of stress later.

Getting enrolled

How enrolment works

The practical steps are straightforward, and schools are helpful with new arrivals. A few things worth knowing as you go.

Contact the school directly

You enrol with the individual school rather than a central authority. Reach out before you arrive if you can: many will start the conversation by email.

Bring your documents

Have passports, visas, immunisation records, and your children’s previous school reports and any learning-support paperwork ready, they help with the right placement.

Year level follows age

Children are generally placed by age rather than by exactly where they were in their old system, which keeps them with their peers.

Extra language support

Schools provide English-language learning support where it’s needed, so children who are still building their English are well looked after.

What they’ll learn & earn

Curriculum & qualifications

A broad, inclusive curriculum runs through the school years, and senior students work towards a national qualification, with some schools offering international alternatives too.

The curriculum

Broad & inclusive

Schools follow the New Zealand Curriculum, with te reo Māori and Aotearoa’s bicultural heritage woven through. The emphasis is on well-rounded learning, not just exams, including plenty of time outdoors and on sport.

The main qualification

NCEA

The National Certificate of Educational Achievement is the principal secondary qualification, taken across the final three years (Levels 1–3). It’s flexible, combining internal and external assessment across a wide range of subjects.

Other options

Cambridge & IB

Some schools also offer Cambridge International or the International Baccalaureate alongside or instead of NCEA, worth asking about if your child is mid-pathway or you’d like an internationally portable qualification.

Told straight

What it costs

State schooling is free for domestic students, but there are everyday extras to budget for, and your visa decides whether your children are treated as domestic or international students.

State school tuition
Free for domestic students, which children of many longer-term work-visa holders are, depending on the visa.
School donations
State schools may request a voluntary donation to top up funding. It’s optional, though many families contribute.
Uniforms, stationery & devices
Practical costs you’ll budget for: uniforms, stationery, a device for older students, and trips or activities.
State-integrated dues
Integrated schools typically charge modest compulsory attendance dues on top of the free tuition.
Private school fees
Independent schools charge full tuition fees, which vary widely by school.
International student fees
If your visa means your children are international students, state schools charge fees, so it’s worth confirming your status early.

Whether your children count as domestic or international students depends on your visa. Confirm it with Immigration New Zealand or the school. Rules and costs change, so always check the current position at the source. Ethicare are recruitment specialists, not an education authority.

What they’ll wear

School uniforms

Whether your child wears a uniform depends mostly on their age and the school. As a rule, the older the student, the more likely a uniform, and the more formal it tends to be.

Primary years

Often simple, sometimes none

Many primary schools have a relaxed, inexpensive uniform. Usually a polo and shorts, skirt or trousers in the school colours, and some have none at all. Summers are casual, and it’s common for young children to go barefoot on warm days.

Intermediate & secondary

Usually compulsory

From intermediate and especially at secondary school, a uniform is normally required, often with a separate PE kit. State-integrated and private schools tend to be more formal, sometimes including a blazer.

Sun-smart

Hats in summer

New Zealand takes sun protection seriously. Many schools run a “no hat, no play” rule through the summer terms, so a wide-brim or bucket hat is usually part of the kit.

Where to buy uniforms

  • The school’s own uniform shop or office. The first stop, and where compulsory branded items are sold.
  • The school’s approved uniform supplier, many schools nominate a specialist supplier for items like blazers, jerseys and the PE kit.
  • Big-box retailers such as The Warehouse, handy and affordable for generic basics: plain polos, shorts, socks, hats and shoes.
  • Second-hand uniform sales, run by most schools or their parents’ association, and by far the cheapest way to kit children out.
For the under-fives & after the bell

Childcare & out-of-school care

Whether you’ve got pre-schoolers or school-age children, there’s a well-established system to help working parents, funded early learning, before- and after-school care, and clubs that cover the gaps around your shifts.

20 Hours ECE

The government’s 20 Hours ECE scheme funds up to 20 hours a week of early childhood education for eligible children aged three to five (and some younger children at certain services), at participating providers. It makes pre-school care far more affordable than many newcomers expect.

Extra help with costs

Beyond the funded hours, families may be able to get further help, such as a Childcare Subsidy through Work and Income, depending on income, hours worked and circumstances. It’s worth checking what you’re eligible for once you’ve arrived.

Finding a place

Options include kindergartens, daycare (education & care centres), home-based care, playcentres and kōhanga reo. Popular centres can have waitlists, so enquire early. Ideally before you arrive, to line something up for your start date.

Childcare near work

Some hospital and healthcare sites have an onsite or nearby early learning centre, though it isn’t universal. If childcare close to work matters for your shifts, tell us, we can check what’s available at a specific site.

Breakfast clubs

Many schools take part in a national breakfast programme, providing a free, healthy breakfast before class. It’s a quiet help on busy mornings and a warm start to the day for children settling in.

Before & after-school care

Out of School Care and Recreation (OSCAR) programmes run before school, after school and through the holidays, often onsite or nearby. A Work and Income OSCAR Subsidy can help with the cost for eligible families, useful when your hours don’t match the school bell.

Funding rules, hours and subsidies change and depend on your circumstances and visa, always confirm the current detail with the Ministry of Education, Work and Income or the provider. Ethicare are recruitment specialists, not an education authority, but we’re glad to help you weigh up options near a role.

More than logistics

Helping your children settle

Enrolment is the easy part; helping a child feel at home is the real work. Happily, New Zealand’s school culture does much of the heavy lifting, but a little intention helps.

Younger children

Tend to settle within weeks. Friendly classrooms, lots of outdoor play and sport make it easy to make friends fast, often before their parents have unpacked the last box.

Teenagers

May need more time and more say. Leaving friends at a decisive age is hard, so involve them in school choice, keep talking, and give the first term or two to find their feet.

Lean on the school

Schools are experienced at welcoming new arrivals and often pair newcomers with a buddy. Get to know teachers early, and the school gate is a great place for parents to make friends too.

Sophie’s insight

The children are so often the part parents worry about most, and so often the ones who settle first. My one piece of practical advice is to start with the school, not the suburb: find schools you’d be happy with, check their zones, and let that guide where you look to live. And don’t underestimate a Kiwi childhood: the outdoors, the sport, the easygoing classrooms. Plenty of families tell me their kids ended up happier here than they ever expected.

SophieCEO & Founder, Ethicare Resourcing

Common questions

The questions parents ask us

Will my children need a school uniform, and what does it cost?

It depends on the school and the age. Primary schools are often relaxed, a simple, inexpensive uniform or none at all, while intermediate and secondary schools usually require one, sometimes with a separate PE kit. It’s a cost worth budgeting for upfront, but most schools run second-hand uniform sales that bring it right down. See our uniforms section above for the detail.

Can my children go to state school for free on my visa?

Children of New Zealand citizens and residents are domestic students, so state schooling is free. Children of many work-visa holders. Including the Accredited Employer Work Visa (AEWV), are also generally treated as domestic students where the parent holds an eligible work visa, which means free state schooling too. Eligibility hinges on your specific visa and its conditions, and the rules can change, so confirm your children’s status with Immigration New Zealand, the Ministry of Education or the school before you enrol. If they’re classed as international students, state schools charge fees.

What childcare is available for under-fives, and is it subsidised?

Early childhood education (ECE) is well established, kindergartens, daycare (education and care centres), home-based care, playcentres and kōhanga reo (Māori-language). The government’s 20 Hours ECE scheme subsidises a set number of hours each week for eligible children aged three to five, so it’s often more affordable than newcomers expect. Popular centres can have waitlists, so it’s worth researching and contacting them before you arrive. Confirm what funding your children are eligible for based on your visa.

When in the year should my child start school?

The school year runs from late January to mid-December across four terms, so starting at the beginning of the year lets children begin alongside everyone else. That said, schools enrol new arrivals throughout the year and are well practised at it. The right time is usually the one that fits your job and visa, so don’t force it to line up perfectly.
