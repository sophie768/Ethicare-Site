A first impression

A small country with a big heart

Aotearoa is a nation of around five million people at the bottom of the South Pacific, closer to Antarctica than to most of the world. That distance is part of its character.

What strikes most newcomers first is the contrast between the dramatic and the gentle. Soaring mountains, deep fiords and wild coastlines sit beside an unhurried, friendly way of life. People are direct but warm, proud but unshowy, and quick to make room for you. Underpinning it all is a bicultural foundation, the partnership between Māori, the indigenous people, and those who came later, layered today with communities from every corner of the world.

You don’t need to know any of this before you arrive, but a little understanding goes a long way. The people who settle most happily are those who come curious, ready to learn the land’s stories and customs rather than expecting it to be just like home.

The land

Two islands, and never far from nature

New Zealand stretches long and narrow, so wherever you settle the coast, the bush and the mountains are close. The two main islands each have their own feel.

Te Ika-a-Māui

The North Island

Warmer and more populous, home to Auckland and the capital, Wellington. Rolling green farmland, volcanic plateaus, geothermal Rotorua and long surf beaches define it.

Te Waipounamu

The South Island

Wilder and more spacious, with the Southern Alps running down its spine, the lakes and fiords of the south, and Christchurch and Dunedin as its main cities.

The climate

Temperate & seasonal

Mild rather than extreme, with four real seasons, and reversed from the northern hemisphere. Summer is December to February; the far south can be properly cold in winter.

A short history

How Aotearoa came to be

New Zealand was one of the last large landmasses on earth to be settled by people, which makes its history comparatively recent, and all the more vivid for it.

c.1300s

Māori settlement

Polynesian voyagers navigated vast distances of open ocean to reach Aotearoa, becoming the tāngata whenua, the people of the land, and developing distinct iwi (tribes) with rich language, custom and oral history.

1642 & 1769

First European contact

Dutch explorer Abel Tasman sighted the coast in 1642; more than a century later, British navigator James Cook mapped the islands, opening the way to wider European contact and trade.

1840

Te Tiriti o Waitangi

The Treaty of Waitangi was signed between the British Crown and many Māori rangatira (chiefs), the document still considered New Zealand’s founding agreement, and central to public life to this day.

1860s–1900

Colonisation & conflict

Rapid settlement, the New Zealand Wars and large-scale land loss profoundly affected Māori, a difficult history the country continues to reckon with honestly and openly.

1893

Votes for women

New Zealand became the first self-governing country in the world to grant women the right to vote, an early mark of the egalitarian streak it still prizes.

1970s–today

Renaissance & a modern nation

A Māori cultural renaissance revitalised te reo and tikanga, Treaty settlements began addressing historic wrongs, and waves of migration made New Zealand the diverse, outward-looking democracy it is now.

The founding document

Te Tiriti o Waitangi

Signed on 6 February 1840, Te Tiriti o Waitangi is an agreement between the Crown and Māori. It shapes law, public services and the health system you’ll work in.

Article one

Kāwanatanga

Governance, the Crown’s role in governing the country, establishing settled law and order.

Article two

Rangatiratanga

Chieftainship: Māori authority over their lands, resources and taonga (treasures), including language and custom.

Article three

Ōritetanga

Equity, the same rights, protections and citizenship for Māori as for all New Zealanders.

The English and te reo Māori texts differ in important ways, and those differences sit at the heart of New Zealand’s ongoing conversation about partnership. For healthcare workers this matters in practice: the principles of Te Tiriti underpin the health system’s commitment to equity and to better outcomes for Māori, something you’ll see reflected in how care is delivered.

Te reo Māori

A few words you’ll hear every day

Te reo Māori is an official language, woven through greetings, place names, signage and the workplace. No one expects fluency, and a handful of words tends to be warmly received.

Kia oraHello, and a warm “be well”, used constantly.
AotearoaNew Zealand: “land of the long white cloud”.
WhānauFamily, in the broad, extended sense.
Tāngata whenuaPeople of the land: Māori as indigenous people.
ManaakitangaHospitality, kindness and care for others.
KaitiakitangaGuardianship and care of the environment.
TikangaCustoms, the right and proper way of doing things.
MaraeCommunal meeting grounds, the heart of a community.
PākehāNew Zealanders of European descent.
Haere maiWelcome: come in, come here.
Ngā mihiThanks and warm regards: common at the end of emails.
Kia kahaStay strong, an everyday word of encouragement.

Place names are often te reo too, so it’s worth learning to say where you live correctly. Locals will warmly help you. Pronunciation is consistent once you learn the vowels, and getting it right is appreciated.

People & culture

What New Zealanders are like

Generalisations only go so far, but a few cultural threads come up again and again, and they tend to make settling in easier than newcomers expect.

Manaakitanga

Welcoming

Hospitality runs deep. Neighbours, colleagues and other families who’ve made the move tend to fold you in quickly: many newcomers are surprised how at home they feel within months.

Tūrangawaewae

A love of the outdoors

Weekends are for the beach, the bush, the boat or the rugby. Connection to the land is central, and an easy, shared way to make friends and feel you belong.

Mahi tahi

Egalitarian & informal

Hierarchy is downplayed and first names are the norm, even at work. People value humility, fairness and pitching in, the “number eight wire” knack for practical problem-solving is a point of pride.

Kanorau

Diverse & multicultural

Alongside its bicultural foundation, New Zealand is home to large Pacific, Asian, South African, British and many other communities, so you’ll rarely be the only one who has come from somewhere else.

A note on the everyday

Life here is comfortable and modern but a little more low-key than some are used to: shops close earlier, cities are smaller, and the pace is gentler. For most people that’s precisely the appeal. The trade is a simpler, more spacious kind of life, and it’s one our clinicians rarely regret.

Sophie’s insight

When my own family arrived, the thing that moved me most wasn’t the scenery, it was how readily people made space for us. Learn a few words of te reo, say yes to the invitation to the beach, ask people how to pronounce their town’s name properly. None of it is hard, and all of it tells New Zealand you’ve come to belong, not just to work. That openness, more than anything, is what turns a posting into a home.

SophieCEO & Founder, Ethicare Resourcing

Common questions

The things people ask us first

Do I need to speak te reo Māori?

Not at all. English is the everyday working language, and you’ll get by perfectly without a word of te reo. That said, learning a handful of common greetings and saying place names correctly is warmly received, and it’s a lovely, easy way to show you respect where you’ve come to live. You’ll absorb plenty just by being here.

Why does the Treaty matter for my work in healthcare?

Te Tiriti o Waitangi underpins how public services, including health, are designed and delivered. In practice that means the system places real emphasis on equity and on improving outcomes for Māori, and you’ll see that reflected in policy, training and day-to-day care. You don’t need to be an expert, an open, respectful willingness to understand it is exactly what’s hoped for.

Is New Zealand very different culturally from home?

It depends where you’re coming from, but most newcomers find it familiar enough to settle quickly and different enough to feel like a genuine change. It’s a modern, English-speaking democracy with a relaxed, outdoorsy, egalitarian culture and a strong bicultural heart. The adjustments tend to be gentle, a slower pace, smaller cities and earlier nights rather than anything jarring.

Will my family fit in?

Almost always, yes. New Zealand is welcoming and diverse, and there are well-established communities from all over the world. Children in particular settle fast, helped by friendly schools and an outdoor childhood. We’re also glad to connect you with families who’ve made the same move, so you arrive with a few familiar faces rather than none.
