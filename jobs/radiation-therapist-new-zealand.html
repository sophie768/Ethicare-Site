What the work is actually likeYou know every patient on the unit, and they know you.

The treatment itself will feel familiar. How radiotherapy is organised here is what changes the job, and it is worth understanding before you shortlist locations.

Continuity

You follow the whole course of treatment

Teams are smaller and less segmented than in large overseas services, so many therapists move between pre-treatment, planning and delivery instead of sitting in one section for years. You see the same people from CT simulation through to their final fraction. Therapists tell us this is the thing they did not know they were missing.

Geography

Where you live decides where you work

There are only a handful of radiation oncology centres in the country, so the order of decisions is different from most imaging professions: settle where you want to live, and the shortlist of services follows. The upside is that these centres are well resourced, and you will know your whole department by name within a fortnight.

Equity

Equity sits inside the clinical conversation

Māori and Pacific patients experience worse cancer outcomes here, and services are actively organised around closing that gap. Travel and accommodation support, patient navigation and working with whānau are part of the treatment pathway rather than an add-on. Expect it in your interview, and expect it to change how you plan someone’s course around their life.

Technique range is good for the size of the country. Conformal and intensity-modulated treatment, image guidance, stereotactic work and brachytherapy are all delivered in New Zealand, though not every centre offers everything. If a specific technique matters to your development, that is a question to ask about a particular service rather than about the country.

Progression runs through depth, teaching and research. Planning and dosimetry specialisation, technique lead roles, clinical education and student supervision, quality and trials work, and charge therapist positions are the established routes. Advanced practice is developing here rather than fully formalised, so an early conversation about where you want to end up shapes the role.

Planning systems and record-and-verify platforms are the mainstream ones you will already have met, and services train you across their own configuration. Nobody expects you to arrive knowing it.

The trade is real and worth naming: less choice of city, more of the work you came into the profession for. We would rather talk that through with you properly than sell you a location.

Getting registeredRegistration, in brief

Reviewed July 2026 · checked against the Medical Radiation Technologists Board’s current published guidance

You register with the Medical Radiation Technologists Board, which assesses your qualification, clinical experience and competency against New Zealand standards. Once registered you hold an Annual Practising Certificate.

What the Board will typically want to see:

  • Verification of your qualification
  • Evidence of recent clinical practice
  • A certificate of good standing from every authority you have registered with
  • A police or criminal-history clearance
  • Confirmation of English-language proficiency, if required

Document shelf life is what catches people out. Certificates of good standing and certified copies have a short validity window, and the Board’s window and Immigration New Zealand’s are not the same — so the order you do things in matters more than the speed. We map that sequence with you, and review your documents before you submit anything.

The full detail — both pathways, the document checklist, fees and what to do if something is missing — lives on the registration guide, which is where we keep it up to date.

Life in New ZealandFewer cities to choose from, and good ones.

A short commute, and the coast or the hills at the end of it.

Cancer services sit in provincial cities and mid-sized centres rather than clustered in one metro area. If you are coming from a large city that can read as a downgrade, and for most people it turns out to be the opposite: the commute is measured in minutes, there is a garden, and the thing you drive to at the weekend is half an hour away rather than three hours.

It also changes what your time off is for. A six-week course of treatment is emotionally heavy work, and having somewhere to put that down properly at the end of a shift is not a lifestyle bonus. It is part of doing this job for a long time.

In a regional city, the mountains are a Saturday trip.

Where you could workHow to choose, when there are only a handful of services

Most professions start from the vacancy and work outwards. Radiation therapy runs the other way round, and it pays to know that before you start looking.

Start with the map

Decide where you want to live first. With so few services the list of places you could work is short enough to hold in your head, and the location decision is the one that is hard to undo.

Main centres

Larger services, more subspecialisation, students on placement, and a bigger roster to share out-of-hours cover between.

Provincial services

Smaller teams, more of the pathway in your own hands, and a shorter distance between you and the people making the decisions.

Timing

Options in any given month are counted in ones and twos rather than dozens. We tell you what is open, not what we would like to be.

Common questions

Frequently asked

Does my Australian registration and experience count?

Australian-trained and Ahpra-registered therapists are usually the simplest group to move, helped by the trans-Tasman recognition arrangement between the two countries. It is still an application to the MRTB rather than an automatic transfer, and the Board will want evidence of recent practice. We confirm what applies to you before anything is lodged.

There are so few centres. Will that limit my career?

It limits where you live more than what you do. With a small number of services, the honest position is that your options in any given month are counted in ones and twos, not dozens, and timing matters. What you get in exchange is breadth: rotation across pre-treatment, planning and delivery, and a genuine say in how your role develops because the team is small enough for that conversation to happen.

Most therapists who move here find their next step is within their service rather than by changing employer — which is a different career shape, not a worse one.

Will I be siloed into planning or into treatment?

Usually the opposite. Most services expect movement across the pathway, and many therapists prefer it. If you want to specialise in planning and dosimetry you can, and there are real roles in it, but you will typically keep some delivery work alongside rather than leaving the units entirely.

What are the hours and on-call patterns?

Treatment units run to a daytime list, with extended hours in the larger centres. Palliative and urgent treatment means some out-of-hours or weekend cover in most services, and how that is arranged varies quite a bit between centres.

It is one of the specific things we ask about on your behalf during offer discussions, because the answer differs enough to change how a role feels.

Do I need experience of a particular planning system?

No. Services here use the mainstream planning and record-and-verify platforms you will already have worked with in some form, and they train you on their own configuration. What employers care about is your planning judgement and your patient care, not which vendor’s interface you learned on.

What is the team structure like?

Small and flat. You will work closely with radiation oncologists and medical physicists, often in the same room rather than through a referral chain, and decisions get made faster because there are fewer layers to go through. Therapists arriving from very large departments often say the access to senior colleagues is the biggest single change.

It also means visibility. Good work is noticed here, and so is the other kind.
