Welcome to Victoria

A warm welcome, you’re in good hands

Deciding to move your life and career to the other side of the world is a big step, and an exciting one. Whether Victoria is already home to people you know or a brand-new chapter, I want you to feel supported from the very first conversation.

Victoria is Australia’s cultural heart: Melbourne’s laneways, coffee, sport and arts, wrapped around a compact, walkable city, with surf coast, wine country, alpine snow and the Great Ocean Road all within a short drive. It offers big-city depth with genuine four-season variety, and its hospitals are among the best in the country.

This guide walks you through healthcare, visas and registration, finances, housing, schooling and cost of living across the state, then breaks down the main places you might live so you can picture daily life in each. Think of it as the conversation we’d have over coffee, in your pocket, whenever you need it.

Welcome to Victoria. We’re so glad you’re here, and we’ll be alongside you every step of the way.

Ethicare insight

Candidates often picture Australia as one job market. It isn’t. Victoria registers nationally through Ahpra but hires locally through its health services, and demand across the regional cities is strong, often with incentives a headline Melbourne salary won’t show.

Overview · Culture

Ancient country, cultural capital

The Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung people of the Kulin Nation are the traditional custodians of the Melbourne region, known as Naarm, part of one of the oldest living cultures on earth, a heritage increasingly woven into Victorian life.

Victoria is Australia’s cultural capital, famous for its coffee and laneways, its live music and sport, and a calendar that runs from the Australian Open and the AFL to the Melbourne Cup and endless festivals. Beyond the city the pace softens fast into surf coast, wine country, goldfields towns and the alpine high country.

Sophie’s tip

Learn the local names early, Naarm (Melbourne) and the Country names across the state. Using them warmly is genuinely appreciated and helps you feel part of the community faster.

Overview · Transport

Getting around

Melbourne has one of the world’s great public-transport networks, including the largest tram system on earth, so you rarely need a car in the inner city. Beyond the metro area, driving comes back into the picture.

Myki & trams

Trains, trams and buses run on the tap-on Myki card, with the free City Circle tram and Free Tram Zone in central Melbourne, one of the world’s largest tram networks.

Driving

Australians drive on the left. Watch for Melbourne’s famous 'hook turns’ in the CBD; a car is useful for the coast, the regions and the alps.

Trains

Metro and V/Line regional trains reach Geelong, Ballarat, Bendigo and beyond, so many people commute in from the regional cities.

Flying

Melbourne Airport (Tullamarine) and Avalon link to every capital and regional Victoria.

Sophie’s tip

You can usually drive on a valid overseas licence for a period after arriving, but you’ll need to transfer to a Victorian licence once you’re a resident. Pick up a Myki in your first week, and read up on hook turns before you drive in the CBD.

Overview · Climate

A year in the Victorian climate

Victoria is genuinely four-season and famous for 'four seasons in a day’, warm summers, crisp winters and, in the north-east, real alpine snow. Remember, the seasons are flipped from the Northern Hemisphere.

Figures are indicative for Melbourne, whose weather is famously changeable, layers are your friend. Summers can be hot with occasional heat spikes; winters are cold and grey but rarely freezing in the city; and the alpine resorts (Falls Creek, Mount Buller, Hotham) have a real ski season from about June to September.

Ethicare insight

Two things catch newcomers out. First, Melbourne’s weather can swing 15 degrees in a day, always carry a layer. Second, winters are genuinely cold and many homes are under-heated, so budget for warm bedding and heating for your first winter, a point worth making to family before they arrive.

Sophie’s View

Why I like Victoria

One of the things I love most about Victoria is how much it fits into such a compact state. Whether you’re drawn to Melbourne’s culture and coffee, a coastal city like Geelong or a heritage town like Ballarat or Bendigo, there’s usually somewhere that fits your priorities. For healthcare professionals, that flexibility means you rarely have to choose between career progression and quality of life, you can often have both.

Having spent many years in the NHS, I know how easily work can take over. What stands out to me about Victoria is how much people make of the seasons, a coffee and a gallery in winter, the coast or the vines in summer, even the snow in the alps. If I could give one piece of advice, it would be to think about your commute, your community and what you’ll enjoy on your days off. Those things shape your happiness more than people expect.

Sophie

Section two · Healthcare

Healthcare in Victoria

Victoria runs one of the country’s strongest public health systems, universal Medicare cover, a network of major tertiary hospitals and a large private sector, delivered through local health services led by Melbourne’s world-renowned centres.

Go deeperUnderstanding healthcare in Australia→

Royal Melbourne & St Vincent’s

Inner Melbourne’s leading tertiary hospitals and major trauma centres, with global research reputations.

The Alfred

Hosts the country’s largest ICU and statewide trauma, burns and heart-lung services.

Monash & Austin Health

The state’s largest health service in the south-east, and the north-east’s leader in transplants and rehabilitation.

Regional referral hospitals

Barwon Health (Geelong), Ballarat, Bendigo and Latrobe Regional anchor their regions with major referral hospitals.

Good to know

Eligibility for Medicare usually begins once you hold an appropriate visa. Reciprocal Health Care Agreements give some countries limited Medicare access. Ambulance cover is not automatic in Victoria, an Ambulance Victoria membership is inexpensive and widely recommended, and many people add it to their private health insurance.

Visa rules change regularly. Always confirm current requirements with the Department of Home Affairs (homeaffairs.gov.au) and your Ethicare contact before making decisions. Victoria’s skilled-nomination programs are run by the state and update through the year.

Registration · Ahpra

Professional registration

Most healthcare professions are regulated nationally through Ahpra, the Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency, and its profession-specific boards.

One national register

Once registered with Ahpra you can work anywhere in Australia, including all of Victoria, registration is national, not state-by-state.

Qualification assessment

Overseas qualifications are assessed against Australian standards; some roles require bridging programs or exams.

English language

You’ll usually need to evidence English proficiency through an approved test or a recognised pathway.

Start early

Registration takes time, begin gathering documents and references well before you plan to travel.

Ethicare insight

Across our Victorian moves, the one thing that delays a start is documents left too late. Certified qualifications, references and ID take real time to gather, so begin the moment Melbourne or the regions feel like a genuine option.

Section four · Banking & Finance

Setting up your finances

Getting your finances in order early makes day-to-day life much easier. Most of this can be started before you arrive.

Opening a bank account

The major banks are CommBank, Westpac, NAB and ANZ, plus Melbourne’s own Bendigo Bank. Many let you open online before you arrive, then verify ID in branch with your passport.

Tax File Number (TFN)

Every worker needs a TFN from the ATO. Apply online once you arrive and give the number to payroll so you’re taxed correctly. Without it, you’ll be taxed at the highest rate.

Sophie’s tip

Open your bank account before you fly where you can, having an account ready makes your payroll setup and first rent payment far smoother in those hectic first weeks.

The money set-up, in order

1Activate your bank account, verify ID in branch, order a debit card and online banking.
2Apply for your TFN, online via the ATO, then give it to payroll immediately.
3Choose your super fund, or accept the employer default, then review later.
4Set up salary packaging, ask payroll or your provider on day one if you’re in public health.
5Register for Medicare, if eligible under your visa or a reciprocal agreement.
6Get a local SIM & number, many banking and government steps need an Australian mobile.
Sophie’s tip

The pay packet is only half the picture. The clinicians who land in Victoria most smoothly budget for the bridge, the few weeks between arriving and your first full pay clearing. Think about temporary accommodation, a rental bond (typically one month’s rent) lodged with the RTBA, a first car, and setting up a home. None of it is dramatic, but together it’s real, and planning for it removes almost all of the early stress.

Sophie Careem · Founder & Director

Section seven · Housing & Areas

Finding a place to live

From Melbourne apartments to coastal and regional homes, where you live in Victoria shapes your commute and your budget. In a competitive market, preparation wins.

Rentals move quickly in popular areas. You’ll usually apply with ID, references and proof of income, then pay a bond (typically one month’s rent) lodged with the Residential Tenancies Bond Authority (RTBA). Many newcomers rent short-term first to learn an area. The main sites are realestate.com.au (largest) and domain.com.au.

Choosing your area, the order that saves the most stress

1Fix your work site first. Everything flows from your commute, decide it before you fall for a suburb.
2Check school catchments if you have children. Local intake is guaranteed; out-of-area isn’t.
3Shortlist two or three areas and rent short-term in one before committing.
4Weigh the tram/train lines. Proximity to a good line shapes daily life in Melbourne.

Utilities & getting set up

Power & gas

Choose a retailer (AGL, Origin, EnergyAustralia and others); Victoria has its own comparison site, Victorian Energy Compare.

Water

Your local retailer (Yarra Valley, South East or Greater Western Water); if renting, most water charges are the landlord’s.

Internet

NBN via Telstra, Optus, TPG or Aussie Broadband.

Setting up home

Kmart, IKEA and Bunnings furnish a first home fast; Marketplace for second-hand.

Sophie’s tip

No Australian rental references? Bring landlord references from home, proof of income and savings, and your job offer, all as clean PDFs ready to send. A complete, instant application often beats a longer local history.

Housing & family · Choosing where to live

Choosing where to live

For many healthcare professionals, the job is only half the decision. The other half is where your family will settle well. Schools, commute, housing cost and lifestyle are best weighed together. Here’s a practical way to think it through.

Schools often come first

Access to a government school is usually tied to where you live. Popular schools can be pinned to an exact address, catchments can change street by street, and out-of-area places aren’t guaranteed. If you have children, check the catchment before you sign a long-term lease. Independent, Catholic and faith schools offer more flexibility, but usually charge fees.

Your commute still matters

Melbourne has one of the world’s great tram and train networks, so many people don’t need a car in the inner city, though one is useful for the coast, the regions and the alps. Think about your shift start and finish times, whether the trams and trains realistically reach your site, and parking, which is a real cost near the big hospitals.

The order that usually works best

A simple sequence that keeps the big decisions in the right order.

  1. 1Confirm your likely work site.
  2. 2Shortlist suitable school options if you have children.
  3. 3Check the catchment maps for those schools.
  4. 4Compare commute times to work at shift hours.
  5. 5Review rental availability and cost.
  6. 6Rent short-term first where you can.
  7. 7Choose a longer-term suburb once you know the area.

Section eight · Shopping & Food

Groceries, food & community

The essentials in one place, the big chains, the budget picks, and where Victoria’s many communities find the flavours of home, this is one of the most diverse states in the country.

The big chains

Woolworths & Coles for full-range everywhere; ALDI for the cheapest weekly shop; IGA and independents for neighbourhood top-ups, vital in regional towns.

Flavours of home

Footscray and Springvale for Vietnamese and South-East Asian; Brunswick and Coburg for Middle-Eastern and Halal; Box Hill and Glen Waverley for Chinese, Korean and Malaysian; Carlton for Italian; and Dandenong for a world of cuisines.

Sophie’s tip

Bring your own bags, lightweight single-use plastic bags are banned across Victoria, and most people keep a few reusable ones in the car for exactly this reason.

Good to know

Melbourne has an enormous range of specialist food. Halal butchers and grocers are easy to find (Brunswick, Coburg, Dandenong); kosher supplies cluster around Caulfield and the south-east, home to one of Australia’s largest Jewish communities; and every Asian, African and European staple is well stocked. In the regional centres it’s more limited, Geelong, Ballarat, Bendigo and Shepparton have good halal ranges, but dedicated kosher supplies are largely Melbourne-based, so many regional families stock up on a city trip or order online.

Faith & community · Places of worship

Religion & places of worship

Australia has no state religion and law protects freedom of belief. Victoria is highly religiously diverse: at the last census around a third of residents reported no religion, Christianity remained the largest faith, and Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism and Judaism are all large and well established, most strongly across Greater Melbourne, with active communities in the regional centres too.

Most communities list service and prayer times online and warmly welcome newcomers, often one of the quickest ways to build a support network after you arrive.

The main faiths & where to find them

Christianity

Catholic, Anglican, Uniting, Pentecostal and Orthodox churches statewide. Landmark cathedrals include St Patrick’s (Catholic) and St Paul’s (Anglican) in Melbourne.

Islam

A large, long-established Muslim community, with major mosques including the Preston and Broadmeadows mosques, plus mosques across the suburbs and in the regional cities.

Hinduism

Temples across Melbourne, including the Shri Shiva Vishnu Temple in Carrum Downs, serve a large community with festivals through the year.

Buddhism

Many temples and centres, including the Quang Minh Temple in Braybrook and Vietnamese, Thai and Sri Lankan temples across the suburbs.

Sikhism

Gurdwaras across Melbourne and in regional Victoria welcome worshippers and visitors, with community kitchens (langar) open to all.

Judaism

One of Australia’s largest Jewish communities, centred on Caulfield and the south-east, with synagogues, schools, museums and kosher supplies.

A guide to the main faith communities in each city in this guide. Melbourne has the fullest range; the regional centres focus on Christian churches and a local mosque.

Faith & community · Diversity & inclusion

Diversity, inclusion & belonging

Victoria is one of the world’s most multicultural societies, and the law protects everyone’s right to be treated equally. Whoever you are, and whoever your family is, you’re welcome here.

Go deeperReligion, community & belonging in Australia→

A multicultural home

Victoria is one of Australia’s most culturally diverse states, with around a third of residents born overseas and communities from every corner of the world. Victorian Government multicultural programs and local councils run settlement and community services to help newcomers connect and belong.

Your rights in law

The Victorian Equal Opportunity Act 2010, the Charter of Human Rights and Responsibilities Act 2006 and federal law protect people from discrimination on the basis of race, religion, age, sex, disability, sexuality and gender identity. The Victorian Equal Opportunity and Human Rights Commission handles complaints.

LGBTQ+ inclusion

Same-sex marriage has been legal Australia-wide since 2017, and Victoria has some of the country’s strongest LGBTQ+ protections, including a ban on conversion practices. The Midsumma Festival and the Pride March are part of the calendar, and QLife (1800 184 527) offers free, anonymous peer support.

Inclusive workplaces & care

A growing number of health services hold Rainbow Tick accreditation or run active inclusion networks. Interpreters are free through TIS National (131 450), and the NDIS supports people with disability.

Ethicare insight

Victoria’s public health services and most employers have active diversity and inclusion commitments, and many run staff networks for culturally diverse, First Nations and LGBTQ+ workers. If belonging at work matters to you, tell us, we can factor a team’s culture and inclusion record into where we suggest you land.

Section ten · Education & Family

Schooling & family life

Victoria offers a full range of early childhood education, schools and universities, run by the state’s Department of Education. The school year runs late January to mid-December across four terms, a flip from the Northern Hemisphere calendar worth planning around.

Go deeperEducation & schools in Australia→

Early childhood

Long daycare, kindergarten and family day care for ages 0–5, with subsidies.

Primary

The first years of school through to the end of primary, across government, Catholic and independent schools.

Secondary

The senior years, ending with the Victorian Certificate of Education (VCE), leading to university or training.

Tertiary

The University of Melbourne, Monash, RMIT, Deakin and La Trobe, plus TAFE across the state.

Public school fees for visa-holder families

Victoria is the simplest state for families: dependent children of 482 visa holders are not charged tuition to attend government schools.

Ethicare insight

Victoria is the easiest state on school fees. Dependent children of 482 visa holders attend government schools without paying tuition, the same as local families, though schools may ask for voluntary contributions and charge for materials, excursions and some elective (VET) subjects. Non-government schools set their own fees. It’s one less thing to budget for when you’re weighing up where to move.

School catchments come first

For Victoria government schools, your home address usually determines your local-intake (catchment) school, children inside the zone are generally guaranteed a place, while out-of-area places depend on capacity, and popular schools can be oversubscribed. Boundaries can run street-by-street, so always check the exact address before you sign.

For families, the school decision usually leads the housing decision here, deciding it first prevents an expensive mid-year move.

Education & Family · Partners & work

Employment for your partner

A move only works if it works for the whole family. The good news: on most skilled and employer-sponsored visas your partner is included with full work rights, free to take any job, full or part-time, with any employer.

Work rights come as standard

Partners added to a 482, 186, or skilled 189/190/491 visa generally have unrestricted work rights, no separate employer sponsorship needed. Always confirm the exact conditions on your grant letter.

A broad job market

Victoria is a large, diverse economy, with demand across healthcare, education, construction, technology, hospitality, logistics and professional services, strongest in Greater Melbourne, with real opportunities in the regional cities too.

Helping your partner land work

1Reformat the CV to Australian style, concise, achievement-led, no photo or date of birth. Set up a strong LinkedIn with an Australian location.
2Search the main boards, SEEK, Indeed and Jora, plus Victorian Government careers and health-service sites for public-sector roles.
3Check if the profession is regulated, teaching, nursing, trades and others need registration or a skills assessment. Start it before you arrive.
4Register with recruitment agencies, many Victorian roles are filled through agencies, who can also advise on local expectations and pay.
Ethicare insight

Partner employment is one of the biggest factors in whether a move sticks, so we build it into the plan rather than leaving it to chance. Tell us early what your partner does; if they’re also in healthcare, we can often help them directly in Victoria.
