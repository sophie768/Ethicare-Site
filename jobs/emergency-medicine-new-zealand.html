What the work is actually like

The consultant is in the department, not on the end of a phone.

The medicine is the medicine. What FACEMs notice in the first week is where the consultant stands, and what comes through the door.

Consultant-delivered

You see patients alongside your registrars

Consultant-delivered care is the working norm rather than a strategy document, and departments are small enough that you know everyone’s name inside a fortnight. The flip side is honest: fewer people to hide behind on a bad night.

The caseload

More outdoors than motorway

Trauma here skews towards the water, the hills and the farm rather than the ring road. Drowning, hypothermia, mountain and marine injuries turn up in volumes most incoming consultants have not seen, and rural departments see them first.

The resus room

Whānau are in the room

Te ao Māori shapes how families are involved in resuscitation and in the conversation afterwards. Nobody expects you to arrive fluent. Most consultants describe it as the part of the move that changed their practice most.

Departments are smaller than the ones most FACEMs arrive from. That cuts both ways. There is less subspecialty streaming, and in a regional ED you are the paediatric resus consultant, the toxicology opinion and the trauma team leader on the same shift. Consultants who want breadth find it immediately; consultants who want a narrow role usually need a tertiary centre.

Overnight consultant presence is partial rather than resident in most departments. How much of the night you personally carry varies enormously between EDs, and it is the single biggest difference between two jobs that look identical on paper. The honest answer is the roster itself, so we send it before you interview.

If the roles drawing you are in small hospitals, look at rural hospital medicine as well — it is a separate vocational scope, and some acute posts sit inside it rather than inside emergency medicine.

Getting registered

Fast, but only for one qualification

Reviewed August 2026 · checked against the Medical Council of New Zealand’s current published guidance

You register with the Medical Council of New Zealand in the vocational scope of emergency medicine. The route is the same one every specialist follows; three details are specific to you.

The UK route is the fast one. Emergency medicine is an approved area for the fast-track pathway, and the approved list names the UK fellowship together with a CCT or CCST. The Council publishes twenty working days for a complete application on that pathway.

FACEM is effectively a domestic qualification. Trained through ACEM in Australia? Your fellowship is approved, with no comparability assessment and no college advice to wait on.

Ireland is not on the approved list, and that surprises people every time. Irish and other overseas qualifications take the college-assessed route, where ACEM advises the Council against the FACEM standard using its own additional-information forms. Start that paperwork early.

What the Council will want to see, whichever route you are on:

  • A consultant-level job offer — a listed prerequisite on the fast-track pathway
  • Primary-source verification of your medical degree and specialist qualification
  • Evidence of at least 24 months’ recent practice in the specialty
  • A certificate of good standing from every authority you have registered with
  • Identity, criminal-history and English-language evidence where required

The order you do things in matters more than the speed. Certificates of good standing and certified copies have short validity windows, and the Council’s window and Immigration New Zealand’s are not the same. We map that sequence with you and review your documents before anything is lodged.

Which vocational pathway fits you

Almost every emergency physician we move arrives by one of four MCNZ vocational pathways. They differ in what you already hold, not in how good a doctor you are — and the one that applies to you decides your timeline more than anything else in the move.

VOC4 · fast track

A UK fellowship, with a CCT or CCST

Emergency medicine is on the Council’s fast-track list, but the United Kingdom is the only country on the approved list for it: Fellowship of the College of Emergency Medicine with a CCT or CCST. An Irish or other overseas qualification goes to VOC3 instead, and a FACEM to VOC1 or VOC2.

20 working daysCouncil’s published time for a complete application
VOC1

FACEM, and already registered here

An approved Australasian postgraduate qualification plus general registration in New Zealand. Nothing is assessed for comparability, because your fellowship already is the standard.

10 working daysThe quickest route the Council publishes
VOC2

FACEM, but no NZ general registration

The same approved Australasian qualification, without general registration here yet. Most ACEM fellows moving from Australia for the first time come this way.

20 working daysFrom a complete application
VOC3

Trained outside New Zealand and Australia

Your training and experience are assessed on their merits, with ACEM advising the Council against the FACEM standard. This is the route where early college paperwork earns you the most time.

Around three monthsIn most cases, once the application is complete

What the fast track actually asks of you

VOC4 has prerequisites the other routes don’t, and one of them surprises people: a job offer in Aotearoa New Zealand at consultant or specialist level is a listed prerequisite, so the role comes first and the application is built around it. You’ll also need 24 months’ practice in emergency medicine at 0.5 FTE or more within the past five years — 12 of them in the 18 months before you apply — in a health system the Council treats as comparable, three verified references, and your qualifications already moving through EPIC / MyIntealth. Verification is usually the slowest single step, which is why we start it before anything else.

And what follows approval

VOC3 and VOC4 both lead to provisional vocational registration rather than the full scope. You practise in emergency medicine under a named supervisor in an approved position — supervision is a condition of the provisional scope, not a judgement on you. If your job, location or supervisor changes during it, you apply to vary your scope at least 20 days ahead.

Two things worth diarising. Your registration meeting happens in New Zealand, within two weeks of your intended start date, and you cannot practise until it is done and your practising certificate issued — allow about three days after the meeting for that. And moving to the full vocational scope is an application you make once the Council’s requirements are met, not something that arrives on a date.

Where this comes from. The pathway definitions, prerequisites, processing times and approved areas of medicine above are the Medical Council of New Zealand’s own published guidance, read in August 2026 against its approved-qualifications list dated 13 August 2026. Times are for complete applications; an incomplete one restarts the clock, and the list is added to periodically. Check your own case against the Council before you make a decision on it.

Life in New Zealand

You finish a run of nights and the mountains are half an hour away.

A short commute, and the coast or the hills at the end of it.

Emergency medicine is hard to do for thirty years anywhere. What makes it possible is what happens between the shifts, and that is the honest argument for working here: the commute is measured in minutes, and the thing you drive to at the weekend is close enough to reach on a day off rather than a holiday.

It also changes the shape of a career. Consultants here more often stay in the same department for a decade and build something in it, because the reasons people usually leave — the commute, the cost of a house, never seeing the family — are smaller problems than they were.

A regional post means the mountains are a Saturday, not a holiday.

Where you could work

Every department is recruiting, so the question is which kind

Emergency medicine is one of the few specialties where you can genuinely choose the place first. What differs between them is how much of the department rests on you.

Tertiary departments

Trauma, paediatric and toxicology streams, ACEM trainees to teach, and a roster with people in front of you.

Regional and provincial

Where the majority of consultant posts sit. Broad caseload, real autonomy, and a department you know entirely.

Rural hospitals

Generalist acute care with a wide remit. Some of these posts sit under rural hospital medicine rather than emergency medicine.

Retrieval and pre-hospital

Helicopter and fixed-wing services contracted regionally. For many FACEMs this is the reason to take a regional post.

What we can’t promise

A move this size deserves the limits as well as the pitch. Three things you will never hear us guarantee — and what we do instead.

Timelines that aren’t ours to give

Registration sits with the Medical Council and visas with Immigration New Zealand. We give you the realistic range, keep your file complete and moving, and chase what can be chased — but we will not quote a faster number to win you over.

A department that will suit you

Two EDs with the same name on the door can be entirely different jobs. We will tell you what the overnight cover really looks like, even when it costs us the placement, because you cannot un-move a family.

That the move is right for you

Some moves shouldn’t happen. If the scope, the pay maths or the family logistics don’t stack up, we will say so plainly. We would rather lose a placement than see you land in the wrong life.

Common questions

Frequently asked

How bad are the nights?

Consultants take a share of evenings and nights, and overnight consultant presence is partial rather than resident in most departments. It varies enormously by ED, so the honest answer is the roster itself — we send it before you interview.

Will I be running the paediatric resus too?

Outside the few centres with a dedicated paediatric ED, yes — a general department sees all ages. Most incoming consultants find the volume manageable and the paediatric support better than they expected.

Is retrieval work available?

Yes. Helicopter and fixed-wing retrieval is contracted regionally, and a retrieval commitment inside the working week is common. For a lot of FACEMs it is the reason to take a regional post over a city one.

What if the job is really rural hospital medicine?

It happens, and it is not a trick — some acute posts in small hospitals genuinely sit in that separate vocational scope. It changes your registration route, so we check which scope a role belongs to before you apply rather than after.
