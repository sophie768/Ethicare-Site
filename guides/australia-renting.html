Renting
in Australia

There is no national tenancy law. Eight states and territories, eight rulebooks, and one application problem every arriving clinician shares.

Start here

There is no national tenancy law in Australia

This is the thing that catches people out, and everything else on this page follows from it. Renting is regulated by each state and territory, not by the Commonwealth.

Eight jurisdictions, eight separate Residential Tenancies Acts, eight different bond authorities and eight different sets of rules on how often rent can rise, what minimum standards a property must meet, how much notice ends a tenancy and whether you can keep a pet. Someone who rented in Melbourne and then moves to Brisbane is learning a new system, not a regional variation of the same one.

So the useful question is never “how does renting work in Australia”. It is “how does renting work in the state I am moving to”. The table below points you at the right authority for each one — they are the primary source, they are free, and they are more current than any guide including this one.

What is consistent across the country is the shape of the process: you find a property listed online, you attend a short inspection, you apply afterwards in competition with other applicants, and the successful applicant pays a bond that is lodged with a government body rather than held by the agent. The rest of this page is about that shape, and about the one part of it that is harder for an arriving clinician than for a local.

The honest difficulty

The real obstacle is not money. It is that you have no history here.

Australian agents rarely accept or reject an application. They rank the applications they receive and put a shortlist to the owner, who chooses. Understanding that changes what you should be trying to do.

A local applicant arrives with things you do not have: a rental ledger showing two years of payments on time, references from an Australian agent, and a local credit file. You are not being judged as unsuitable. You are being judged as unknown, and an owner choosing between a known quantity and an unknown one will usually take the known one unless you have given them a reason not to.

The good news, and it is good: a signed healthcare employment contract is one of the strongest documents in that pile. Clinical employment reads as stable, well-paid and unlikely to evaporate. Many arriving clinicians are, on paper, a better financial prospect than the local applicant with the rental ledger. The task is to make that legible.

In our experience

The applications that succeed are the ones that answer the questions nobody asked out loud

An agent looking at an overseas application has three unspoken worries: can this person actually pay, will they still be in the country in a year, and can I verify any of this. A pack that answers all three before being asked tends to move to the top of the pile.

The single most effective thing we see is a short covering note. Three or four sentences, no more, that says what you do, who you are employed by, when you start and that you are happy to provide anything else. It costs nothing and it is startlingly rare.

What to prepare

Building an application that competes

Have all of this ready as PDFs before you view anything. Applications are usually submitted through an online platform within hours of an inspection, and the ones that go in complete and first do best.

What every application asks for

  • Photo identification. Passport, and your driving licence if you have it. Most platforms score you on points of ID.
  • Proof of income. Your signed employment contract or offer letter is the key document. Recent payslips from your current post help.
  • Proof of funds. A bank statement showing you can cover bond, rent in advance and the first weeks.
  • Rental references. From your current landlord or agent, wherever in the world that is. Translated if not in English.
  • Personal or professional references. A colleague or manager is fine, and easy to arrange before you leave.
  • Visa evidence. Your grant notice or application status, which tells the owner how long you can stay.

What separates a strong application

  • The covering note. Three sentences: what you do, where you will be working, when you arrive. Nothing more.
  • Offering to pay more rent in advance rather than more bond. Bond is capped by law; rent in advance usually is not, and it reassures without breaking any rule.
  • Being contactable in Australian hours during the week you apply, even if that is inconvenient from your time zone.
  • Applying to fewer properties, properly. Six complete applications beat twenty rushed ones.
  • A local guarantor if you happen to have one, useful, but never necessary, and never worth delaying for.
  • Honesty about pets. Rules vary by state and hiding an animal is the fastest way to lose a tenancy.
The money, as rules

Bond and what you pay up front

We deliberately publish no rent or bond amounts anywhere on this site. They move every quarter, and a figure that was true when this was written would mislead you later. The rules behind the numbers are what last.

  1. Bond is expressed in weeks, not dollars

    It is a multiple of the weekly rent, and it is capped by your state or territory’s tenancy law. Four weeks is the common maximum for an unfurnished home, with some jurisdictions allowing more where the property is furnished or the rent is above a threshold. Because it is a multiple, it tracks the market automatically, which is exactly why quoting an amount here would be pointless.

  2. You will also pay rent in advance

    Typically two weeks, sometimes more, paid at signing on top of the bond. Together these are the largest single outlay of the whole move for most people, and the one worth having liquid and ready before you land.

  3. A government authority holds your bond, not the agent

    This is a genuine protection and it is worth understanding. In every state and territory the bond is lodged with the relevant authority from the table above, and neither you nor the owner can release it unilaterally at the end of the tenancy. You should receive written confirmation of the lodgement. If you do not, ask for it.

  4. Rent is usually paid fortnightly

    Australian rent is quoted per week and most commonly paid fortnightly or monthly by direct debit. If you are used to thinking in monthly rent, multiply the weekly figure by 52 and divide by 12 rather than by four. The difference over a year is most of an extra month.

  5. Letting fees are not charged to tenants

    Unlike some countries, you should not be asked to pay a fee to the agent for the privilege of applying or being granted a tenancy. If you are, that is a warning sign worth checking against your state authority.

For what a month actually looks like in a particular city, which lines are large, which are small, and which catch people out, each of our destination guides has a cost-of-living chapter written for that place, with live calculators so you can compare against the city you are leaving.

A practical surprise

“Unfurnished” does not mean what you might expect

This one costs arriving families real money in their first fortnight, and almost nobody warns them.

Usually not included

  • A fridge. The single most common surprise. Unfurnished means no fridge, and you will want one on day one.
  • A washing machine, and a dryer, though in much of Australia a line in the sun does the job for most of the year.
  • A dishwasher in older stock, though newer builds and apartments usually have one fitted.
  • Furniture of any kind, including beds, in the ordinary sense of unfurnished.

Usually included

  • Built-in wardrobes in most bedrooms. Freestanding wardrobes are far less common here than in Europe.
  • Oven, cooktop and rangehood, fitted as standard.
  • Window coverings, blinds or curtains, which matters more than it sounds given the summer sun.
  • Air conditioning or a reverse-cycle heat pump in most of the country, and increasingly treated as essential rather than a luxury.
  • Ceiling fans, often in every room in the warmer states.

The practical answer is to buy second-hand for the first year. Australia has a large, entirely unsnobbish second-hand market for whitegoods and furniture, and buying a used fridge and washing machine in your first week is completely normal behaviour rather than a sign of struggling. Replace them properly once you know which suburb you actually want to stay in.

How it actually goes

Open homes, and how quickly this moves

The rhythm is different from a private appointment with an agent, and it rewards preparation over deliberation.

Most properties are shown at an open home. A fixed slot, often fifteen minutes on a Saturday morning, where anyone who has registered turns up at once. You will be one of several groups walking through the same rooms. You register online beforehand, you are asked to sign in on arrival, and the agent is there to answer questions rather than to sell you anything.

You do not negotiate at the inspection and you do not make an offer there. You go away, submit an application through whichever online platform that agency uses, and wait. Decisions frequently come within a day or two in a busy market, and properties are commonly leased within a week of first being listed.

Two practical consequences. First, have your documents assembled before you attend anything, because the gap between viewing and applying is hours, not days. Second, if you are arriving into a tight market, expect to be unsuccessful a few times before you are successful. That is the normal experience of a good applicant in a competitive market, not a signal that something is wrong with your application.

If you cannot attend in person, most agencies will accept an application from someone who has viewed by video or had a friend attend on their behalf. It is not ideal, and the next section explains why we would still rather you waited.

Once you are in

Your rights as a tenant

Australian tenancy law is reasonably protective, and it is enforced through free, accessible tribunals rather than the courts. The detail varies by state; the principles are broadly shared.

Condition report

Photograph everything on day one

You will be given a condition report to check and return within a few days of moving in. Complete it properly and take date-stamped photographs of every room, every mark and every appliance. This document is what protects your bond at the other end, and it is the single most-skipped step in the process.

Repairs

Urgent and non-urgent are different in law

Every jurisdiction distinguishes urgent repairs. No hot water, a burst pipe, an unsafe electrical fault, from ordinary maintenance, with faster obligations and a route to arrange the work yourself if the owner does not act. Report in writing, always, even after phoning.

Rent increases

Limited in frequency, and notice is required

Rent cannot be raised whenever the owner likes. Every state limits how often an increase can occur during a periodic tenancy and requires written notice, and several have tightened those rules recently. Your state authority publishes the current position.

Entry

The owner cannot turn up

Inspections, repairs and viewings all require notice, in a form and period set by your state. Routine inspections happen. Commonly a couple of times a year, and are entirely normal rather than a sign of suspicion.

Ending a tenancy

Notice periods run both ways

Fixed-term leases are the norm, typically six or twelve months, after which most roll into a periodic tenancy. Breaking a lease early is possible but usually carries a cost. Ask what it would be before you sign, particularly if your role has a probationary period.

Disputes

Free tribunals, and they are used

Every state has a tenancy tribunal that is accessible, low-cost and used routinely by ordinary tenants. You do not need a lawyer. Most disputes about bond are resolved this way, and going there is not an escalation to be embarrassed about.

Our advice

We would not sign a lease before you land

This is the one piece of housing advice we give everyone, and the one people most often want to ignore because uncertainty is uncomfortable.

A twelve-month lease signed from photographs commits you to a suburb you have never stood in. Photographs do not carry the sound of the road at seven in the morning, which way the living room faces in February, whether the walk to the station is pleasant or grim, or how the commute to your actual hospital feels on a wet Monday. Those are the things that decide whether you like where you live, and none of them survive a listing.

What we suggest instead: book four to six weeks of temporary accommodation near where you will be working, arrive, and spend the first fortnight looking properly while you settle into the job. It feels less organised. It consistently produces better decisions, and it costs less than breaking a lease.

In our experience

Every clinician we have placed who regretted their first home had signed it remotely

Not one of them chose badly through carelessness. They chose from the information available, which was photographs and a map, and the information available was not enough.

The families who settle fastest are usually the ones who arrived with somewhere to sleep for a month and no lease, which sounds like the riskier plan and is not. If your employer offers temporary accommodation as part of the package, take it, and if they have not mentioned it, it is a reasonable thing to ask for.

Protect yourself

Scams, and how to spot them

Overseas applicants are targeted specifically, because the scam depends on you being unable to visit the property. The pattern is nearly always the same.

Treat any of these as a stop sign
  • A request for bond or rent before a lease is signed. No legitimate agent asks for money to hold a property you have not been granted.
  • An agent who will not do a live video walkthrough. If they cannot show you the property in real time, on request, there may not be a property.
  • Payment by anything other than bank transfer to a business account, gift cards, cryptocurrency, a personal account, a money transfer service. Never.
  • A bond that is not being lodged with the state authority. Ask which authority and request the lodgement confirmation. A real agent produces it without hesitation.
  • Pressure and a deadline. “Three other applicants are waiting, I need the deposit today” is the core of the scam. Real competition exists; real agents do not take deposits to end it.
  • A price noticeably below the market for the suburb, especially with photographs that appear elsewhere online. Reverse-image search the listing photos.
  • An agency you cannot verify. Licensed agents are searchable on your state authority’s register. Check the licence, not the website.

If something feels wrong, send it to us before you send anyone money. We would much rather read a listing for you than help afterwards, and it takes us a few minutes.

Common questions

The things people ask us first

Can I rent with no Australian rental history?

Yes, and most arriving clinicians do. Remember that agents rank applications rather than accepting or rejecting them, so the aim is to remove every reason to rank you lower: a complete pack, your employment contract, references translated where needed, proof of funds, and a three-sentence covering note. A signed healthcare contract is a genuine strength.

How much bond will I need?

Bond is set as a number of weeks’ rent rather than a fixed sum, and it is capped by your state or territory’s law. Four weeks is the common maximum for an unfurnished home. You will usually pay rent in advance on top. Because it is a multiple of rent it moves with the market, so check it against the actual property rather than any published figure.

Who holds my bond?

A government authority in every state and territory, never the agent or the owner. Neither party can release it alone at the end of the tenancy. If an agent asks to hold it themselves, or wants it before a lease is signed, stop and check with the authority in the table above.

Does unfurnished mean completely empty?

Not quite, but it usually means no fridge and no washing machine, which is the part that surprises people. You should expect built-in wardrobes, an oven and cooktop, window coverings and very often air conditioning. Buy whitegoods second-hand for the first year; it is completely normal here.

Should I sign a lease before I arrive?

We would advise against it. Book four to six weeks of temporary accommodation, arrive, view in person and sign then. Every clinician we have placed who regretted their first home had signed it remotely, not through carelessness, but because photographs cannot tell you about the road noise or the commute.

Can I keep a pet?

It depends on the state, and the rules have been changing in tenants’ favour in several of them. Some jurisdictions now require an owner to have a valid reason to refuse. Never hide an animal. Declare it in the application, because a disclosed pet occasionally costs you a property while an undisclosed one can cost you the tenancy. Getting the animal into the country is a separate matter, covered in moving with pets.

Is it better to buy instead?

Not in your first year, in our view, whatever the numbers say. Renting for twelve months teaches you which suburb you actually want, and that knowledge is worth more than a year of capital growth. There may also be foreign-investment approval requirements and additional stamp duty depending on your visa status, worth proper advice rather than a paragraph here.
