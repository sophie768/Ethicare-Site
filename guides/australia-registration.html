The short version

Registration is what lets you practise, and it’s very doable

Reviewed July 2026 · checked against the current published guidance of the relevant Australian national board or accrediting authority

Australia runs one national scheme, so once you are registered it is recognised in every state and territory. For most medical-imaging professions that means Ahpra and the MRPBA, though a couple of roles sit slightly differently.

The one thing to hold onto: registration lets you practise; a visa lets you live and work here. They’re separate, run by different bodies, and getting them in the right order is most of the battle. We’ll come to visas in Stage 07; this stage is about the right to practise.

“Can I work there?” is four questions, and registration is one of them

Ahpra and your National Board decide whether you can practise. Whether you can live and work in Australia at all is decided elsewhere, and each part has its own owner. This page is the first.

  • Registration — the right to practise. This page, then your profession.
  • The visa — the right to live and work here, decided by the Department of Home Affairs, not the board, and for most clinicians sponsored by an employer. A skills assessment for migration is a third, separate thing. Immigration & visas owns all of that.
  • English, health and character — asked by the board and by Home Affairs in slightly different forms. English requirements.
  • Time and money — months rather than weeks, with fees at each stage. How long it takes; what it costs sits in Make the numbers work.

The quickest first pass is Check my pathway: your profession, where you trained and what you already hold, and the route most likely to apply to you.

There’s a second thing worth knowing early: for the professions that use radiation, national registration is separate from a state radiation use licence. Registration is Australia-wide; the licence to operate radiation equipment is issued by each state or territory, and your employer usually helps you arrange it.

The distinction that trips people up

Registration and migration are two separate things

International clinicians routinely confuse these. They sound alike, they’re run by different bodies, and you may need one, the other, or both, depending on your visa.

To practise

Professional registration

Assessed by the relevant board (the MRPBA for radiographers, radiation therapists and nuclear medicine technologists). This is what legally lets you work in your role, whatever your visa says.

To migrate

A skills assessment

Required for certain skilled-migration visas, by a separate assessing authority: ASMIRT for radiographers and radiation therapists, ANZSNM for nuclear medicine. It’s a migration step, not a registration one.

The order

Lead with registration

On an employer-sponsored (482) visa a skills assessment generally isn’t needed just to register. Most candidates lead with registration and sort the skills assessment only if their visa route needs it.

The shape of it

How registration actually unfolds

It isn’t one form and a decision. It’s a sequence, and knowing the order matters more than knowing the detail, because two of these steps depend on other people and can’t be rushed at the end.

1

Work out which pathway is yours

Your profession, where you qualified and which regulator you’re currently registered with together decide whether you’re assessed as substantially equivalent, streamed through a comparable-regulator route, or assessed individually.

2

Request the slow documents first

Certificates of good standing from every regulator you’ve held registration with, and verified copies of your qualifications. These come from other organisations on their timetable, not yours. Start them on day one.

3

Settle the English question

Either evidence an exemption or book a test. Doing this third rather than last is what separates a smooth application from a stalled one.

4

Submit a complete application

Identity, qualifications, registration history, English, criminal-history check and your practice history, all in one properly evidenced pack. Incomplete applications don’t get assessed slowly; they get set aside.

5

Respond quickly to any request

Assessors often come back with a question or ask for one more document. How fast you answer is one of the few levers you control after submitting.

6

Registration, then the state licence

Once you’re registered, the professions that operate radiation equipment arrange a state or territory radiation use licence. Employers deal with this routinely and usually help.

Where we fit: we can’t assess or approve anything, and we’d be wary of anyone who says they can. What we can do is tell you which pathway looks like yours before you spend money, keep the role and the visa moving alongside the registration, and flag a gap while it’s still cheap to fix.

Common ground

What every application needs

Whatever your profession, a clean, complete documentation pack is the single biggest factor within your control on timing. The specifics vary, but you’ll generally be assembling the same core.

Your qualifications

Your qualifying award and full academic transcript, and, where required, evidence of supervised clinical training and recent practice.

Good standing & identity

Certified proof of identity, your registration history, and a current Certificate of Good Standing from each authority you’re registered with.

English language

You’ll meet the board’s English Language Skills Registration Standard, by an approved test or an exemption. Confirm the current requirement before booking anything.

Two things some applicants also meet: a national MRP examination (where your qualification isn’t assessed as substantially equivalent: comparable-regulator applicants are generally exempt), and a state radiation use licence for the professions that operate radiation equipment. Your profession page sets out which apply to you.

Start these now, they depend on other people

  • Certificates of good standing from every regulator you’ve registered with
  • Verified or certified copies of your qualifying award and transcripts
  • Police or criminal-history checks from each country you’ve lived in
  • Certified translations of anything not in English
  • References who can be reached, and who know you’re using them

These you can settle yourself

  • A clear, dated practice history with no unexplained gaps
  • Your continuing professional development records
  • Evidence of recent practice in your profession
  • Proof of identity, certified as the board requires
  • An English test booking, or the evidence for an exemption
The one people underestimate

The English requirement, plainly

Every national board sets an English language skills standard, and it catches out more experienced clinicians than anything else on this page, usually because they assumed years of practising in English would speak for itself.

You may already be exempt

Boards recognise pathways based on where you were educated and where you’ve practised, in English, over a sustained period. If that’s you, gather the evidence rather than booking a test.

If you do sit a test

Several approved tests are accepted, and the minimum scores are set per component, not as an average. One low component can fail an otherwise strong result, so prepare for your weakest, not your strongest.

Results have a shelf life

Test results expire, and registration and immigration don’t always accept the same tests or the same scores. Check what both require before you book, rather than paying to sit twice.

The honest advice: confirm your position on English in your first week of looking into this, not your last. It’s the single item most likely to add months, and the one most easily sorted early.

The question everyone asks

How long it takes, honestly

We will not print a number, because it depends on your pathway and how complete your application is. What we can tell you is what makes it fast and what makes it slow.

1

Faster: a comparable-regulator route

Where your qualification and current registration are recognised as comparable, assessment is more streamlined and an examination generally isn’t required.

2

Faster: one complete submission

Applications that arrive complete get assessed. Applications that arrive missing a document wait, then wait again for the answer to come back.

3

Slower: individual assessment

Where your qualification needs assessing on its own merits, expect more evidence, more questions and, sometimes, an examination.

4

Slower: documents from elsewhere

Good-standing certificates, verified transcripts and overseas police checks all move at another organisation’s pace. This is the most common reason a timeline slips.

5

Slower: a gap in practice

Boards look at recency of practice. A significant break isn’t a barrier, but it usually means more evidence and sometimes a supervised period.

6

Slower: leaving English to the end

If a test turns out to be needed, booking, sitting and waiting for results can add months to an application that was otherwise ready to go.

Sophie’s insight

Registration looks daunting on paper, but it’s really a sequence, and the people who move fastest are the ones who started the slow items early and kept their evidence organised. Common questions

The things people ask us first

Is my registration valid in every state?

Yes. Australia’s registration is national, so once you’re registered (or accredited, for sonographers) it’s recognised in every state and territory. The exception is the radiation use licence, which is issued by each state separately for the professions that operate radiation equipment.

Do I need to sit an exam?

It depends on your profession and how your qualification is assessed. Applicants recognised through a comparable regulator (such as the UK’s HCPC or Ireland’s CORU, for radiographers and radiation therapists) are generally exempt; others may sit the national MRP examination. Your profession page explains what’s likely for you.

Can I start applying for jobs before I’m registered?

You can, and many employers will support you through the process. That said, Australian employers generally expect your registration to be in place or well underway, so it’s wise to get your application moving first. We help line the role, the registration and the visa up so they don’t hold each other up.

How long does it take?

It varies with the completeness of your application, document verification and your circumstances. The single biggest lever is a complete, well-evidenced submission, and starting the slowest items (certificates of good standing, document verification) early.

Do I need a skills assessment as well?

Only if your visa route requires one. On an employer-sponsored route it generally isn’t needed just to register. For points-tested skilled visas it is, and it’s done by a separate authority: ASMIRT for radiographers and radiation therapists, ANZSNM for nuclear medicine. Two different processes, often confused.

What if I’ve had a break from practice?

It’s common and it’s manageable. Boards apply a recency of practice standard, so a significant break usually means more evidence about what you’ve been doing, and occasionally a period of supervised practice. Tell us early and we’ll be straight with you about what it’s likely to mean.

Will my overseas CPD count?

Generally yes, and it’s worth having your records in order. Keep evidence of what you’ve completed and when. Once registered you’ll meet Australian CPD requirements going forward, which most clinicians find familiar rather than onerous.

What does it cost?

There’s an application fee and then annual renewal, plus the costs around it: document verification, certificates of good standing, any English test, and a state radiation licence where one applies. None of it is unaffordable, but it adds up, so budget for the whole set rather than the headline fee.

Do you charge me for any of this?

No. Our support is free to you, employers pay us. The boards, assessment authorities and test providers charge their own fees, and we’ll tell you which are coming before they arrive.
