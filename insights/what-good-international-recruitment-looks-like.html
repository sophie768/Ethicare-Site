International recruitment

What good international recruitment actually looks like

Sophie CareemFounder, Ethicare Resourcing · former NHS change leader
7 min read

It’s far more than a job offer and a visa. Grounded in internationally recognised ethical-recruitment principles, here’s what "good" actually involves.

Ask most people what international recruitment involves and they’ll describe the visible bits: a job advert, some interviews, a visa, a flight. In my experience that’s perhaps a quarter of the work, and rarely the part that determines whether it succeeds.

Having spent years inside the NHS, I saw how seriously good international recruitment is taken there: the ethical codes, the pastoral care, the structured onboarding. The NHS does this genuinely well, and it shaped how I think about it. Bodies like the World Health Organization and national health departments publish ethical-recruitment codes for the same reasons. Doing it well isn’t complicated so much as thorough, and the principles travel wherever you’re recruiting to.

It starts with ethics, not availability

Good international recruitment begins before a single candidate is contacted: with where you’re recruiting from. The UK’s Code of Practice for International Recruitment and the WHO Health Workforce Support and Safeguards List exist to make sure recruiting into one country doesn’t strip another of clinicians it can’t spare. Ethical sourcing isn’t a compliance box: it’s the foundation everything else stands on.

Preparation beats speed

The temptation, under vacancy pressure, is to move fast. But the moves that last are the well-prepared ones. That means being honest with candidates about how the system works, what registration really involves, and what the first few months will feel like, not just selling the destination. A clinician who arrives with accurate expectations settles far more readily than one who arrives to surprises.

Pastoral care is not optional

Pastoral support is the piece most often underestimated. Someone moving countries isn’t just changing jobs; they’re rebuilding an entire life. Housing, bank accounts, schools, a support network, the small administrative indignities of being new to a country. All of it affects whether they can actually do the job they came for.

A successful international hire isn’t someone who arrives. It’s someone who’s still thriving three years later: clinically confident, settled, and part of a community.

Integration is a two-way effort

Good programmes prepare the receiving team as much as the arriving clinician: a named contact, a genuine induction, awareness of the adjustment involved, and a culture that welcomes rather than merely absorbs. Retention is built here, in the first weeks, far more than in the recruitment campaign.

How we think about it

Everything we do is built around the whole journey, not the transaction: ethical sourcing, honest preparation, hands-on relocation and family support, and staying in touch long after arrival. It’s why the clinicians we’ve placed have stayed, and it’s the standard the NHS frameworks describe, applied to a move to New Zealand or Australia.

Final thoughts

Good international recruitment is quiet, unglamorous and mostly invisible: it lives in the preparation and the support, not the placement. But it’s the difference between a clinician who leaves within a year and one who builds a life and a career that lasts. The frameworks already tell us what good looks like. The work is simply choosing to do all of it, every time.
