A reality check

Moving with children and teenagers

What childhood can look like here, and what you would be leaving

More room. More outdoors. Shorter distances. A childhood that can feel a little less hurried — and, at the same time, further from the people who know them best.

Answer first

“A better life for your children” is too simple a claim for us to make

What we can describe is the shape of what you would be moving towards, and the shape of what you would be moving away from. Both are real.

Towards

More room. More outdoors. Shorter distances.

A childhood that can feel a little less hurried, and a week where finishing work still leaves some day in it.

Away from

The people who know them best

A new school, new friendships, and a completely new sense of home — built from scratch, at whatever age they are when you go.

That trade is the question most parents are really asking, and it is not one a salary comparison can answer. Two things decide how well it goes more than the country does: where each child sits in their qualification years, and whether they had any real part in the decision.

The practical detail on schooling — enrolment zones, year levels, fees on a visa — lives in the country guides: education in New Zealand and education in Australia. This page is about deciding, not about enrolling.

Section 01 · What a week can look like

Children’s lives can spill outside more

After school might mean kicking a ball around, swimming, heading to the beach, riding bikes or being in someone’s garden — rather than every activity feeling like a planned expedition.

Weekends can feel less dominated by shopping and indoor entertainment, and more by “shall we go somewhere?” The distinction parents describe is not that there is more to do, but that less of it needs organising in advance.

Unpolished

Muddy, barefoot, outside in imperfect weather

School camps, swimming, sport, bare feet in summer. There is something practical about childhood here, and less pressure to make it look curated.

Club sport

Saturday morning is the social structure

Rugby, football, netball, cricket, athletics, surf lifesaving and nippers all run at junior level through local clubs. It is where a good deal of the settling happens — for parents on the sideline as much as for children.

Water

Swimming is treated as a life skill

With this much coastline, learning to swim is closer to a requirement than a hobby, and holiday programmes lean heavily on the water — kayaking, sailing, surf skills.

Section 02 · The part that changes most

Space and time, and the geography of an ordinary day

For parents coming from London or another large city, this is the change that tends to matter most — and it does not show up in a salary comparison at all.

In many places, the geography of everyday life becomes much simpler. Home, school, the hospital, the sports club and the supermarket can all sit within a relatively small radius. You might finish work and still have some day left.

It is not everywhere. Auckland traffic is not idyllic, and neither is a long Sydney or Melbourne commute. But in Palmerston North, Christchurch, parts of Wellington, or the regions in either country, the distances are shorter — and that changes family life in ways a pay figure cannot describe.

Worth checking against a specific address

This is the one claim on the page you can verify before you commit to anything. Take a real suburb in the town you are considering, and measure the actual distances: to the hospital, to two schools, to a supermarket, to the nearest beach or park. A ten-minute commute is a different life from a fifty-minute one, whichever country it is in. The destination guides set out what each place is like at that level of detail.

Section 03 · The calendar inverts

Summer falls at Christmas, and the year has a different shape

School finishes, Christmas arrives, and families head into the long summer break — all in the same fortnight.

Christmas can mean barbecues, beaches, camping and children outside until late, rather than darkness at four in the afternoon. The school year then runs from late January or early February through to December, which is also why a family arriving from a September-start system often lands mid-year.

Parents who grew up with a northern Christmas describe this as the change that took longest to stop feeling strange, and the one their children adapted to immediately.

Section 04 · Two different offers

Australia has a different feel again

If New Zealand can feel smaller, greener and a little gentler, Australia can feel bigger, brighter and more energetic.

New Zealand

More room to breathe

Small communities, nature at the doorstep, and a slightly slower rhythm. Bare feet, school camps, and weather you go out in anyway.

Australia

More life lived outside

Houses opening onto outdoor areas, children in and out of the water, school sport, eating outside, and Christmas Day in the heat.

In places such as Perth, Brisbane, the Gold Coast and the coastal regional cities, outdoor life can be woven into the ordinary week rather than saved for holidays. Pools, beaches, parks, barbecues, sports grounds and eating outside are simply part of the environment, and the sporting culture is strong. Parents describe the rhythm as: school, home, swimmers on, down to the beach or the pool. Weekends might be junior sport in the morning, breakfast somewhere afterwards, then friends, beach or a barbecue.

Not only a stereotype, and not a screen-free utopia either

The Australian Institute of Family Studies describes parks, beaches and other natural spaces as important settings for children’s outdoor and nature play. The same body also cautions that Australian children are spending increasing amounts of time indoors and on devices. Both things are true at once, which is why we would not write “move to Australia and get your children off their iPads.”

Primary source: Australian Institute of Family Studies (aifs.gov.au). Reviewed September 2026.

Australia gives you far more choice of what kind of family life you want

Melbourne can feel sophisticated and metropolitan. Brisbane is subtropical and increasingly big-city. Perth offers city infrastructure alongside an enormous coastline. Adelaide can feel manageable and family-oriented. Then there are regional coastal places where life becomes considerably smaller and slower.

Bundaberg is a completely different proposition from Melbourne. Moving a child to Bundaberg is not selling them “Australia” — it is a regional Queensland childhood.

Which is why it is worth deciding on a place rather than a country. The Australian destination guides are written at that level.

What families find difficult

  • The heat can be intense, and sun protection becomes part of daily life rather than a holiday habit.
  • Distances are enormous. Depending on where you live, a car is essential rather than optional.
  • Housing in some major cities is very expensive, and it is the item most likely to change which suburb, or which city, works for you.

What could childhood look like here? Not a permanent holiday. School runs still happen, parents still work late, and children still want their screens. What changes is that family life has the opportunity to move outside — into the garden, the pool, the park, the sports club and, for many families, the beach. The biggest change may not be what your children do. It may be where they do it.

Section 05 · The practical constraint

The exam-year problem

Qualification systems do not line up between countries, and mid-course transfers are where families most often say they wish they had known earlier.

New Zealand

NCEA, awarded across the senior years

Senior secondary students work towards the National Certificate of Educational Achievement, gathered through internal and external assessment across the final school years rather than in one terminal exam series. Some schools also offer Cambridge International or the International Baccalaureate.

Because credits accumulate, the timing of a move within a senior year matters. Schools can advise on what transfers and what has to be repeated.

Primary source: NZQA (nzqa.govt.nz). Reviewed September 2026.

Australia

A senior certificate set by each state

The senior secondary certificate is a state matter — the HSC in New South Wales, the VCE in Victoria, the QCE in Queensland, and their equivalents elsewhere — each with its own subject rules and assessment pattern, feeding a nationally comparable university rank.

Moving between Australian states mid-certificate is its own question, not only moving into Australia from abroad. If you have a choice of state, and a child in their final two years, that is worth factoring in.

Primary source: each state curriculum authority, and the Australian Curriculum (australiancurriculum.edu.au). Reviewed September 2026.

The pattern we see

Families who move before the senior qualification years, or after them, describe far fewer academic problems than families who move partway through. Where the timing cannot be chosen, the schools themselves are the ones to ask — before you accept a role, not after. Ask what your child’s current course maps onto, what is credited, and what would need repeating. Two schools in the same city can answer differently.

We are not qualified to advise on curriculum equivalence, and neither is any recruiter who tells you otherwise. What we can do is help you ask the right question of the right school early enough for the answer to change your plan.

Section 06 · What changes at this age

Why this age is different from moving a seven-year-old

A younger child’s world is largely the one you provide. By thirteen or fourteen, a good deal of it is theirs.

  • Friendships are the structure, not an accessory. Younger children make friends through proximity; teenagers have chosen theirs, often over years, and rebuilding that takes a term or two rather than a fortnight.
  • Qualifications are already in progress. A move mid-course has consequences a primary-school move simply does not have — the section above is about exactly that.
  • Sport, music and clubs are commitments with a history. A place in a squad or an orchestra took time to earn, and it does not transfer.
  • Identity is under construction. Being the new person with an accent lands differently at fifteen than at eight.
  • They can see the trade-off you are making. Younger children take a move on trust; teenagers form a view about whether it is fair, and they will tell you.

None of that makes it a bad idea. It means the move is a negotiation with another person rather than a piece of logistics — and the outdoor, sporty week described earlier is often what a teenager gains in exchange for what they give up.

Section 07 · Honestly

The other side

Not to talk you out of it. These are the things families tell us afterwards they would have handled differently.

  • The move was announced rather than discussed. The most common regret we hear, and the one with the longest tail.
  • The timing was set by the job alone. A start date that suits an employer can land in the middle of a qualification year.
  • Term dates were not checked. The southern-hemisphere school year starts in late January or early February. Arriving mid-year means joining classes already underway.
  • The house was chosen before the school. In New Zealand, enrolment zones mean the school your child can attend is often decided by where you sign a tenancy.
  • Nobody planned for the friendships left behind. Time zones make staying in touch harder than families expect; the UK is eleven to thirteen hours behind New Zealand depending on the season.
  • One parent wanted it much more than the rest of the household. Teenagers notice, and it shapes how they read every difficulty that follows.

The families who found it manageable were surprised by very little of it.

Section 08 · What we hear works

What families say helped

Reported rather than prescribed — these come up repeatedly when we ask families a year on what made the difference.

Before you decide

Give them something real to look at

Not a brochure. The actual school website, the actual suburb on a map, the club that plays their sport, what a Saturday looks like. Families say the decision landed better when their teenager could picture a specific life rather than a country.

Before you fly

Line up one thing they already do

A club, a squad, a teacher, a class. Arriving with one fixed point in the week comes up more often than anything else as the thing that shortened the settling.

On arrival

Let the first fortnight be small

Most families find the early days are best kept quiet — sleep, food, the local shops — rather than filled with sightseeing that everyone is too tired to enjoy.

The first term

Expect a dip, and know roughly when

Families describe a harder stretch somewhere around the second or third month, once the novelty has gone and the friendships have not yet arrived. Knowing it is a stage rather than a verdict is what people say helped.

Keeping the old life

Make the contact practical

A standing weekly call at a time that works in both hemispheres tends to survive; ad-hoc messaging across a twelve-hour gap tends not to. Several families budget for one trip home in the first two years and say it changed how the move felt.

Sport and the outdoors

It does a lot of the work

Club sport runs at every age and level in both countries, and it is where a good deal of the settling happens — for teenagers and for the parents standing on the sideline. There is more on this in moving with your family.

Section 09 · At your own kitchen table

The conversation before you decide

Questions worth talking through together, early — while the answers can still change the plan.

  • Where is each child in their qualification years, and what would a move cost them academically?
  • Is there a start date that would sit between stages rather than inside one?
  • What would each of them be giving up that cannot be replaced — a squad place, a grandparent nearby, a course?
  • What would have to be true for them to think this was a good idea?
  • What is the plan if it is not working after a year? Saying it out loud changes the conversation.
  • Who else are you bringing, or leaving — a parent, a sibling at university, a pet?

These are not questions we answer for you. They are the ones we would rather you had answered before an offer needs a decision in five days.

Section 10 · The harder case

If they don’t want to come

Some teenagers will say no, and mean it. That is an answer rather than an obstacle to be managed.

Some families go anyway, and the young person settles. Some delay by a year or two until an exam series is finished. Some decide it is not the right moment and revisit it later — the roles will still exist, and so will the registration you have already earned. Some arrange for an older teenager to stay on with family to finish a course.

We are not going to tell you which of those is right for your household. What we will say is that we have seen all four work, and that the one which tends not to work is proceeding as though the objection was not made.

Worth knowing about registration and visas

Registration, once granted, generally does not evaporate if you delay a move by a year, though maintenance requirements and fees continue to apply. Visa conditions and dependant age limits are a different matter and do have deadlines. If timing is the question in your household, ask us before you assume either way — the sequencing is the part we do.

Common questions

What families ask us

Is there a worst age to move a child?

There is no single answer, but the constraint families run into most is the qualification years rather than age itself. A move partway through a senior certificate carries academic consequences that a move a year either side does not. Beyond that, what people report matters more is how much say the young person had.

Will my child have to repeat a year?

Sometimes, and it depends on the system, the school and where your child is in their course. The school year runs late January to December in both countries, so a family arriving from a September-start system often lands mid-year, and placement is decided school by school. Ask the specific schools you are considering, before you accept a role.

Can we choose the school before we choose the house?

In New Zealand this is the order we would suggest thinking in: many popular schools manage demand with an enrolment zone, so where you rent can decide where your child can go. The detail is in education in New Zealand. In Australia, government schools also use catchments, set state by state.

Do we pay school fees on a temporary visa?

In New Zealand, children of parents on longer work visas can usually attend state schools as domestic students; schools still ask for donations and activity costs. In Australia it depends on the state and often on your occupation — the current position by state is in school fees on a visa.

What about university afterwards?

Domestic or international tuition status depends on your visa and residency at the time your child enrols, not on when you arrived, and the thresholds differ between the two countries. It is a question worth asking early if you have a fifteen or sixteen-year-old, because it can be worth a great deal of money.

Is one country easier than the other for teenagers?

Not in a way we would generalise. Australia has more large cities and more choice of school and pathway; New Zealand is smaller, and a sporty, outdoor school culture comes up often when families describe why their children settled. The bigger variable is the specific town and school, not the country.

Want to talk it through before you decide anything?

We would rather have this conversation with you early than have you make a decision on a five-day deadline. No pressure, no obligation, and no fee to you at any stage.
