In her words

“I never planned to leave the NHS.”

Megan Barnes was a diagnostic radiographer in Manchester, and she was happy. She wasn’t looking for a job in Australia. This is what happened anyway.

Megan Barnes · Diagnostic radiographerManchester → Sunshine Coast, QueenslandIn her own words, published with her consent

I never planned to leave the NHS.

I was working as a radiographer in Manchester and I was happy. I had good friends, I enjoyed my job and I’d built a life there. I certainly wasn’t sitting at home searching for jobs in Australia.

I’d always dreamed about living somewhere warmer and by the sea, though.

It was one of those things I’d talked about for years without ever really believing I’d do it. I’d see pictures of Australia and think imagine living somewhere like that, and then get on with normal life.

The message

Then one day I received a message on LinkedIn.

They were recruiting radiographers for Australia and asked whether I’d ever considered making the move.

My first reaction was probably, not really.

And then I thought about it.

I had. I just hadn’t thought of it as something that could realistically happen to me.

We had an initial conversation and there was no pressure to apply for anything. We talked about my experience, the sort of work I enjoyed and, importantly for me, what I wanted my life outside work to look like.

When I said I’d always wanted to live somewhere warmer and near the sea, the Sunshine Coast came up.

That was probably the moment the whole thing started.

The rabbit hole

I went home and Googled it. Big mistake.

Suddenly I’m looking at beaches, suburbs and apartments and checking how far different hospitals are from the coast. I’m looking at the temperature in Queensland while it’s raining in Manchester.

Open tabs, that first night

  • Beaches
  • Suburbs
  • Apartments to rent
  • How far is the hospital from the coast
  • Queensland weather in February
  • Cost of living
  • Radiography jobs Australia

The idea had gone from wouldn’t that be nice? to hang on, could I do this?

The questions

And there were a million questions.

Every one of these is a question, and every one has an answer.

These are the questions Megan asked in her first fortnight, in the order she asked them. This is also, more or less, what Plan Ethicare is: the same list, sequenced, with the answers attached.

  1. What would I earn?Salary & pay
  2. Could I get registered?Pathway checker
  3. Where would I live?Queensland suburbs
  4. How much money would I need to move?Cost calculator
  5. Would I need a car?Driving & licences
  6. What was radiography like in Australia?The profession
  7. Would I know anyone?Community
  8. And what if I fly to the other side of the world and hate it?Living there

That was probably the biggest thing Ethicare helped me with in the beginning. Nobody was telling me I should move. I was just able to explore it. And the more I found out, the more possible it started to feel.

Eventually there was an interview, then an offer, and suddenly this thing I’d casually talked about for years wasn’t hypothetical anymore. I was moving to the Sunshine Coast.

The hard part

And that’s when the excitement was joined by absolute terror.

There were forms, registration, paperwork, flights, packing up my life in Manchester, saying goodbye to people and the slightly horrifying amount of money you spend when you first arrive somewhere.

There were definitely moments when I wondered what on earth I was doing.

The first few weeks weren’t an Instagram reel either. I was jet-lagged, living somewhere temporary, trying to work out supermarkets and phone contracts and where I was going to live while also starting a new job.

I missed home.

The turn

Then things started becoming normal.

I found somewhere to live. I made friends at work. I stopped using Google Maps every time I got in the car. I found my coffee place and started having favourite beaches rather than just knowing their names.

And I remember one afternoon particularly clearly.

I’d finished work, had my swimmers in the car and decided to go down to the beach.

About half an hour later I was in the water.

And I remember thinking: this is it.

This was the completely ordinary life I’d imagined when I was sitting in Manchester.

Not giving up being a radiographer. Not escaping the NHS. Not reinventing myself. Just doing the job I love somewhere that gives me the kind of life I’d always imagined outside it.
Told straight

Australia isn’t perfect.

Work can still be busy. I miss people at home enormously and sometimes being this far away is really hard. There are things about Manchester and the NHS that I’ll probably always miss.

But I don’t regret coming.

The funny thing is, if that LinkedIn message hadn’t arrived, I’m not sure I would ever have done it. I might still have been saying “one day I’d love to live somewhere warmer by the sea.”

Now I do.

If you’re where Megan was

You don’t need a role, a decision or a date. Most people start with the same two questions she did — could I register, and what would it cost — and both have answers you can get to yourself, today, without speaking to anyone.
