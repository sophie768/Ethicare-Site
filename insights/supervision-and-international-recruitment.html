International recruitment

Supervision should not be the reason international recruitment fails

How better planning, collaboration and support can turn a regulatory requirement into one of the strongest predictors of long-term success.

Sophie CareemFounder, Ethicare Resourcing · former NHS change leader
16 min read · in four parts

International recruitment has become an increasingly important part of workforce planning across New Zealand. For many professions, internationally trained healthcare professionals are no longer an occasional addition to the workforce: they are an essential part of maintaining safe, sustainable services.

Alongside professional registration, immigration and relocation, supervision is one of the most significant stages of that journey. Yet despite its importance, supervision is often discussed only in terms of capacity.

Can we supervise another clinician? Do we have enough experienced staff? How quickly can they become fully independent?

These are understandable questions, particularly for departments already managing workforce shortages and increasing clinical demand. But they risk overlooking something more important. Supervision is not simply a regulatory requirement to complete before someone becomes another member of the rota.

The conversation should not be about whether supervision is necessary. It should be about how we make it work better.

Done well, supervision is an investment in patient safety, professional confidence and long-term retention. Done poorly, it can become one of the reasons talented clinicians choose not to remain. That better conversation is one that works for patients, for supervisors, for departments and for the internationally trained professionals we are asking to build their futures here.

Why supervision matters

Internationally trained healthcare professionals often arrive with many years of experience, advanced technical skills and leadership responsibilities gained in respected healthcare systems around the world. Many have worked in busy tertiary hospitals, major trauma centres or internationally accredited organisations. Some have supervised others themselves.

The purpose of supervision is not to question that experience. Instead, it recognises that every healthcare system has its own professional standards, clinical governance arrangements and ways of working. Even highly experienced clinicians need time to understand:

  • local clinical pathways and documentation
  • legislation and professional standards
  • communication and escalation processes
  • multidisciplinary team working
  • cultural safety and the principles of Te Tiriti o Waitangi
  • local technology, equipment and information systems
  • organisational policies and expectations

This period of supported transition helps clinicians adapt safely and confidently while protecting patients and supporting professional development. When framed positively, supervision demonstrates that New Zealand values safe practice and invests in helping internationally trained professionals succeed.

The challenge is not supervision itself. The challenge is ensuring that it is adequately planned, resourced and supported.

The challenge facing employers

Many services are attempting to solve two workforce problems simultaneously. They are experiencing vacancies that are increasingly difficult to fill, while also needing experienced clinicians to supervise newly recruited staff.

This creates an understandable tension. The clinicians most able to provide supervision are often the same people already carrying significant clinical responsibilities. Without protected time or clear organisational support, supervision can begin to feel like an additional burden rather than an investment.

The consequences are familiar. Managers hesitate before recruiting internationally because they are concerned about supervision capacity. Existing supervisors become overstretched. Candidates experience inconsistent support. Registration takes longer than expected. Everyone becomes frustrated.

None of these outcomes are inevitable. They are usually symptoms of a system that has not been designed around supervision as an integral part of workforce planning. Instead, supervision is often treated as something to organise after the appointment has been made, and by that point, many opportunities to set both the candidate and the department up for success have already been missed.

When supervision is unplanned

An added burden on stretched staff

When supervision is planned

Protected, resourced supervisor time

A strong reason to stay

For many internationally trained healthcare professionals, the decision to relocate is one of the biggest they will ever make.

It often involves resigning from a secure position, selling a home, moving a family, leaving elderly parents, withdrawing children from school and committing to a healthcare system they have only experienced through interviews and online research. By the time they arrive, they are not simply starting a new job.

They are rebuilding their lives.

Against that backdrop, the supervision period becomes about far more than professional registration. It shapes their first impression of their employer, their profession and, ultimately, their new home.

Experience should be recognised

One of the greatest concerns candidates express before relocating is whether their previous experience will be recognised. Many have worked for years in highly respected healthcare systems. Some have held senior positions, supervised junior colleagues or managed complex clinical services.

When supervision is poorly explained, it can unintentionally feel as though those years of experience no longer count. This is rarely the intention. Regulatory supervision exists to support transition into a different healthcare environment, not to question professional competence, and how that message is communicated matters enormously.

When employers acknowledge a clinician’s previous achievements while explaining the purpose of supervision, candidates are far more likely to see it as a period of professional orientation rather than professional judgement. Respect should remain at the centre of every supervision relationship.

Uncertainty creates anxiety

Candidates often arrive with very little understanding of what supervision will actually involve. Questions commonly include:

  • How long will supervision last?
  • What happens if I make a mistake?
  • Who decides when I can practise independently?
  • Will I always be supervised?
  • What happens if my supervisor changes?
  • What evidence do I need to provide?
  • Will this affect my salary or future career progression?

When these questions remain unanswered, uncertainty quickly becomes anxiety. Some candidates begin to worry that they have made the wrong decision. Others lose confidence despite performing well clinically. Clear expectations from the outset can remove much of this. Candidates should understand:

  • what supervision looks like in practice
  • who their supervisor will be
  • how progress will be reviewed
  • what success looks like
  • how concerns will be managed
  • what happens once supervision has been completed

Transparency builds confidence.

First impressions last

The first few weeks in a new country are often overwhelming. Candidates are adapting to unfamiliar clinical systems while simultaneously opening bank accounts, finding housing, enrolling children in school, learning new roads and establishing a new social network. Professional confidence and personal wellbeing become closely linked.

If supervision feels supportive, structured and encouraging, candidates often describe feeling welcomed into both the organisation and the profession. If it feels inconsistent or disorganised, the opposite can happen. Small things matter:

  • being introduced properly to colleagues
  • having protected time with a supervisor
  • receiving constructive feedback
  • knowing who to ask for help
  • feeling included in team conversations rather than simply observing them

These experiences often shape whether candidates begin to feel that they belong.

Supervision is not just the supervisor’s responsibility

There is a natural tendency to view supervision as a relationship between two individuals. In reality, successful supervision depends on the wider team.

Internationally trained professionals frequently describe feeling uncomfortable when they believe they are creating additional work for colleagues. Some hesitate to ask questions because everyone else appears busy. Others become reluctant to seek clarification because they worry about being perceived as inexperienced.

Creating an environment where questions are encouraged benefits everyone. The responsibility for successful integration should not sit solely with one supervisor. Departments that embrace international recruitment successfully often create a culture where the whole team contributes to helping new colleagues settle into practice.

Belonging begins long before sign-off

Professional registration may eventually come to an end. Belonging does not. Many internationally trained professionals can clearly remember the people who welcomed them, answered their questions, invited them for coffee or simply checked how they were settling into life outside work.

Those moments are rarely written into supervision frameworks, yet they often have the greatest influence on retention. Healthcare organisations invest significant time and resources recruiting internationally trained professionals; helping those individuals feel like valued members of the team is one of the most important investments they can make after arrival.

When supervision is delivered with structure, respect and genuine support, it becomes the foundation on which long-term careers (and long-term workforce stability) are built.

There is no single model of supervision that will work for every profession, organisation or regulatory body.

Different professions have different requirements. Different services have different workforce pressures. Some departments have experienced supervisors and established processes; others may be supporting an internationally trained clinician for the first time. However, the organisations that consistently achieve good outcomes often share one thing in common.

They plan supervision long before the candidate arrives.

Successful supervision should never feel like something added to an already busy workload. It should be an integral part of workforce planning: just as important as recruitment, onboarding and professional development.

A simple framework: plan supervision before the vacancy is advertised

Before recruitment

  • Do we have capacity to support someone?
  • Who is best placed to supervise?
  • How much protected time is needed?
  • What support will the supervisor need?
  • What did we learn last time?

Before arrival

  • Explain how supervision will work
  • Name the supervisor early
  • Agree a meeting and review rhythm
  • Describe the path to independence
  • Prepare the existing team

During supervision

  • Regular, structured conversations
  • Timely, specific, balanced feedback
  • Reflect on complex cases together
  • Build on existing strengths
  • Recognise and celebrate progress

Before recruitment

The most successful supervision programmes begin before the vacancy is even advertised. Managers should consider questions such as:

  • Do we have the capacity to support an internationally trained professional?
  • Who is best placed to supervise?
  • How much protected time will be required?
  • What additional support might the supervisor need?
  • What experience have we gained from previous internationally recruited staff?

These conversations allow organisations to recruit with confidence rather than uncertainty. If supervision capacity has not been considered until after an appointment has been made, avoidable delays often follow.

Before arrival

Once a candidate has accepted an offer, preparation becomes essential. The supervision period should never be a surprise. Candidates should understand how supervision will work, who their supervisor will be, how often they will meet, how progress will be measured, what support will be available, and what the anticipated pathway to independent practice looks like.

This early communication helps reduce anxiety before relocation and demonstrates that the organisation has invested time in planning their arrival. Equally important is preparing the existing team. International recruitment should never feel like something that simply happens to a department. Staff should understand:

  • why the appointment has been made
  • the experience the new colleague brings
  • the purpose of supervision
  • how they can contribute positively to the individual’s transition

When teams understand the process, they are far more likely to support it.

During supervision

Perhaps the biggest misconception is that supervision is simply observation. In reality, effective supervision is built on regular conversations, constructive feedback and shared learning. Good supervision should provide opportunities to:

  • discuss clinical decision-making
  • reflect on complex cases
  • explore differences between healthcare systems
  • identify learning needs
  • celebrate progress
  • address concerns early

Feedback should be timely, specific and balanced. Candidates need to understand not only where improvements are required, but also what they are doing well. Confidence grows when progress is recognised.

Recognising previous experience

One of the most important qualities of an effective supervisor is the ability to distinguish between competence and familiarity. An internationally trained clinician may require support learning local documentation systems or referral pathways while simultaneously bringing years of advanced clinical expertise. Both things can be true.

Supervision should therefore build on existing strengths rather than assuming every clinician begins from the same starting point. Recognising previous experience creates mutual respect and encourages candidates to contribute their own knowledge and perspectives to the wider team. International recruitment should not simply introduce new staff. It should also introduce new ideas.

Consistency matters

Candidates often describe inconsistent supervision as one of the most challenging aspects of the registration process. Receiving different advice from different people can create uncertainty and reduce confidence. Where possible, organisations should aim for:

  • clear objectives
  • agreed expectations
  • regular review meetings
  • documented feedback
  • consistent communication between supervisors

This creates a more transparent process for everyone involved.

Supporting the supervisors

It is easy to focus entirely on the candidate. However, supervisors themselves also require support. Many undertake supervision alongside full clinical workloads, leadership responsibilities and service pressures. Without organisational recognition, supervision can begin to feel like invisible work. Healthcare organisations should consider:

  • protected supervision time
  • manageable caseloads
  • opportunities for supervisors to share experiences
  • access to guidance and training
  • recognition of supervision within professional development

Supporting supervisors ultimately improves the experience for candidates.

Measuring success differently

Traditionally, supervision is viewed as complete once regulatory requirements have been met. Perhaps success should be measured more broadly. Questions organisations might ask include:

  • Did the candidate feel supported?
  • Did they become a confident member of the team?
  • Did the supervision process develop the supervisor as well?
  • What lessons were learned for future international recruitment?
  • Has the candidate remained with the organisation?
  • Would they recommend the organisation to other internationally trained professionals?

These questions move supervision beyond compliance and towards continuous improvement.

From supervision to professional belonging

The most successful supervision programmes have something in common: they stop feeling like supervision. Instead, they evolve into mentorship. As confidence grows, conversations become less about demonstrating competence and more about professional development, leadership, education and future career aspirations.

This is where organisations begin to see the true value of investing in internationally trained professionals. The goal should never simply be to help someone complete a supervision period. It should be to help them build a long and rewarding career within the organisation and the New Zealand healthcare system.

If New Zealand is to continue attracting internationally trained healthcare professionals, it may be time to broaden the conversation about supervision.

For many employers, supervision has become something that each organisation is expected to solve independently. Every service develops its own processes. Every department identifies its own supervisors. Every organisation creates its own induction. Every manager works through similar challenges.

This approach has served New Zealand reasonably well, but as international recruitment becomes an increasingly important component of workforce planning, there is an opportunity to think differently. Perhaps supervision should not simply be viewed as an organisational responsibility. Perhaps it should also become a regional workforce strategy.

A shared challenge deserves a shared solution

The challenges surrounding supervision are remarkably consistent across professions and regions. Managers talk about:

  • limited supervision capacity
  • competing clinical priorities
  • experienced staff carrying multiple responsibilities
  • uncertainty around supervision requirements
  • variation in practice between organisations
  • candidates waiting longer than necessary to reach independent practice

None of these challenges are unique to one employer. They are system-wide challenges. And when organisations face similar barriers, collaboration often gets further than working in isolation.

Learning from other sectors

Across healthcare, regional collaboration is already common. Hospitals work together to deliver specialist services. Training programmes are shared. Clinical networks exchange expertise. Smaller services regularly rely on larger centres for advice, education and peer support.

There is no reason the same collaborative thinking could not be explored in relation to supporting internationally trained professionals. The aim would not be to remove organisational responsibility. Rather, it would be to share expertise where it already exists.

What could regional collaboration look like?

There is unlikely to be a single solution, but there are several ideas worth exploring.

Shared induction programmes

Many internationally trained professionals receive very similar information during their first weeks: understanding the New Zealand healthcare system, documentation standards, cultural safety, communication, professional expectations and working within multidisciplinary teams. Much of this learning could potentially be delivered collaboratively across organisations, reducing duplication while giving candidates the chance to build networks with other internationally trained professionals.

Communities of practice for supervisors

Supervisors often describe learning through experience. Creating regional supervisor forums would allow professionals to discuss challenges, share good practice, learn from experienced colleagues and develop greater consistency across organisations. Supporting supervisors ultimately strengthens supervision itself.

Cohort recruitment

One observation from organisations that recruit internationally on a regular basis is that candidates often benefit from arriving alongside others. A cohort model allows organisations to deliver structured induction together, share orientation sessions, provide peer support between candidates, reduce duplication and build professional networks from day one. Equally importantly, candidates realise they are not navigating the transition alone, and that sense of shared experience can significantly improve confidence during the first few months.

Shared educational resources

Every organisation currently develops many of the same materials independently. Regional collaboration could include shared resources covering orientation, supervision frameworks, competency documentation, cultural safety, reflective practice and professional development. Rather than replacing local induction, these resources could provide a consistent foundation across organisations.

Preparing teams, not just candidates

One area that receives surprisingly little attention is preparing existing teams. International recruitment changes workplaces, and that change should be acknowledged openly. Without preparation, existing staff may understandably wonder why the organisation is recruiting internationally, whether supervision will increase their workload, and how best to support a colleague who is unfamiliar with local systems.

These are entirely reasonable questions. Creating opportunities to discuss international recruitment before new colleagues arrive helps build understanding and shared ownership. International recruitment should never feel like an additional burden placed on existing staff. It should feel like an investment in strengthening the whole service.

Success should be measured over years, not months

Healthcare organisations often celebrate successful recruitment when a candidate accepts an offer. Others celebrate when registration is completed. Perhaps the more meaningful milestone comes much later: when the clinician is thriving professionally, their family has settled, they have become a valued member of the department, they are supervising others, and they have chosen to build their future in New Zealand. That is the point at which international recruitment delivers its greatest value.

A conversation worth having

There is no suggestion that every profession or every regulator should approach supervision in the same way, nor is there a simple solution to workforce shortages. However, if New Zealand intends to continue competing internationally for skilled healthcare professionals, it is worth asking whether supervision itself could evolve: not by reducing standards, and not by shortening important learning, but by supporting supervisors more effectively, encouraging greater collaboration, and recognising supervision as an investment rather than a cost.

Conclusion

International recruitment is often measured by the number of appointments made.

Perhaps it should instead be measured by the number of professionals who remain.

Supervision sits at the heart of that journey. When it is carefully planned, properly resourced and delivered with respect, it becomes far more than a regulatory requirement. It becomes an introduction to a new healthcare system, a new profession and, for many, a new life.

The challenge facing New Zealand is not whether internationally trained professionals require supervision. The challenge is ensuring that supervision reflects the same values we hope they will experience throughout their careers: collaboration, respect, learning and belonging. Because when supervision is approached in that way, it stops being something to complete. It becomes the foundation on which sustainable international recruitment is built.
