Build your CV

Write it once, properly, and keep it

A clinical CV written for one health system rarely reads well in another, and most people are told this only after they have been turned down. So start here instead. Answer the nine sections, watch the document build itself underneath, and print it when it is done.

These are the nine sections of the Ethicare template — a guide you are welcome to use, not a form we require. It is your CV: use it wherever you are applying, through us or not, this year or in two years.

Nine sections

In the order a reader wants them

Open one section at a time. Leave out anything that genuinely does not apply — though an unexplained gap is usually more noticeable to a reader than a short explanation.

Top of page one, and the only place a reader looks for how to contact you. No photograph, no date of birth, no marital status, no identity number — this builder gives you nowhere to put them on purpose.

The role you want, not necessarily the one you hold now.

It has to work from overseas. A number that only rings locally costs you interviews.

Nationality is not standard CV content, so there is nowhere to put it here. Where an immigration process genuinely needs it, we ask separately.

This belongs on page one, not page two. If you are not sure how to word your stage, the Registration Pathway Checker tells you where you are and what to call it.

Four moves: who you are professionally, what you bring, what makes your experience distinctive, and why this country. Aim for 80 to 130 words — two hundred is the ceiling, not the target.

Active verbs — led, managed, developed. Every claim here should be one you would be happy to be asked about, because you will be.

A service you have helped improve, an unusual clinical environment, specialist experience, leadership responsibility, or something that shaped the clinician you are. Our clients read these side by side, and this is the line that separates two otherwise similar CVs.

Everyone reading it is wondering, and a plain personal reason reads better than a polished one.

Nothing written yet. Aim for 80 to 130 words.

The section that gets scanned, and where shortlisting actually happens. Group it under headings a reader recognises, then name the procedures — a reader will not assume a skill you have not written down.

One department’s headings, to show what a grouping looks like. Rename them to match how your own service is organised — a reader who sees the same seven headings on seven CVs stops reading them.

Three or four, chosen because they are true of you. Copying the list is the classic mistake — you will be asked for an example of each, so the example is the test of whether it belongs on the page.

Most recent first. This is where you learned the skills you have just listed, so do not repeat them — use the space for what was specific to that job, and put the scale in.

Roles that started before 2010 need only a title, an organisation and a date range. A gap gets a line too — unexplained gaps invite worse assumptions than the truth.

Highest qualification first. Give each one as it was awarded — do not translate it into a local equivalent yourself, because that is the regulator’s job and guessing it wrong reads badly.

Evidence that you are current, so recency matters more than length. Clinical training first. Mandatory training counts, but a list that is only fire safety and manual handling tells a reader you have done nothing else.

One line each. A department running the same equipment as you will notice this before anything else on the page.
