Registering with the MRTB

For internationally qualified medical imaging technologists, sonographers, nuclear medicine technologists and radiation therapists: how registration in New Zealand works, in the order you will meet it — with the checklist built into the page so nothing you tick goes out of date.

At a glance

Registration comes first

In New Zealand you generally need to be registered — or clearly registrable — before an employer progresses your application. Plan everything else on top of it.

You register into a scope

Medical imaging, sonography, nuclear medicine or radiation therapy. Mammography and MRI sit inside the imaging scope. Your qualification decides the scope, not your job title.

Two questions, one decision

Are you suitably qualified, and are you currently competent and safe? Every document you supply answers one of those two.

Supervision is normal

Many international registrants start with a supervised period. It is a structured settling-in, not a doubt about your ability.

Section 01

Understanding the Board

The Medical Radiation Technologists Board registers and oversees medical radiation professionals in New Zealand under the Health Practitioners Competence Assurance Act. Its job is to protect the public by making sure everyone practising in a regulated imaging or radiation therapy role is qualified, competent and fit to do so.

The titles and clinical roles are legally protected. You cannot practise, or call yourself a registered practitioner, without being registered with the Board and holding a current practising certificate. That is not a formality — it is the mechanism that tells patients, employers and colleagues you have met a national standard.

Scopes of practice

The Board registers you into a defined scope — diagnostic imaging, sonography, nuclear medicine or radiation therapy — and you must practise within it. Your qualification and experience are assessed against the scope you apply for, which is why matching your application to the right scope from the start matters so much.

Registration and the Annual Practising Certificate

Registration confirms you have met the standard. The Annual Practising Certificate (APC) is what permits you to actually work, year to year. Think of registration as the licence and the APC as keeping it current: you must hold a valid APC while working, renewed annually alongside a declaration about your health, conduct and continuing professional development. The Board can ask for evidence of CPD, so keep records from day one.

Section 02

Which professions register with the MRTB

Your scope is determined by your qualification and the work you are trained and experienced to do — not by your job title, which varies from country to country.

Scope

Medical Imaging Technologist

Diagnostic images across general X-ray, CT, MRI, fluoroscopy and theatre imaging. Technical precision with direct patient care, often across many clinical settings.

Scope

Sonographer

Ultrasound across obstetric, abdominal, vascular, musculoskeletal and other applications. Highly autonomous and interpretive, needing strong clinical judgement.

Scope

Nuclear Medicine Technologist

Functional imaging with radiopharmaceuticals — PET and SPECT — blending imaging with the safe handling of radioactive materials.

Scope

Radiation Therapist

Planning and delivering radiation treatment, most often within oncology services, across a whole course of treatment.

Within the imaging scope

Mammography and MRI

Neither is a separate registration. You register as a Medical Imaging Technologist and practise into mammography or MRI with the appropriate training. Note that MRI, like sonography, has no fast-tracked country pathway — see Section 03.

Not sure which scope applies to you?

Ask before you apply, not after. Assessment against the wrong scope costs months. We can help you map your background to the right scope so the application is judged against the correct standard from the start.

Section 03

Registration pathways and how you are assessed

At its heart, registration asks two questions: are you suitably qualified, and are you competent and safe to practise within the scope you are applying for? The pathway your application takes is simply the route the Board uses to answer those two questions for your background.

Qualification assessment. The Board first considers whether your qualification is comparable to the New Zealand standard for the scope. Some countries and institutions are well recognised; others need closer examination of course content and clinical hours.

Competence assessment. Beyond the paper, the Board wants assurance you are currently competent — recent practice, employment references and your clinical history.

Requests for more information. Common, and not a red flag: a clearer document, an extra reference, clarification of scope. Responding promptly and completely is the single biggest thing within your control.

Possible outcomes

Being registered with a supervised period is normal, not a setback

Many internationally qualified applicants are registered with an initial period of supervised practice. It is a structured way of supporting you while you settle into the New Zealand system, equipment and protocols — not a sign the Board doubts your ability. Whether it applies, and for how long, depends on how closely your training and regulatory background match New Zealand’s. Applicants from very comparable systems — the UK, Ireland, Australia, Canada — often face little or none; others more commonly complete one, and in some cases an examination also forms part of the pathway.

Registration granted — you meet the standard and proceed to your APC.
Registration with supervised practice — you can work, under the supervision of an experienced colleague for an initial period.
Conditions or further requirements — pending additional evidence, assessment or an examination.
Further assessment required — the Board asks for more before deciding.

If a supervised period applies, factor it in early: you will need an employer and a supervisor who can support it. Many employers are used to this and have arrangements ready.

The online examination pathway

For some applicants the assessment leads to an online examination. The trigger is equivalence: it is offered where the Board concludes your qualification is not straightforwardly equivalent to the New Zealand standard for your scope. It is a recognised, formal pathway — not a penalty.

This is where scope matters. For imaging technologists, radiation therapists and nuclear medicine technologists, applicants from closely aligned systems (Australia in particular) often have a more streamlined route. Sonographers and MRI technologists do not: even Australian-qualified applicants in those two scopes go through the full overseas-trained assessment, and there is no fast-tracked country pathway for sonography at all. A full equivalence assessment — and therefore the examination — is more likely if that is your scope. Anticipate it rather than be caught out.

The exam is sat online under remote proctoring: a supervisor verifies your identity (two forms of ID, one a government-issued photo ID, plus your authorisation code) and oversees the session. The fee is paid in advance and forfeited if you are refused access on the day for failing to establish your identity — have your documents in order.

Section 04

Eligibility requirements

The Board’s requirements look like a long checklist. They are easier to satisfy once you understand why each one exists — so that is how they are explained here.

Qualifications
Required: a recognised qualification appropriate to the scope you are applying for.
Why: your qualification is the foundation of safe practice. The New Zealand qualifications that lead to registration are bachelor’s degrees (Level 7 on the New Zealand Qualifications Framework), so that is broadly the benchmark. The Board does not simply count years: it assesses whether your qualification is equivalent to, or as satisfactory as, an approved New Zealand qualification for your scope — content, clinical hours and outcomes, not level alone. If you are not certain how yours compares, check before you apply.
Recent practice
Required: evidence that you have practised recently and to a sufficient extent.
Why: skills fade when unused. If you have had a significant break, flag it early so it is addressed rather than discovered mid-assessment.
English language
Required: evidence of English language ability — Section 07.
Why: clear communication is a patient-safety issue: referrals, explaining procedures, precise communication with patients and colleagues.
Fitness to practise
Required: a declaration, and sometimes evidence, about your physical and mental health as it relates to safe practice.
Why: this is about practising safely, not about excluding people with health conditions. The vast majority of declarations raise no issues.
Professional conduct
Required: disclosure of any disciplinary history, conditions or investigations.
Why: the Board must protect patients. Full, honest disclosure — even of matters you think minor or resolved — is always right. Non-disclosure is treated far more seriously than the underlying matter usually is.
Certificates of Good Standing
Required: one from every regulator you have been registered with, sent directly to the MRTB.
Why: they are how one regulator tells another you have no unresolved concerns. They have a limited validity window, which makes their timing one of the trickier parts of an application — Section 05.
Read every requirement as a question.

The Board is asking whether you are safe to practise. Frame each document as the answer to that question and your application becomes far more persuasive.

Section 05

Preparing your application

A strong application is rarely about doing anything clever. It is about assembling clean, complete, correctly certified documents and presenting them well.

How it looks matters more than people expect. The Board is forming a picture of you as a professional, and a folder of crooked, half-legible photocopies quietly undermines that picture even when every document is genuine. Clean colour scans, pages straight and square, nothing cut off, consistent file names, certification and translations done properly the first time. Treat it like a portfolio you would be proud to hand over.

Passport

Why
Confirms identity and nationality; underpins your visa and right to work.
Where
Your existing passport — valid, and not expiring mid-process.
Mistake
A passport close to expiry, or a scan that crops the machine-readable strip.
Tip
Scan the full photo page flat, in colour, all four edges visible.

Qualification certificates

Why
They prove you hold the qualification registration is based on.
Where
Your awarding university or college; request certified copies early if originals are missing.
Mistake
Only the final certificate, when the Board also wants the detail of what you studied.
Tip
Keep certificates and transcripts together — they are assessed as a pair.

Academic transcripts

Why
Course content, clinical hours and modules — often what lets the Board map your training to a scope.
Where
Your institution’s registry; many issue official transcripts directly.
Mistake
Missing pages, or transcripts that do not show clinical placement hours.
Tip
If the transcript is light on clinical detail, add a course outline or syllabus.

Employment history and CV

Why
Demonstrates recent practice and the breadth of your experience.
Where
Compiled by you — accurate and complete.
Mistake
Unexplained gaps, or dates that do not match the rest of the application.
Tip
Account for every period. A one-line explanation of a break beats an unexplained hole. Build your CV is written for this.

Certificates of Good Standing

Why
Confirm good standing with every regulator you have registered with.
Where
Requested from each regulator and sent directly to the MRTB — not via you.
Mistake
Requested too early so they expire before assessment; or a regulator from years ago forgotten.
Tip
Limited validity, so timing is everything. Line them up to arrive when the application is ready, not months before.

Employment references

Why
They verify competence and recent practice — so important they have Section 06 to themselves.
Where
Current and recent supervisors or managers.
Mistake
A referee chosen for seniority rather than knowledge of your clinical work.
Tip
Brief your referees properly — read Section 06 before approaching anyone.

Name change documents

Why
If your name differs across documents, the Board needs to link them to one person.
Where
Marriage certificate, deed poll or equivalent.
Mistake
A name difference between a degree certificate and a passport, overlooked.
Tip
Check every document carries a consistent name, and include the bridging document if not.

Certified copies and translations

Why
Certification confirms a true copy; translation makes non-English documents assessable.
Where
An approved person such as a notary; a recognised, independent translator.
Mistake
Wrong or incomplete certification wording; translations not independently certified.
Tip
Confirm exactly who may certify and the wording required before you spend money. Getting it wrong means doing it twice.
One folder, one file per document.

When everything is gathered, certified and in date at the same time, the application itself takes a fraction of the effort.

Section 06

References are the part most within your control

Your qualification proves what you were trained to do. Your references prove what you actually do, now, in clinical practice. The Board relies on them to confirm you practise safely and competently. A vague reference leaves a gap the Board may have to fill by asking for more — which means delay.

Choosing the right referee

The instinct is to ask the most senior person available. Resist it. The best referee has directly supervised or worked alongside your clinical practice recently and can speak to it in specific terms. A clinical lead who knows your work beats a department head who barely does.

A poor reference
“I have known Sam for some years. Sam is hard-working and a pleasure to work with. I am happy to recommend Sam.”

No clinical detail, no dates, no scope, no basis for assessing competence.

A strong reference
“As Imaging Lead, I have directly supervised Sam for three years. Sam independently performs general X-ray, CT and MRI to a high standard, including complex and emergency cases, and consistently demonstrates safe, patient-centred practice.”

Specific, dated, scope-clear, credible.

What a reference that never prompts a follow-up contains

  • The referee’s full name, job title and professional relationship to you
  • How long, and in what capacity, they have known your work
  • The specific scope and modalities you work within — settings and responsibilities
  • An assessment of your clinical competence, safety and professionalism, with examples
  • Confirmation of the dates and the hours or extent of the practice described
  • Official letterhead, signature, date and verifiable contact details

Brief your referee

Most weak references are weak because the referee was not told what was needed. Explain that this is for professional registration in New Zealand, share which scope you are applying for, and tell them what the Board values: clinical detail, dates, scope and a clear assessment of competence. Ask early — a referee on leave when you need them is a common, avoidable delay.

We can review references before submission against what the Board looks for and flag anything likely to prompt a follow-up. The content always remains your referee’s own honest, independent assessment.

Section 07

English language requirements

This exists for one reason: patient safety. Clear communication in a clinical setting is non-negotiable, so the Board needs objective assurance.

Test

IELTS (Academic)

Widely accepted. The Board sets minimums overall and in each component — listening, reading, writing, speaking. A strong overall score does not help if one component falls short, so prepare evenly.

Test

OET

The healthcare-specific alternative many clinicians prefer because its tasks are set in medical contexts. Minimum grades apply in each of the four components.

Alternative evidence

English-medium training and practice

In some circumstances the Board accepts alternative evidence in place of a test — for example an English-medium education and career, or employer references. Eligibility is specific and decided by the Board; check before assuming you qualify.

Results expire.

Typically around two years from the test date. Sitting the test early and then taking many months to assemble the rest of the application is a frequent reason for paying to sit it again. Time it to remain valid through to assessment. And even fluent speakers stumble on technique: sit a timed practice test, find your weakest component, book early — centre availability can be limited.

Which test should I choose?

Either, where accepted. Many clinicians find OET more natural because the content is medical. Pick the one that plays to your strengths.

Can I combine scores from two sittings?

The Board’s rules on combining sittings are specific — check current guidance before relying on it.

What if I narrowly miss one component?

You generally need to re-sit. Build a little time buffer into your plan in case.

Section 08

Completing the application

Once your documents are gathered, certified and in date, the application itself is careful assembly.

  1. 1

    Create your application on the Board’s portal

    Work through each section methodically. Read every question carefully — accuracy here saves queries later.

  2. 2

    Upload your documents

    Clear, high-quality colour scans, named so the Board can see at once what each file is.

  3. 3

    Pay the fees

    Assessment and registration fees as they apply. Keep your payment confirmation.

  4. 4

    Submit

    Review everything once more against the requirements. A ten-minute final check is the cheapest delay-insurance there is.

  5. 5

    Respond to any requests

    Expect an acknowledgement, possibly requests for further information, then the decision. Check email, including spam, and the portal. A request that sits unanswered for three weeks adds three weeks.

Put yourself in the assessor’s shoes.

Could a stranger open this application and find everything they need without emailing you? If yes, you have prepared it well.

Understanding the fees

Depending on your circumstances the Board’s charges can include an application or qualification-assessment fee, the registration fee, your Annual Practising Certificate and — if it applies — an examination fee. On top sit third-party costs: criminal history checks, translation, certification. The examination fee is paid in advance and forfeited if you are refused access on the day, so do not pay it until you are ready and your ID is in order.

Amounts are deliberately not printed here. The Board publishes its prescribed fees in a notice on its website and revises them; confirm the current figures on the Board’s own schedule before you budget or pay. Where an Ethicare package applies to your placement, APC costs are reimbursed — ask us what your package includes before you rely on it.

Where people get stuck

Almost every avoidable delay traces back to one of these

Incomplete references

Warm but vague, with no clinical detail, dates or scope. The single most common cause of a follow-up request.

Poor-quality scans

Cropped, blurry or greyscale. If the assessor cannot read it, it does not count.

Missing transcripts

A certificate without the transcript that shows course content and clinical hours.

Incorrect certification

Copies certified by the wrong person or with the wrong wording — redone from scratch.

Expired Good Standing

Requested too early, lapsing before assessment, reissued.

Inconsistent names

Passport, certificates and references that differ, with no bridging document.

Gaps in employment history

Unexplained periods that raise questions about recent practice.

The fix for all of these

Prepare everything together, check it as a set, and have it read by someone else before you submit. Most of these mistakes are invisible to the person making them.

Section 11

The questions we are asked most

Can I apply for registration before finding a job?

Yes — and in most cases you should. Because registration usually comes before employment, starting early puts you in a far stronger position when roles arise.

Do I need registration before interviewing?

Not always for an initial conversation, but employers want confidence that you are registrable. Being registered, or clearly on track to be, makes you a much stronger candidate.

How long does registration usually take?

It varies considerably with your background, your scope and — crucially — how complete your application is. A clean, well-prepared application is the single biggest thing you can do to keep the timeline short.

Can I submit additional documents later?

Yes. If the Board requests further information, you provide it during assessment. Responding promptly and fully is what keeps things moving.

Can I work while my application is being assessed?

You cannot practise in a regulated role until you are registered and hold a current APC. Plan your move around that fact rather than assuming you can begin and sort registration out afterwards.

Will I have to work under supervision?

Quite possibly — and that is normal. Those who trained in very comparable systems (the UK, Ireland, Australia, Canada) often face little or none; others commonly complete a period. It is not a judgement on your ability, and the Board decides case by case.

Will I need to sit an examination?

Some applicants do. The Board offers an online examination where it assesses your qualification as not straightforwardly equivalent to the New Zealand standard. It is more likely for sonographers and MRI technologists, because those scopes have no fast-tracked country pathway — even Australian-qualified applicants go through full overseas assessment. If it applies, it is a defined step rather than an obstacle.

What if my referee has left the organisation?

Common and manageable. A referee can still provide a reference after moving on, provided they knew your clinical work; another suitable supervisor can also step in. Genuine knowledge of your work is what matters, not their current employer.

How much does registration cost?

Typically an assessment fee, the registration fee, your APC, an examination fee if one applies, plus third-party costs such as criminal checks and translations. Figures are not quoted here because the Board updates them — always check its current fees notice.

Can Ethicare review my application?

Yes. We review your documents and references before submission against what the Board looks for and flag likely issues while they are easy to fix. We support and guide; the registration decision always rests with the Board.
