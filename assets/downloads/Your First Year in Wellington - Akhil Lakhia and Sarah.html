A note to the three of you

You have moved a family, not just taken a job

Kia ora, and welcome home — because that is what Wellington is now. You are here, Akhil has started work, and the part everyone congratulates you on is behind you. This short book is about the quieter part that comes next.

Akhil, nearly everything sent to you so far has been about the job. Registration, contract, roster, first day. All of that felt like the hard part, and it was not really the part that matters most. Whether you are all still happy here in two years will come down to things no contract covers — whether Lakhia has found her own feet, whether Sarah has a friend down the road, and whether the house is warm in July.

Lakhia, your side of this is harder, and it tends to get less attention. Moving country when the job belongs to someone else is its own kind of hard. The families who settle best are the ones where the partner starts building something of their own early, instead of waiting to feel ready. There is a page in here that is only yours.

And Sarah, at three, is at a lovely age for this — old enough to enjoy a beach and a cable car, and young enough that she has not had to leave friends behind. Do not be surprised when she sounds like a New Zealander long before you do.

Wellington is a small city that acts like a big one, and six months in you will start bumping into people you know. That is usually when it begins to feel like home.

We have kept this short on purpose. A few things worth knowing, told honestly, and a page of numbers at the back for the fridge.

Your first year02

Where to begin

The house matters more here than anywhere you have lived

More than the suburb, and more than the commute. How warm and dry the building is will shape your first winter more than anything else you choose.

Wellington · timber houses built for a climate that has changed its mind

One · the cold house

Mild winters, cold houses

Wellington winters are mild — eight to twelve degrees, and almost never any snow. Even so, you will probably feel colder here than you did at home.

The reason is the houses rather than the weather. Many were built for a mild climate by people who assumed you would simply put on a jumper — single glazing, thin insulation, no central heating, and air moving about under the floor. You are not really fighting the cold outside; you are fighting an indoor temperature that follows it.

Damp is the other half of it. Wellington is humid and windy, washing will not dry outside for weeks at a time, and damp air makes a cool room feel properly cold. Mould around the windows is usually about airflow rather than cleaning.

What actually works

  1. 01

    Heat one room properly, not the house

    This is what people here do, and it is much the cheaper way — heat pump on in the living room, doors shut, electric blankets in the bedrooms. Trying to heat a whole New Zealand house to a British setting is how people end up with frightening power bills.

  2. 02

    Buy a dehumidifier in your first month

    It is not a luxury here. It makes a cool room feel warm, dries the washing and keeps the mould away, and every New Zealander will tell you the same thing, usually with feeling.

  3. 03

    Open the windows every morning, even in winter

    Ten minutes is enough, and although it sounds wrong it really does work — the air you let out is wetter than the air you let in, and dry air is much cheaper to heat.

  4. 04

    Curtains closed before dusk

    Thermal-lined if you can manage it. Most of your heat goes straight out through the glass, so the difference is immediate, and it costs nothing.

Sarah’s room specifically

Aim for dry and draught-free rather than hot, and have a look at the inside of her window each morning — condensation there is your early warning. It is damp bedrooms rather than cold ones that cause the winter coughs filling Akhil’s department every July.

Two · where to live

Choosing a suburb around one commute and one school zone

Wellington Hospital is in Newtown, just south of the centre, and almost the whole city is within half an hour of it. So the commute really should not decide this for you. Two other things should.

Zoning is the whole game

Popular state schools have enrolment zones, and living inside the zone is what guarantees a place. Places for children outside the zone go to a ballot. That means the school decision and the housing decision are really one decision. The good news is that Sarah is three, so you have time to get this right rather than hope to be lucky, and moving house now is far easier than moving in the year she starts.

Three websites will settle it in an evening. educationcounts.govt.nz lists every school. ero.govt.nz has the Education Review Office report on each one, which is the honest read. And each school’s own website carries its enrolment zone map. Do check the exact street before you commit to anywhere, because zone boundaries often run down the middle of a road.

The shortlist, with the trade named

Island BayOur pick for a young family. A real village, a beach at the bottom, strong community, ten minutes to the hospital. Trades: it takes the southerly first, and it is priced for being popular.
Karori & NorthlandThe default family suburbs — schools, shops, bush at the end of the road, fifteen minutes in. Trades: inland, and one slow road in and out at peak.
Newtown & BerhamporeOn the hospital’s doorstep and genuinely walkable, which changes what a night shift feels like. Trades: busier, older houses, less garden.
Miramar & SeatounFlatter and sunnier than most of the city, beaches close. Excellent with small children. Trades: twenty minutes in, aircraft noise in parts.
Khandallah & NgaioLeafy, quiet, on the train line. Trades: hilly enough that the walk from car to door matters, and the longest of these commutes.
BrooklynClose in and often overlooked. Ten minutes to the hospital, a village of its own, and the wind turbine and the town belt on the doorstep. Trades: hilly, and the higher streets catch the wind properly.
Lower Hutt & PetoneFlat, noticeably better value, the river and Days Bay nearby. Trades: twenty-five to thirty-five minutes, and a different city with its own council.

Three · Sarah

Her week, and how she meets people

Almost all of this is free, and most of it is where Lakhia will meet the people who end up making Wellington feel like home. That is not a side effect of it; it is rather the point.

The free drop-ins

Library rhymetimeFree, weekly, at branches across the city. Half an hour of songs. The easiest possible first outing, and nobody minds a three-year-old who will not join in.
Plunket playgroupsRun by the national well-child service. Free or nearly, and full of parents in exactly your position. Ring 0800 184 803 and they will point you at your nearest.
PlaycentreParent-led and co-operative — you stay and help rather than drop off. More commitment, and much faster friendships.
Community centre morningsMost suburbs run a weekly under-fives session. Ask at the local centre or check the council’s events listing.

Swimming, which is treated as a life skill

Council pools and private schools run learn-to-swim in blocks that follow the school terms, and the younger age groups fill quickly — put her name down now and move her later if the pool does not suit. The ones worth knowing: Freyberg on Oriental Parade, the big Wellington Regional Aquatic Centre at Kilbirnie with a warm learners’ pool, Karori Pool, and Keith Spry at Johnsonville.

On beaches, be clear early: the harbour ones — Oriental Bay, Days Bay, Petone — are calm and fine for paddling. The south coast is beautiful and not for swimming with a three-year-old.

The days out she will ask for again

Wellington Zoo on Daniell Street, close enough to the hospital that Akhil could walk over on a day off — small, well run, doable before she runs out of legs. Zealandia in Karori for native bush and birds. Staglands up the Akatarawa Valley for a proper hands-on farm day. Te Papa on Cable Street, free, with a children’s level, for every wet Sunday. Annual passes for the first two pay for themselves in about three visits.

Four · Lakhia

Your side of this matters as much

Akhil arrived to a department, a rota and forty new colleagues. You arrived to a house, a three-year-old and a city you did not know. That difference is normal and it does not last, but it is much better said out loud now than quietly resented in month four.

Start now, not once you feel settled

Wellington is a government and professional-services city, so the job market here is different from Auckland’s — public sector, policy, tech, education, health administration, and a good small-business scene. It is also small enough that people will happily have a coffee with a stranger, which is the most useful thing about looking for work here. LinkedIn helps, but a conversation helps more.

Two things to check early

  1. 01

    What your visa allows

    What a partner is allowed to do depends on the visa Akhil holds. Check what you are entitled to before you start applying, and do ask us if the wording is unclear, because it usually is.

  2. 02

    Whether your qualification needs assessing

    If your field is regulated here, having your qualification assessed takes time, so it is worth starting before you need the answer. If it is not regulated, a New Zealand referee will count for more than a New Zealand qualification.

What consistently helps

Something in the diary every week that is yours alone, rather than Sarah’s or the household’s. The playgroups and her kindergarten, which are the quickest way into a neighbourhood. And saying yes to invitations for the first six months even when you would rather stay in — the ones that turn into friendships are never the ones you expect.

If the first few months are harder than you expected, it does not mean the move was wrong. It is simply the ordinary shape of it, and it does lift.

Five · Sunday

Church, and finding the right one

If church is part of your life, this is the most important page here. It is also, in our experience, the fastest way a family goes from knowing nobody to being known.

Wellington has established congregations across the Christian traditions — Catholic parishes in most suburbs and the cathedral in the city, Anglican, Presbyterian, Baptist, Methodist, Pentecostal and independent churches, and a number of vibrant migrant-led congregations.

For Kerala Christian families the tradition you belong to is likely to be represented. Syro-Malabar Catholic, Malankara and Syrian Orthodox, and Mar Thoma communities are all active in New Zealand, with Malayalam liturgy in the main centres — sometimes a dedicated church, sometimes a regular Mass hosted by a local parish. Wellington’s arrangement changes with the priest and the roster, so rather than print something that may already be wrong, tell us your tradition and we will find the current time, the place, and a name to ask for.

What we will do

Put you in touch with someone in the congregation rather than send you an address. A person expecting you on your first Sunday is worth ten websites, and it means Lakhia and Sarah walk in knowing a face.

We will also find out what is there for Sarah — Sunday school, children’s liturgy, a family service time — because that decides whether the whole morning works or only part of it.

Beyond Sunday, churches here run the practical scaffolding of a new life: playgroups, meals, lifts, someone who knows a good landlord and someone whose cousin is a plumber. If your first months include one deliberate act of putting down roots, this is the one we would choose.

And for completeness, because colleagues will ask: Wellington also has a Hindu temple, mosques, a synagogue, and Buddhist and Sikh communities, all well established.

Six · te reo Māori

The words you will hear on the ward

Te reo Māori is an official language, and it runs through ordinary working life here — emails, handovers, school newsletters. Nobody expects you to be fluent, but people do notice when you try.

Saying it right

The vowels never change, which makes it easier than it looks: a as in far, e as in egg, i as in tree, o as in awe, u as in food. A macron lengthens the vowel and it matters. Wh is usually an f, and ng is the sound in the middle of “singer”. Say every vowel — nothing is silent.

Kia orakee-ah OR-ah — hello, thanks, cheers, agreed. The most useful word in the country.
MōrenaMAW-reh-nah — good morning. Akhil will hear this before anything else.
WhānauFAH-no — family in the broad sense. Used constantly in healthcare: “is whānau with her?”
Tamarikitah-MAH-ree-kee — children. Sarah’s centre will use this more than the English word.
Hauorahealth and wellbeing in a whole-person sense, rather than the absence of disease.
Karakiaa blessing or prayer. Meetings and handovers often open with one. You are not expected to lead it, only to stand quietly.
Kaumātuarespected elders. Their presence with a patient is significant and worth accommodating.
Te Tiriti o Waitangithe Treaty of Waitangi, 1840 — it underpins how health services here are designed. Worth reading properly rather than picking up second-hand.

The best thing you can do is learn to say colleagues’ and patients’ names correctly, and ask when you are unsure. Asking is welcomed here. Guessing repeatedly is not.

Seven · the honest one

Missing home, and what actually helps

This is the page we would most like you to read. Not because anything is wrong, but because this is coming, it is completely ordinary, and knowing that in advance takes most of the sting out of it.

The first few weeks are busy enough to carry you along. It is usually around the third or fourth month, once the admin is done and the novelty has worn off, that the distance starts to feel real — a birthday you are not at, or an appointment back home you cannot drive anyone to. It tends to arrive sideways, on an ordinary Tuesday.

What consistently helps

  1. 01

    Book the next reunion now

    A date in the diary changes everything, whether it is your trip back or their trip out. Having something to count towards is worth more than any amount of reassurance.

  2. 02

    A fixed weekly call, not an open invitation

    Same time, same day, or the time difference will quietly eat into it. Sarah will grow up knowing exactly when she talks to her grandparents.

  3. 03

    Keep two rituals exactly as they were

    A Sunday meal, a festival, or a particular way of doing birthdays. Children especially need some things to stay the same, and keeping them costs nothing.

When family come to you

Visitors from most countries can come as tourists without applying in advance, but check the current requirement before anyone books — there is usually an online authority to obtain, and it is quick. Longer, repeated stays for parents sit in their own visa category with particular conditions, and the rules there change more often than most. If that matters to you, ask us early and we will point you at the current settings.

Two things to tell them twice: the seasons are upside down, so Christmas is summer and July is cold indoors; and biosecurity is enforced with instant fines, so no food, no seeds, and no soil on boots.

Eight · keep this page

Numbers to save, and where to go

Worth putting the first four into both your phones this week. The rest are the places you will find yourself driving to.

Save these now

111 — emergencyAmbulance, fire or police. Free from any phone, including a locked one. The operator asks which service you want.
Healthline — 0800 611 116Free health advice from a nurse, 24 hours, any age. This is the number for a 2am “is this serious?”
PlunketLine — 0800 933 922Free, 24/7, answered by a Plunket nurse. Under-fives and parenting. You do not need to be registered to call.
Plunket appointments — 0800 184 803Well Child checks, playgroups and general enquiries, 8.30–4.30 weekdays. No referral needed.

For Sarah, ring PlunketLine. For the three of you, Healthline. Both are free, both are answered by nurses, and both will tell you honestly whether something needs a GP, an after-hours clinic, or nothing at all. Using them is not making a fuss — it is what they are for.
