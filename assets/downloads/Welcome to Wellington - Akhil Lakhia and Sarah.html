A book for your first year here

A note to the three of you

You have moved a family, not taken a job

Kia ora, and welcome home — because that is what Wellington is now. You are here, Akhil has started, and the part everyone congratulates you on is done. What follows is the longer, quieter part, and this is a book for that.

Akhil, almost everything written for you so far has been about the job — registration, contract, roster, first day. That was the easiest part of this move, even when it did not feel like it, and it is not the part that decides whether the three of you are still happy here in two years.

Lakhia, your side of this is harder and gets less attention. Moving countries when the job is someone else’s is a particular kind of hard, and the people who settle best are usually the ones whose partner found their own footing early rather than waiting for it to happen. There is a page in here that is only about that.

And Sarah — three is a very good age for this. Old enough to enjoy a beach and a cable car, young enough that friendships have not yet become the thing she would grieve. Do not be surprised if she picks up a New Zealand accent long before either of you do.

Wellington is a small city that behaves like a big one. Six months in, you will keep running into people you know, and that is when it starts to feel like home.

How to use this book

It is not a manual and it is not urgent. Keep it somewhere you will come back to — a shelf, a drawer, the kitchen — and open it when a question comes up rather than reading it through. The first weeks page is the one for the fridge. Everything about Sarah is on one page, everything about Lakhia’s work is on another, and the honest page near the back is the one we would most like you to read before you arrive rather than after.

The city itself

A harbour, a ring of hills, and a twenty-minute life

Everything is close. That single fact is the reason people who love Wellington love it — the commute, the coffee, the coast and the hills are all inside twenty minutes.

Oriental Bay · a ten-minute walk from the city centre

Where to live

Suburbs, for a family with a small child

Wellington Hospital sits in Newtown, just south of the city centre. That one fact should shape your shortlist — and the good news is that almost everywhere in this city is inside half an hour of it.

Getting to Wellington Hospital

Walk or ten minutes’ drive — Newtown, Berhampore, Mount Cook, Melrose. Some staff walk in, which is a genuine luxury on a night shift.

Fifteen to twenty minutes — Island Bay, Karori, Northland, Hataitai, Miramar, Kilbirnie, the city centre.

Twenty-five to thirty-five minutes — Khandallah and the northern suburbs, Johnsonville, Lower Hutt and Petone. All perfectly normal commutes here, and cheaper housing.

There is no such thing as a long commute in Wellington by the standards of the city you are leaving. Choose the suburb that suits the three of you and the drive will follow.

Karori, Northland, WiltonGreen, established, family-heavy. Close to Zealandia and the botanic gardens, a straightforward drive in, and good sections for children. A sensible first choice.

Buying or committing to a long lease in month one is the most common regret we hear.

Choosing well

What to check before you sign

Wellington houses vary more than the photographs suggest. Three questions answer most of it.

Three things to check at every viewing

The hill. Wellington is steep. A house up ninety steps is charming until you are carrying a sleeping three-year-old and the shopping.

The sun. A south-facing Wellington house in winter is a genuinely different proposition from a north-facing one. Ask which way it faces and visit in the afternoon.

The warmth. Ask directly what it costs to heat, and what insulation and heating it has under the Healthy Homes standards. This matters more here than the rent does.

Do not sign anything from overseas. We will help you book four to six weeks somewhere temporary so you can look properly, drive the commute at eight in the morning, and see a suburb on a wet Tuesday rather than in a photograph.

Welcome to Wellington06

For Sarah

Three years old, in a new country

The practical list is short. The reassuring part is that Wellington is unusually good for small children, and that at three Sarah will adapt faster than either of you.

Early childhood education

Sarah is not school age — that starts at five here — so what you are looking for is early childhood education: a kindergarten, an education and care centre, or a home-based service. At three, Sarah is eligible for up to 20 funded hours a week, capped at six hours a day. You may still pay for hours beyond that and for optional extras, so ask each centre what a normal week actually costs.

The important thing: good centres have waiting lists measured in months, not weeks. Start enquiring now and put her name down at two or three places near the suburbs you are considering. This is the single most time-sensitive item in this pack.

Worth knowing

Plunket is the free national well-child service and a genuinely useful first point of contact — health checks, and playgroups where Lakhia will meet other parents, which matters as much as the checks do.

Enrol Sarah with a general practice in your first week, before anyone is unwell. Enrolled patients pay less and get continuity; children’s dental care is publicly provided.

Crèches, kindergartens and the difference

The words are used loosely, so here is the actual distinction. Education and care centres (what you may call a nursery or crèche) take children from babies to five and open long days — the usual choice when both parents work. Kindergartens generally take children from two or three, often in shorter sessions, and are strongly play-based. Home-based care places Sarah with a qualified educator in a small group at their home, which suits some children far better. Kōhanga reo are full immersion in te reo Māori and welcome families who want that.

At three, Sarah is eligible for government-funded hours, and all four options can offer them. Visit two or three before deciding — you will know within ten minutes of walking in.

For Sarah

Swimming, and where to do it

Swimming is treated as a life skill here rather than an activity, and in a city surrounded by water that makes sense. Book early — learn-to-swim terms fill up.

How lessons work

Council pools and private swim schools both run learn-to-swim programmes, taught in blocks that follow the school terms. Places for the younger age groups go quickly, so put Sarah’s name down in your first fortnight rather than waiting for a settled routine — you can always move her once you know which pool suits. Most pools also run parent-and-child sessions from a much younger age, which are as much about confidence in the water as technique.

Pools worth knowing

Freyberg PoolOriental Parade, on the waterfront. Indoor, central, and the view from the lane is hard to beat. Good for an adult swim while the other one takes Sarah to the beach outside.
Kilbirnie — Wellington Regional Aquatic CentreThe big one. Multiple pools including a warm learners’ pool and a leisure area with slides. If you land in the eastern suburbs this will become a Saturday habit.
Karori PoolLocal, friendly and the obvious choice if you settle in Karori or Northland.
Keith Spry PoolJohnsonville. The northern suburbs’ pool, with learn-to-swim classes and a good warm pool for small children.

The harbour beaches — Oriental Bay, Days Bay, Petone — are calm and fine for paddling with a three-year-old. The south coast is beautiful and genuinely not for swimming with small children: cold, rough, and with currents. Learn the difference early and you will relax.

For Lakhia

Your side of this matters as much

One of the easiest things to overlook in a move like this is the partner’s life. If one of you arrives with a job, colleagues and a ready-made routine while the other has none of those, the first few months can feel very different for each of you.

Start now, not once you feel settled

Wellington is a government and professional-services city, which means the job market is different from Auckland’s — public sector, policy, tech, education, health administration and a lot of small consultancies. It is also small enough that networks matter more than portals. Begin conversations now rather than applications later: professional associations, LinkedIn, and anyone we can introduce you to.

Two things to check early

  1. 01

    Your work rights

    These flow from Akhil’s visa class rather than being a separate thing you apply for. Some visas carry open work rights for a partner and others are more restricted. Confirm which applies before you plan anything, because it changes your options entirely.

  2. 02

    Whether your qualification needs recognising

    If your field is regulated or requires a recognised qualification, find out now what the New Zealand equivalent process is and how long it takes. If it is not regulated, the question is simply how your experience reads to a local employer — and that is a CV problem we can help with.

Ask us for an introduction to someone who did exactly this a year or two ago. It is the single most useful conversation available to you, and we can usually arrange it within a week.

The first few months, honestly

Akhil will have a department, a rota and forty new colleagues from day one. You will have a house, a three-year-old and a city you do not know. That asymmetry is normal and it is temporary, but it is worth naming out loud rather than discovering it in month three.

What consistently helps: something in the diary every week that is yours alone; Sarah’s kindergarten and Plunket playgroups, which are the fastest route into a neighbourhood; and saying yes to invitations for the first six months even when you are tired, because they thin out if you decline them.

Welcome to Wellington14

The honest page

What we would rather you knew now

None of this should change your mind. All of it is easier to meet when you were expecting it.

  1. 01

    The wind is not a joke

    Wellington is the windiest city in the country and it is a real feature of daily life, not a quirky detail. Buy proper coats rather than umbrellas — umbrellas do not survive here. Some people find it invigorating and some find it wearing; you will know which you are by month two.

  2. 02

    Houses are colder than you expect

    Winters are mild by many standards and the housing stock is less prepared for them. This is why we keep going on about insulation and heating: a cold, damp house is the most common regret of a first year here, and it is entirely avoidable at the viewing stage.

  3. 03

    Earthquakes

    Wellington sits on a fault line and you will feel small shakes. Buildings are engineered for it and this is genuinely normal life, but prepare properly once and then stop worrying: a stored water supply, a torch, a plan for where to meet. Sarah’s kindergarten will drill it, calmly.

  4. 04

    The sun is stronger than it looks

    UV levels here are far higher than at comparable northern latitudes, and you can burn in under twenty minutes in summer, including on a cloudy day. Hat, sunscreen and shade in the middle of the day are the local standard and Sarah will learn it at kindergarten.

  5. 05

    The distance from family is the hard part

    Not the flights themselves — the ordinary Sundays. Book the first trip home, or someone’s first trip out, before you leave. Having a date in the calendar changes how the first year feels.

Almost everyone dips somewhere between three and six months, when the novelty has gone and the friendships have not arrived yet. It passes. Ring us when it happens rather than after it.

Getting through winter

Managing the cold, which is not what you expect

Wellington winters are mild by British or northern standards — eight to twelve degrees in the day, and snow essentially never. And you will very likely be colder here than you were at home.

That sounds contradictory and it is the most useful thing in this pack. The reason is the houses, not the weather: much of the housing stock was built for a temperate climate by people who assumed you would put on a jumper — single glazing, limited insulation, no central heating, air moving under the floors. You are not fighting the temperature outside, but an indoor one that follows it.

Damp is the other half. Wellington is humid and windy, washing does not dry outside for weeks, and moisture indoors makes a cool room feel genuinely cold. Mould on window reveals and in wardrobes is a ventilation problem rather than a cleaning one.

What actually works

  1. 01

    Choose the house on warmth, not charm

    The single biggest decision. North-facing, insulated, double glazed if you can find it, with a heat pump. Ask the landlord directly what heating and insulation it has under the Healthy Homes standards, and ask what it costs to heat in July. A cold house cannot be fixed with blankets.

  2. 02

    Heat one room properly, not the whole house

    The local habit, and the cheap one. A heat pump in the living area, doors closed, and electric blankets or a small heater in bedrooms. Trying to hold a whole New Zealand house at a British thermostat setting is how people get frightening power bills.

  3. 03

    Get a dehumidifier in your first month

    Not a luxury here. It makes a cool room feel warm, dries washing indoors, and prevents the mould. Ask any New Zealander and they will tell you the same thing, usually with feeling.

  4. 04

    Ventilate every morning, even when it is cold

    Ten minutes of open windows, including in winter. Counterintuitive and correct: the air you let out is wetter than the air you let in, and dry air is far easier to heat.

  5. 05

    Curtains closed before dusk

    Thermal-lined if possible. Most of the heat you lose goes through the glass, and the difference is immediate and free.

  6. 06

    Dress for indoors

    You will wear slippers and a fleece inside in a way you may find undignified. Everyone does it. Merino is worth the money and is everywhere here.

Welcome to Wellington16

Getting through winter

Sarah’s room, and the wind

Two things worth their own page: the bedroom, because damp is what causes the winter coughs, and the wind, because nothing prepares you for it.

For Sarah’s room

Keep it dry and draught-free rather than hot, and check for condensation on the inside of the window each morning — that is your early warning. Sleeping bags designed for cots hold warmth better than blankets she can kick off. Damp bedrooms, not cold ones, are what cause the winter coughs that fill Akhil’s department every July.

And the wind

It deserves its own sentence. Buy proper coats rather than umbrellas — umbrellas genuinely do not survive here, and you will see broken ones in every bin. Layers, a hood and a wind-proof outer shell will serve you better than any single warm coat.

The good news: the winters are short, the light is better than you are used to, and a clear calm Wellington winter day is one of the finest things in the country.

Getting around

You may not need two cars

Wellington is the one New Zealand city where a car is genuinely optional. Plenty of families run one between them, and some run none at all.

WalkingThe centre is small enough to cross on foot in twenty minutes, and a great many people walk to work. It is what residents love most.
Trains and busesThe Metlink network covers the city, the Hutt Valley and the Kāpiti Coast. Get a Snapper card in week one — cheaper than cash and it works across buses.
The cable carGenuinely part of the transport network as well as a lovely thing. Sarah will ask to go on it repeatedly.
FerriesHarbour ferries to Days Bay for a day out, and the Cook Strait ferry to the South Island — three hours, and it takes your car.
The airportTwenty minutes from most suburbs. Domestic and trans-Tasman flights, a short runway and a famously bumpy approach.
CyclingFlat and lovely along the waterfront and the bays. The hills are honest about themselves.

Driving, and the licence clock

New Zealanders drive on the left. Your overseas licence lets you drive here for a limited period after you arrive, and then you must convert. The rules are changing in late 2026, so confirm your own deadline with NZTA once your travel date is fixed, and diarise it — the clock starts the day you land. Bring both licences and any official translation.

Sarah needs an approved child restraint and the rules are strict and enforced. Sort it before you collect a car, not after.

Central parking is expensive and mostly metered, and many older suburban houses have no off-street parking at all. Worth checking at a viewing, along with the hill.

Money, month to month

What the ordinary bills actually are

Not the salary — the small recurring things nobody mentions until the first one lands.

Rates are not council tax, and you probably will not pay them

This is the one that catches British arrivals every time. Council rates are the local property tax and they are paid by the property owner, not the tenant. If you are renting, you do not pay rates — they are already inside the rent. If you later buy, they become yours: they vary with property value and council, and they are billed quarterly.

Water is the same story in practice: as renters you would not normally be responsible for the property’s fixed water-service charge. Wellington’s water funding arrangements changed in 2026, so read the tenancy agreement — the position could differ if usage-based charging is introduced later. Neighbouring councils differ too, so check the specific address.

The bills you will pay

PowerYou choose your retailer and switching takes days. The big one in a Wellington winter, and it depends on the house far more than the provider.
InternetFibre across most of the city and genuinely fast. Unlimited data is standard.
MobileCompetitive and simple. Prepaid for the first month, then commit.
Contents insuranceWorth having. Ask specifically about earthquake cover and the excess.
GroceriesDairy, meat and imported goods cost more than you expect. Produce is excellent and cheap in season.
Early childhoodFunded hours cut this substantially at three, but there are usually extra charges. Ask each centre for a real weekly number.

The first three months are the expensive ones

Bond and rent in advance, a car, a heater, appliances, Sarah’s kindergarten and the gap before Akhil’s first full pay all arrive together. This is the most underestimated part of any move. Use our cost calculator to see the shape of it — and tell us if the timing is tight, because relocation support is more negotiable than people assume.

Welcome to Wellington19

Running a house here

Rubbish, recycling and the small domestic stuff

Genuinely useful, faintly dull, and otherwise learned by getting it wrong in front of a neighbour.

Recycling is taken seriously

Kerbside collection separates recycling from general rubbish, and glass is usually collected separately — often in its own crate so it can be sorted by colour. Mixed recycling takes paper, cardboard, tins and certain plastics, and the accepted grades are narrower than you may be used to. Everything must be clean and dry.

General rubbish is where councils differ most. Some areas use official council bags bought from supermarkets; others use a private wheelie-bin service you arrange yourself. Ask your landlord or a neighbour which applies at your address, because the wrong thing simply is not collected.

Three things people get wrong in month one

Collection day. It varies street by street and shifts over Christmas. Look up your own address at wellington.govt.nz/rubbish-recycling rather than copying next door — the council site takes the address and tells you the day, the bin and what goes in it. Neighbouring councils have the same tool on their own sites: Hutt City at huttcity.govt.nz, Porirua at poriruacity.govt.nz, Kāpiti at kapiticoast.govt.nz.

Soft plastics and e-waste. Neither belongs in the kerbside bin. Supermarkets take soft plastics; e-waste goes to a recycling centre, usually for a fee.

Composting, and why people bother

Home composting and worm farms are common and often council-subsidised. This is not an eccentricity: environmental care is close to a civic value here, part of the same idea as the Tiaki Promise — leave a place better than you found it.

Warming a Wellington house

Heat pumps are the norm and the cheapest to run. Beyond that: curtains closed before dusk, a dehumidifier if the house is damp, and ten minutes of ventilation each morning even in winter — damp, not cold, is what makes a New Zealand house miserable. Ask what heating and insulation the property has under the Healthy Homes standards before you sign.

Food, shopping and community

Where to buy the things that taste like home

Wellington is small but not narrow. Whatever you cook, the ingredients are here — and the community you are looking for almost certainly already exists.

The weekly shop

Two main supermarket groups, with prices differing noticeably between them, so try both. Loyalty cards are free and make a real difference. Beyond them, the Sunday harbourside market is where Wellingtonians actually buy fruit and vegetables — cheaper, fresher, and an outing in itself.

Indian and Sri Lankan groceries

Newtown is the heart of it, with Asian, Indian, Middle Eastern and Pacific grocers within a few streets — spices, pulses, rice in the right size bag, curry leaves, fresh coconut, and the specific brands you have always used. There are more in the central city and out towards the Hutt. If there is one ingredient you cannot find in your first month, ask us: someone we know will know where it is.

Community — and yes, there is a Kerala one

New Zealand has active, organised Malayali and wider Kerala communities in most main centres, alongside long-established Indian associations — Wellington included. Between them they run Onam and Vishu celebrations, Malayalam classes for children, cricket teams, cultural evenings and a great deal of quiet practical help for new arrivals: which school, which suburb, where to get the good fish.

These groups are usually the single fastest route into a new city, and they are used to people arriving cold and asking. Tell us which community you would like introducing to and we will find the current contact rather than leaving you to search — committee details change, and a name from someone is worth more than a website.

Te reo Māori

The words you will hear in your first week

Te reo Māori is an official language and it is woven through ordinary working life here — emails, ward rounds, school newsletters. Nobody expects fluency. They do notice effort.

Saying it right

Wh is usually an f sound, and ng is the sound in the middle of “singer”. Say every vowel — nothing is silent.

Looking ahead for Sarah

School, when it comes

Sarah has two years before this matters — but it decides where you live, so it is worth understanding now rather than then.

How it works

Children may start school on their fifth birthday and must be enrolled by six, so most start partway through a year rather than all together. Year levels run one to thirteen and are age-based: primary is roughly years one to six, intermediate seven and eight, secondary nine to thirteen — so there is an extra transition in the middle that will be unfamiliar.

Zoning is the whole game

Popular state schools operate enrolment zones, and living inside the zone is what guarantees a place. Out-of-zone places go to a ballot. So the school decision and the housing decision are one decision, made in that order — which is why this page sits here, while you are still choosing a suburb.

What to do while Sarah is three

Three tools do this in an evening. educationcounts.govt.nz has the official directory — every school, its type, roll and contact details. ero.govt.nz publishes the Education Review Office report on each one, which is the honest read. And each school’s own website carries its enrolment zone map, which is the document that decides whether an address qualifies — check the exact street before you sign anything, because zone boundaries run down the middle of roads. Ten minutes now saves a move later. State schooling is free for children of most work-visa holders — confirm your entitlement against Akhil’s specific visa class.

Wellington specifics

Earthquakes and the coast

Prepare properly once, then stop worrying. This is ordinary life here.

Earthquakes

Wellington sits on a fault line and you will feel small shakes. Buildings are engineered for it and this is ordinary life here, but prepare properly once and then stop worrying. In a shake: drop, cover, hold — get under a table, cover your head, hold on until it stops. Do not run outside during it.

Keep stored water, food, a torch, a radio and copies of documents. Agree now where the three of you would meet if you were apart and phones were down — that single conversation is worth more than the kit. Sarah’s kindergarten will drill this calmly and she will take it in her stride.

If you are near the coast

The rule taught to every child here is simple and worth learning on day one: long or strong, get gone. If a shake lasts longer than a minute or is strong enough that it is hard to stand, do not wait for an official warning — move inland or uphill immediately. Know your nearest high ground from home, from work and from Sarah’s kindergarten.

The one we most want you to read

Missing home, and what actually helps

You will miss home. All three of you, at different times and about different things. It is not a sign the move was wrong, and it is not something to be brave about.

Homesickness here has a shape people rarely warn you about. The first weeks are busy and bright — new house, new job, everything an adventure. Then the boxes are unpacked, the visitors have gone, winter arrives, and a wet Tuesday evening can feel very far from everyone you love. That dip often lands somewhere between three and six months. Many people describe it, and for most it eases as routines and friendships take shape.

What consistently helps

  1. 01

    Book the next reunion now

    A date in the diary changes everything. Whether it is your trip back or someone’s trip out, having something to count towards makes the distance finite rather than open-ended.

  2. 02

    Regular calls, not occasional ones

    A fixed weekly time beats spontaneous contact, because the time difference will otherwise eat it. Same slot, same day. Sarah will grow up knowing exactly when she talks to her grandparents.

  3. 03

    Keep two rituals exactly as they were

    A Sunday meal, a festival, a particular way of doing birthdays. Children especially need some things to be unchanged, and it costs nothing to protect them.

When family come to you

Visitors, visas and the first Christmas

This is the question your parents will ask before you have finished telling them the news. It is worth having a real answer ready, because it is really a question about whether they will still see Sarah grow up.

The short version

Visiting New Zealand is straightforward for most people. Visitors from a good number of countries do not need a visa in advance at all — they need an electronic travel authority obtained online before they fly, plus a small tourism levy, and they are then admitted for a stay of a few months. Visitors from other countries apply for a visitor visa in advance. Which applies depends on the passport, not on their relationship to you, so check the passport rather than assuming.

Do this before you tell them the dates

Look up each set of grandparents’ passports on the Immigration New Zealand website and establish which route applies. It takes ten minutes and it changes the planning entirely — an electronic authority is a same-week job, while a visitor visa application needs lead time.

Longer stays, and parents in particular

A standard visitor stay is measured in months rather than weeks, and can often be extended, with a maximum total in any period. There is also a specific visa category for parents of migrants that allows considerably longer, repeated stays — the requirements, sponsorship conditions and health cover obligations are particular, and the rules in this space are revised more often than most. If a parent staying for several months at a time matters to you, ask us early and we will point you at the current settings rather than yesterday’s.

Setting up

Insurance, and what is different here

Two surprises for British arrivals: car insurance is not compulsory, and the accident scheme changes what you actually need cover for.

Car insurance is optional — and you should still buy it

There is no legal requirement to insure a car in New Zealand. A meaningful number of drivers carry none at all, which is precisely why third-party cover matters: if someone uninsured damages your car you may have no practical way to recover it, and if you damage someone else’s without cover the bill is yours. Third-party is inexpensive. Comprehensive is not much more on a modest car. Do not skip this because the law lets you.

Contents insurance

As tenants you insure your contents; the landlord insures the building. Take the natural-hazard element seriously here rather than treating it as small print — contents policies carry cover for earthquake and other natural hazard damage, and in Wellington that is not theoretical. Check what the excess is, and whether the policy pays replacement value or current value.

Life and income protection

This is where the accident scheme changes the maths. ACC covers accidental injury for everyone in New Zealand, residents and visitors alike, including treatment and a proportion of lost earnings while you cannot work. What it does not cover is illness. So income protection here is essentially illness cover, and that is a different product from the one you may have held at home.

Life cover is worth a conversation once, properly, rather than a policy bought in a hurry. Two things to check: whether any cover you hold at home still pays out with you living overseas — many do not — and what Akhil’s employment package already includes, because health and life cover are common in New Zealand employment and you may be about to buy something twice.

Health insurance

Public healthcare covers you, and private health insurance here mainly buys speed and choice for non-urgent treatment — elective surgery, specialist appointments, some diagnostics. It is common as an employment benefit. Ask what the job includes before you buy your own.

Do not buy any of this in your first fortnight. Get through arrival, then sit down once with everything on the table — car, contents, life, income, health — and buy the lot in one conversation. It is quicker and you will not double up.
