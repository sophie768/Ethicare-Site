Welcome to Queensland

A warm welcome, you’re in good hands

Deciding to move your life and career to the other side of the world is a big step, and an exciting one. Whether Queensland is already home to people you know or a brand-new chapter, I want you to feel supported from the very first conversation.

Queensland is a place of sunshine and space: golden beaches, the Great Barrier Reef, rainforest and reef within reach of the cities, and a relaxed, outdoors way of life. The pace is easy, the climate is warm, and the healthcare job market, across Brisbane, the coasts and the regional centres, is strong and genuinely welcoming to international staff.

This guide walks you through healthcare, visas and registration, finances, housing, schooling and cost of living across the state, then breaks down the main places you might live so you can picture daily life in each. Think of it as the conversation we’d have over coffee, in your pocket, whenever you need it.

Welcome to Queensland. We’re so glad you’re here, and we’ll be alongside you every step of the way.

Sophie Careem

✓A warm, outdoors lifestyle with beaches, reef, rainforest and endless sunshine.

Ethicare insight

Candidates often picture Australia as one job market. It isn’t. Queensland registers nationally through Ahpra but hires locally through its Hospital and Health Services, and demand in the regions frequently outpaces Brisbane. That’s where the strongest packages tend to be.

Overview · Culture

Ancient country, modern lifestyle

The Turrbal and Jagera peoples are the traditional custodians of the Brisbane region, known as Meanjin, part of one of the oldest living cultures on earth, a heritage increasingly woven into Queensland life.

Queensland blends a deep Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander heritage with a young, multicultural population drawn by health, mining, tourism and the good life. It is Australia’s most decentralised state, with only about half its people in the capital, which gives its regional cities real weight and character. Festivals like the Brisbane Festival and Riverfire light up the calendar, and the reef, the islands and the rainforest are never far away.

Sophie’s tip

Learn the local place names early, Meanjin (Brisbane) and the many Country names across the state. Using them warmly is genuinely appreciated and helps you feel part of the community faster.

Overview · Transport

Getting around

South-east Queensland has a good, integrated public-transport network, but the state’s scale means a car is useful beyond the cities. Here’s how people get around day to day.

Translink & go card

Buses, trains, ferries and trams run on the tap-on go card, with a flat 50-cent fare per journey across the whole network, one of the cheapest fares anywhere.

Driving

Australians drive on the left. Brisbane and the coasts are easy to drive; a car is near-essential in Toowoomba, the regions and the far north.

Cycling & ferries

Brisbane’s riverside bikeways and the free CityHopper and cross-river ferries make getting around the inner city easy and scenic.

Flying

Brisbane Airport links to the other capitals and regional Queensland; Cairns, the Gold Coast and Townsville also have busy airports.

Sophie’s tip

You can usually drive on a valid overseas licence for a period after arriving, but you’ll need to transfer to a Queensland licence once you’re a resident. Keep your licence and any official translation handy, and pick up a go card on day one.

Ethicare insight

Queensland has some of the world’s strongest UV, and it catches most newcomers out in the first fortnight. Sun protection is a year-round habit here, not a summer one. In the tropical north, also plan around the wet season, marine stingers and, in some waterways, crocodiles, a point worth making to family before they arrive.

Sophie’s View

Why I like Queensland

One of the things I love most about Queensland is how much of life happens outdoors. Whether you’re drawn to Brisbane’s easy river-city life, a surf coast like the Gold or Sunshine Coast, or a tropical northern city like Townsville or Cairns, there’s usually somewhere that fits your priorities. For healthcare professionals, that flexibility means you rarely have to choose between career progression and quality of life, you can often have both.

Having spent many years in the NHS, I know how easily work can take over. What stands out to me about Queensland is how much the sunshine and the outdoors pull people out of the hospital and into life, a swim before a shift, a weekend on the reef. If I could give one piece of advice, it would be to think about your climate, your commute and what you’ll enjoy on your days off. Those things shape your happiness more than people expect.

Section two · Healthcare

Healthcare in Queensland

Queensland’s health system combines universal Medicare cover with a strong network of public hospitals, led by Brisbane’s tertiary centres, and a substantial private sector, delivered through 16 Hospital and Health Services.

Go deeperUnderstanding healthcare in Australia→

Royal Brisbane & Women’s

One of Australia’s largest tertiary hospitals, in Herston, a major trauma, surgical, cancer and specialist centre.

Princess Alexandra

A leading tertiary and quaternary hospital in Woolloongabba, known for trauma, transplants and spinal services.

Gold Coast & Sunshine Coast

Gold Coast University Hospital and Sunshine Coast University Hospital are modern tertiary centres serving fast-growing coasts.

Regional referral hospitals

Townsville University Hospital leads the north; Cairns, Toowoomba, Mackay, Rockhampton and Bundaberg anchor their regions.

Good to know

Eligibility for Medicare usually begins once you hold an appropriate visa. Reciprocal Health Care Agreements give some countries limited Medicare access. Ambulance cover is different here in the best way, the Queensland Ambulance Service is free for Queensland residents, funded by the state.

Registration · Ahpra

Professional registration

Most healthcare professions are regulated nationally through Ahpra, the Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency, and its profession-specific boards.

One national register

Once registered with Ahpra you can work anywhere in Australia, including all of Queensland, registration is national, not state-by-state.

Qualification assessment

Overseas qualifications are assessed against Australian standards; some roles require bridging programs or exams.

English language

You’ll usually need to evidence English proficiency through an approved test or a recognised pathway.

Start early

Registration takes time, begin gathering documents and references well before you plan to travel.

Ethicare insight

In Queensland, as everywhere, the biggest cause of a delayed start is paperwork gathered too late. Certified qualifications, references and ID take longer to assemble than people expect, so begin the day the move feels real.

Section four · Banking & Finance

Setting up your finances

Getting your finances in order early makes day-to-day life much easier. Most of this can be started before you arrive.

Opening a bank account

The major banks are CommBank, Westpac, NAB and ANZ, plus Queensland’s own Bank of Queensland. Many let you open online before you arrive, then verify ID in branch with your passport.

Tax File Number (TFN)

Every worker needs a TFN from the ATO. Apply online once you arrive and give the number to payroll so you’re taxed correctly. Without it, you’ll be taxed at the highest rate.

Sophie’s tip

Open your bank account before you fly where you can, having an account ready makes your payroll setup and first rent payment far smoother in those hectic first weeks.

The money set-up, in order

1Activate your bank account, verify ID in branch, order a debit card and online banking.
2Apply for your TFN, online via the ATO, then give it to payroll immediately.
3Choose your super fund, or accept the employer default, then review later.
4Set up salary packaging, ask payroll or your provider on day one if you’re in public health.
5Register for Medicare, if eligible under your visa or a reciprocal agreement.
6Get a local SIM & number, many banking and government steps need an Australian mobile.
Sophie’s tip

Salary is only part of the equation. The people who settle into Queensland most smoothly are the ones who budget for the bridge, the few weeks between arriving and your first full pay clearing. Think about temporary accommodation, a rental bond (typically four weeks’ rent) lodged with the RTA, a first car, and setting up a home. None of it is dramatic, but together it’s real, and planning for it removes almost all of the early stress.

Sophie Careem · Founder & Director

Cost of Living · What the locals know

10 things nobody tells you before moving to QLD

1Book temporary accommodation. Renting sight-unseen from abroad rarely goes well; land first, then look.
2Bring your rental references. No Australian rental history makes early applications harder.
3The UV is relentless. Sunscreen is a daily, year-round habit here, not a summer one.
4Public transport is 50 cents. A flat fare across the Translink network, get a go card on arrival.
5Mind the stinger season. In the tropical north, marine stingers make patrolled beaches and stinger suits the norm in summer.
6Be croc-wise up north. In far-north waterways, obey the warning signs, this is genuine, not folklore.
7Plan around the wet season. The north’s summer brings heavy rain and cyclone risk from about November to April.

9Furniture is cheap second-hand. Marketplace and Gumtree furnish a home for very little.
10Distances are deceptive. Cairns to Brisbane is further than London to Rome, don’t underestimate the state.

Section seven · Housing & Areas

Finding a place to live

From Brisbane apartments to coastal family homes and regional towns, Queensland offers space and value, and where you live shapes your commute, so think about transport links to work.

Most rentals are advertised online and move quickly in popular areas. You’ll usually apply with ID, references and proof of income, then pay a bond (typically four weeks’ rent) lodged with the Residential Tenancies Authority (RTA). Many newcomers rent for a few months first to get to know an area. The main sites are realestate.com.au (largest) and domain.com.au.

Choosing your area, the order that saves the most stress

1Fix your work site first. Everything flows from your commute, decide it before you fall for a suburb.
2Check school catchments if you have children. Local intake is guaranteed; out-of-area isn’t.
3Shortlist two or three areas and rent short-term in one before committing.
4Confirm the exact address is in-catchment before you sign, boundaries can split a street.

Utilities & getting set up

Power

Energex (south-east) or Ergon Energy (regional) run the network; you choose a retailer. Set up on move-in.

Water

Urban Utilities and regional councils; if renting, most water charges are the landlord’s.

Internet

NBN via Telstra, Optus, TPG or Aussie Broadband.

Setting up home

Kmart, IKEA and Bunnings furnish a first home fast; Marketplace for second-hand.

Sophie’s tip

No Australian rental references? Bring landlord references from home, proof of income and savings, and your job offer. A strong, complete application often beats a longer local history.

Housing & family · Choosing where to live

Choosing where to live

For many healthcare professionals, the job is only half the decision. The other half is where your family will settle well. Schools, commute, housing cost and lifestyle are best weighed together. Here’s a practical way to think it through.

Schools often come first

Access to a government school is usually tied to where you live. Popular schools can be pinned to an exact address, catchments can change street by street, and out-of-area places aren’t guaranteed. If you have children, check the catchment before you sign a long-term lease. Independent, Catholic and faith schools offer more flexibility, but usually charge fees.

Your commute still matters

South-east Queensland has a good, integrated network with the flat 50-cent go card fare, so many people commute without a car, though one is useful for the coasts, Toowoomba and the far north. Think about your shift start and finish times, whether buses, trains and ferries realistically reach your site, and parking, which is a real cost near the big hospitals.

The order that usually works best

A simple sequence that keeps the big decisions in the right order.

  1. 1Confirm your likely work site.
  2. 2Shortlist suitable school options if you have children.
  3. 3Check the catchment maps for those schools.
  4. 4Compare commute times to work at shift hours.
  5. 5Review rental availability and cost.
  6. 6Rent short-term first where you can.
  7. 7Choose a longer-term suburb once you know the area.

Section eight · Shopping & Food

Groceries, food & community

The essentials in one place, the big chains, the budget picks, and where different communities find the flavours of home.

The big chains

Woolworths & Coles for full-range everywhere; ALDI for the cheapest weekly shop; IGA and independents for neighbourhood top-ups, vital in regional towns.

Flavours of home

Specialty importers stock South African and British staples; South Asian & Halal in Sunnybank, Inala and Woodridge; busy Asian food precincts in Sunnybank and Fortitude Valley’s Chinatown.

Sophie’s tip

Bring your own bags, single-use plastic shopping bags are banned across Queensland, and most people keep a few reusable ones in the car for exactly this reason.

Good to know

In Brisbane, halal butchers and grocers are easy to find (Sunnybank, Inala, Woodridge, Logan) and kosher goods come through the Brisbane Hebrew Congregation and specialty importers. In the regional centres it’s more limited: Townsville, Cairns and Toowoomba have some halal-certified outlets and the major supermarkets carry a growing halal range, but dedicated kosher supplies are largely Brisbane-based, many regional families stock up on a city trip or order online.

Faith & community · Places of worship

Religion & places of worship

Australia has no state religion and law protects freedom of belief. Queensland is religiously diverse: at the last census around half of residents reported no religion, Christianity remained the largest faith, and Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism and Judaism are all established and growing, most strongly across the Brisbane and Gold Coast areas, with active communities in the regional centres too.

Most communities list service and prayer times online and warmly welcome newcomers, often one of the quickest ways to build a support network after you arrive.

The main faiths & where to find them

Christianity

Catholic, Anglican, Uniting, Pentecostal and Orthodox churches statewide. Landmark cathedrals include St Stephen’s (Catholic) and St John’s (Anglican) in Brisbane.

Islam

The Holland Park Mosque (one of Australia’s oldest, 1908) plus mosques across Brisbane and in the Gold Coast, Logan, Toowoomba, Townsville and Cairns.

Hinduism

Temples across Brisbane including the Brisbane Hindu Temple and the Shri Selva Vinayakar Temple, with cultural and festival programmes through the year.

Buddhism

Several temples and meditation centres, including Chung Tian Temple in Priestdale and Vietnamese and Thai temples across the suburbs.

Sikhism

Gurdwaras in Brisbane and on the Gold Coast welcome worshippers and visitors, with community kitchens (langar) open to all.

Judaism

The Brisbane Hebrew Congregation and Progressive congregations serve Queensland’s Jewish community, alongside kosher supplies and community services.

By location, what you’ll find near you

A guide to the main faith communities in each city in this guide. Brisbane has the fullest range; the regional centres focus on Christian churches and a local mosque.

If regular worship matters to you, factor it into where you settle. In Brisbane and on the coasts it’s rarely an issue, but in the regions your nearest mosque, temple or gurdwara may be an hour or more away, worth confirming alongside schools and your commute.

Faith & community · Diversity & inclusion

Diversity, inclusion & belonging

Queensland is one of the world’s most multicultural societies, and the law protects everyone’s right to be treated equally. Whoever you are, and whoever your family is, you’re welcome here.

Go deeperReligion, community & belonging in Australia→

A multicultural home

Around one in five Queenslanders was born overseas, with communities from every corner of the world. Multicultural Affairs Queensland and local councils run settlement and community programs to help newcomers connect and belong.

Your rights in law

The Queensland Anti-Discrimination Act 1991, the Human Rights Act 2019 and federal law protect people from discrimination on the basis of race, religion, age, sex, disability, sexuality and gender identity. The Queensland Human Rights Commission handles complaints.

LGBTQ+ inclusion

Same-sex marriage has been legal Australia-wide since 2017, and sexuality and gender identity are protected attributes. Brisbane Pride (September) and events across the state are part of the calendar, and QLife (1800 184 527) offers free, anonymous peer support.

Inclusive workplaces & care

A growing number of Queensland health services hold Rainbow Tick accreditation or run active inclusion networks. Interpreters are free through TIS National (131 450), and the NDIS supports people with disability.

Ethicare insight

Queensland Health and most employers have active diversity and inclusion commitments, and many run staff networks for culturally diverse, First Nations and LGBTQ+ workers. If belonging at work matters to you, tell us, we can factor a team’s culture and inclusion record into where we suggest you land.

Section ten · Education & Family

Schooling & family life

Queensland offers a full range of early childhood education, schools and universities, run by the state’s Department of Education. The school year runs late January to mid-December across four terms, a flip from the Northern Hemisphere calendar worth planning around.

Go deeperEducation & schools in Australia→

Early childhood

Long daycare, kindergarten and family day care for ages 0–5, with subsidies. Kindy is the year before Prep.

Primary

From Prep (first compulsory year) through Year 6, across state, Catholic and independent schools.

Secondary

Years 7–12, ending with the Queensland Certificate of Education (QCE), leading to university or training.

Tertiary

UQ, QUT, Griffith, James Cook and others, plus TAFE Queensland across the state.

Public school fees for visa-holder families

Queensland-specific: fees are handled by Education Queensland International (EQI), and many healthcare families are exempt.

Ethicare insight

Queensland’s school-fee rules are more favourable for clinicians than they first appear. Dependants of 482 visa holders whose occupation sits on the Medium and Long-term Strategic Skills List, which covers many nursing, medical and allied-health roles, are usually exempt from state-school tuition, paying only a one-off application fee of about $305. Where a fee does apply, it is charged per child, roughly A$12,600 a year in primary up to A$15,800 in the senior years (2026), billed by the school week and paid in advance, with hardship waivers for lower incomes. Confirm your occupation on your visa grant, and note your employer can sometimes help with costs.

School catchments come first

For Queensland state schools, your home address determines your local-intake (catchment) school, children inside the zone are guaranteed a place, while out-of-area places depend on capacity, and popular schools operate enrolment management plans. Boundaries can run street-by-street, so always check the exact address before you sign.

For families, the school decision usually leads the housing decision here, deciding it first prevents an expensive mid-year move.

Education & Family · Partners & work

Employment for your partner

A move only works if it works for the whole family. The good news: on most skilled and employer-sponsored visas your partner is included with full work rights, free to take any job, full or part-time, with any employer.

Work rights come as standard

Partners added to a 482, 186, or skilled 189/190/491 visa generally have unrestricted work rights, no separate employer sponsorship needed. Always confirm the exact conditions on your grant letter.

A broad job market

Queensland has demand across healthcare, education, mining & resources, construction, tourism, retail and professional services, strongest in the south-east, with real opportunities in the regions too.

Helping your partner land work

1Reformat the CV to Australian style, concise, achievement-led, no photo or date of birth. Set up a strong LinkedIn with an Australian location.
2Search the main boards, SEEK, Indeed and Jora, plus Queensland Health careers and council pages for public-sector roles.
3Check if the profession is regulated, teaching, nursing, trades and others need registration or a skills assessment. Start it before you arrive.
4Register with recruitment agencies, many Queensland roles are filled through agencies, who can also advise on local expectations and pay.
Ethicare insight

A partner who lands well is one of the surest signs a move will last, so we treat their search as part of the plan, not an add-on. Tell us early what your partner does; if they’re also in healthcare, we can often help them directly in Queensland.
