Why Australia

Molecular imaging, growing demand, and a country built for the outdoors

Nuclear medicine technologists are in steady, growing demand as PET/CT and theranostics expand — public and private, metro and regional. One national registration lets you work anywhere in the country.

  • Growing demand as PET/CT and theranostics expand
  • A specialist register, distinct from radiography and radiation therapy
  • National registration recognised in every state and territory
"Specialised, current practice — and the coast on your days off."

Clinicians move here from across the world — the UK, South Africa, Ireland, the UAE and beyond — and many tell us the move gives back real time without compromising the work.

Getting registered

Registration overview

Reviewed July 2026 · checked against the Medical Radiation Practice Board of Australia’s current published guidance

Nuclear medicine technologist is a protected title, so you register with the MRPBA through Ahpra. This division sits differently from the others: there is no HCPC/CORU shortcut, so most overseas qualifications go through individual assessment — and the skills assessment is run by ANZSNM, after registration rather than before.

Lead with registration. You'll typically be assembling:

  • Your nuclear medicine qualification and full academic transcript
  • Evidence of supervised clinical training, where required
  • Registration history and a current Certificate of Good Standing
  • English-language evidence, where the standard requires it

Pathways into registration

Your route depends first on whether the Board already recognises your qualification — for nuclear medicine, individual assessment is the usual route.

Qualification assessment

The Board checks your program against the Australian nuclear-medicine standard — educational level, accreditation, and core competencies including radiopharmacy, radiation safety, instrumentation, SPECT/CT and PET/CT. Where gaps remain, the outcome may direct you to the National MRP examination or supervised practice.

Migration skills assessment

For skilled-migration visas the assessment is carried out by ANZSNM (the gazetted authority for nuclear medicine), generally after MRPBA registration. On an employer-sponsored 482 you may not need one at all.

Scope of practice

From SPECT/CT to PET/CT and theranostics

Nuclear medicine technologists work across radiopharmacy, imaging and an expanding therapy role.

The areas you can work in

The core of the job

Radiopharmacy and radiation safety, gamma-camera and SPECT/CT imaging, and PET/CT — with a fast-growing role in theranostics.

Where it can lead

Senior, lead and educator roles offer a genuine career ladder, and theranostics is expanding fast enough to build a speciality around.

Registration and licence are separate

You need both: MRPBA registration lets you practise, and in most states a radiation use licence covers handling unsealed radioactive materials. Your employer usually helps with the local licence.

Common questions

Frequently asked

Is the skills assessment done by ASMIRT?

Not for nuclear medicine — the migration skills assessment is carried out by ANZSNM (the Australian and New Zealand Society of Nuclear Medicine), the gazetted authority for this profession, and it generally comes after MRPBA registration. ASMIRT assesses radiographers and radiation therapists. On an employer-sponsored 482 you may not need a skills assessment at all.

Will my UK or Irish qualification be recognised?

The HCPC/CORU comparable-regulator routes apply to radiography and radiation therapy, not nuclear medicine — so most overseas nuclear-medicine qualifications go through individual assessment against the Australian standard. We'll help you understand what's likely from your specific qualification.

Will I have to sit the National exam?

It depends on your assessment. Where your qualification isn't assessed as substantially equivalent, the outcome may direct you to the National MRP examination or to a period of supervised practice. Your assessment confirms it.

Is registration the same as a radiation licence?

No. MRPBA registration is national and lets you practise; in most states you also need a radiation use licence — which for nuclear medicine covers handling unsealed radioactive materials — issued separately by each state or territory. Your employer usually helps with the local licence.
