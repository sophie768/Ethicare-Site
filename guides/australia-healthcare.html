Understanding
healthcare in Australia

Whether you’ll be practising in it or relying on it for your family, it helps to know how Australian healthcare fits together, and where you stand from the day you land.

The big picture

A universal system, with private care alongside

Reviewed July 2026 · checked against current Services Australia and Department of Health guidance. Eligibility rules and thresholds change, always confirm your own position at the source.

Australia runs a universal public system, funded through taxation and a Medicare levy, alongside a substantial private sector. Most people use a mix of the two across their lives.

Healthcare is delivered by the states and territories but underpinned by national funding and a national medicines scheme, which is why standards are broadly consistent wherever you live. For clinicians, that scale and that public–private mix are part of the appeal: broad experience, and real choice over where and how you work.

There are two questions on most people’s minds when they read a page like this. Will my family be looked after? And what will it cost us? This guide answers both including the parts Medicare doesn’t cover, which are the parts newcomers are most often surprised by.

The short version. If you arrive as a permanent resident, you enrol in Medicare and most of your care is free or heavily subsidised. If you arrive on a temporary work visa, your position depends on your passport, and for many of our clinicians it’s better than they expect. Either way, plan for dental, ambulance and optical, which sit outside Medicare for most adults.

The backbone

Medicare, in plain terms

Medicare is the national scheme that subsidises or covers much of the cost of care. For your family, it’s the thing to sort early; for your work, it’s the system much of your practice connects to.

What it covers

Much of your care

Free treatment as a public patient in a public hospital, and rebates toward GP visits, tests and many specialist services. Some services are bulk-billed (nothing to pay); others carry a gap fee, which is the difference between the rebate and what the clinic charges.

Who’s eligible

Depends on your visa & passport

Citizens and permanent residents enrol. Many temporary visa holders qualify through a reciprocal health care agreement between Australia and their home country. Others rely on private cover, sometimes as a condition of the visa itself.

The levy

How it’s funded

Most taxpayers pay a Medicare levy through income tax, a proportion of income, deducted like any other tax. Higher earners without private hospital cover may also pay a surcharge on top. Rates change; check the current figures with the ATO.

The question everyone asks first

Where you’ll stand, by how you arrive

This is the part worth reading properly, because it’s the difference between arriving covered and arriving exposed. There are broadly three positions.

1

Permanent resident or citizen, full Medicare

You enrol and you’re in: public hospital care, GP rebates, PBS medicines, the lot. Your dependent children are enrolled alongside you. This is the simplest position and the one most of our clinicians reach eventually, whether they arrive that way or transition later.

2

Temporary work visa, from a reciprocal-agreement country

Australia holds reciprocal health care agreements with eleven countries: the United Kingdom, the Republic of Ireland, New Zealand, Belgium, Finland, Italy, Malta, the Netherlands, Norway, Slovenia and Sweden. If you hold a passport from one of these and you’re here on an eligible visa, you can enrol for Medicare access to medically necessary treatment.

What that covers varies by country, and the differences matter. Most agreements include subsidised GP visits and public hospital treatment; the New Zealand and Ireland agreements are narrower and don’t extend to GP visits in the same way. Check your own country’s agreement at the source before you assume.

3

Temporary visa, no reciprocal agreement

If your passport isn’t on that list, South Africa, the UAE, India, the Philippines and many others aren’t, you won’t enrol in Medicare on a temporary visa. You’ll hold private health insurance instead, and for most sponsored work visas maintaining adequate cover is a formal condition of the visa (you may see it written as condition 8501). Your employer sometimes contributes; often they don’t. Budget for it.

We raise this early with everyone, because it changes the family budget and occasionally the choice of route. It is never a reason not to come, but it should never be a surprise either. And to be clear: we’re recruiters, not migration or insurance advisers. We’ll tell you what to check and where; the answer has to come from the official source or a licensed adviser.

First fortnight

Enrolling, and what to bring

Enrolment is administrative rather than difficult. The people who find it smooth are the ones who arrive with the paperwork already in a folder.

1

Enrol once you’ve arrived

You generally enrol after you land, in person at a service centre or online, depending on your circumstances. It isn’t something to do from abroad, so don’t worry that you’re behind before you fly.

2

Bring passports and visa evidence

Passports for every family member, and evidence of your visa grant. If you’re enrolling under a reciprocal agreement, you’ll usually need to show you’re enrolled in your home country’s system too.

3

Have an Australian address ready

A card has to be posted somewhere. Temporary accommodation is generally fine, but a rental agreement or a utility bill makes everything faster, another reason to sort housing early.

4

Set up a myGov account

MyGov is the front door to most government services here, Medicare included. Linking Medicare to it means claims, records and your digital card all sit in one place. Do it in your first week.

5

Interim cover in the gap

There can be a short window between landing and your card arriving. Travel or private cover across those first weeks is cheap peace of mind, particularly with children.

6

Then choose a GP

Card in hand, register the family with a local practice. Don’t wait for someone to be unwell. New-patient appointments can take a fortnight or more in busy areas.

Two systems, working together

Public and private

Understanding how the two relate makes the whole system click into place, both as a patient and as a clinician.

The public system

State-run hospitals and services provide emergency, acute and much elective care, free or heavily subsidised through Medicare. It’s where a great deal of teaching, training and complex work happens, and where most of the roles we recruit for sit.

The private system

Private hospitals and clinics offer choice of provider and often shorter waits for elective care, funded by insurance and out-of-pocket fees. Many clinicians work across both, and some build a portfolio deliberately.

How patients move between them

The same specialist may see you publicly or privately; the same scan may be bulk-billed at one clinic and charged at another. Elective waits are the usual reason families take out cover, urgent care doesn’t wait for anyone in either system.

Told straight

Private health insurance

Whether you need it depends on your visa, your income and your family. Here’s the shape of the decision, without the sales pitch.

If it’s a visa condition, it isn’t optional. Many temporary work visas require you to hold and maintain adequate health cover for yourself and every family member for the whole time you’re here. Letting it lapse is a visa problem, not just a health one.

If you’re Medicare-eligible, it’s a genuine choice. The public system will look after you. Families choose cover for shorter elective waits, a choice of doctor, a private room, and “extras” policies that put something back toward dental, optical and physiotherapy, the things Medicare largely doesn’t fund for adults.

Two rules are worth knowing before you decide. Higher earners without private hospital cover pay an additional surcharge at tax time, so for some households cover costs less than not having it. And there’s a loading that increases premiums for people who take out hospital cover later in life, with concessions for recent arrivals, so the timing of your first policy can matter for years. Both are threshold-and-percentage rules that change; get the current numbers from the ATO and the government’s comparison site rather than from a broker’s brochure.

Policies also carry waiting periods. Months before some services can be claimed, longer for pregnancy and pre-existing conditions. If a baby or a known procedure is on the horizon, that timing deserves real attention before you pick a policy.

The honest position: don’t buy the first policy you’re shown, and don’t let anyone tell you the public system isn’t good enough. It is. Cover buys convenience, choice and the extras, which for some families is worth a great deal and for others isn’t.

Everyday care

GPs, referrals and the PBS

Day-to-day healthcare runs through general practice, with a national medicines scheme keeping prescriptions affordable.

Your GP is the front door: the first stop for most concerns and the gatekeeper for referrals to specialists and many tests. Unlike some systems you may know, you don’t register with one practice for life. You can see any GP anywhere, but continuity is worth having, so most families settle on one.

Bulk billing and gap fees. A bulk-billing practice accepts the Medicare rebate as full payment, so you pay nothing at the desk. Many others charge a fee and Medicare refunds part of it, leaving you a gap. Bulk billing is more common for children, concession-card holders and in some regional areas; it has become less common generally, so ask when you book rather than assuming. Specialists usually charge a gap, sometimes a substantial one, and you’ll want to ask the cost before the appointment. Nobody minds the question.

The PBS. Prescription medicines are subsidised through the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme, which caps what most people pay for listed medicines, with a lower cap for concession-card holders and a safety net once a household’s spending passes a yearly threshold. Not every medicine is listed, and brand names differ from what you may be used to, so check your family’s regular prescriptions against the PBS before you move, and bring a written summary of your medical history and current doses.

The part people don’t expect

What Medicare doesn’t cover

Clinicians from tax-funded systems are rarely surprised by the hospital care. They’re often surprised by these.

Adult dental

Largely private and out of pocket. There is public dental care for eligible concession-card holders, usually with long waits, and schemes that help with children’s dental for eligible families. For most working adults, plan to pay, and factor it into the year’s budget, especially if anyone is due significant work.

Ambulance

This one catches people out. Ambulance cover is arranged state by state rather than nationally, in some places it’s provided, in others you subscribe or insure, and an uncovered call-out can be a unpleasant bill. Check the arrangement in your state in your first week.

Optical, allied health & more

Glasses, contact lenses, most physiotherapy, psychology beyond limited referred sessions, podiatry and similar sit largely outside Medicare, or attract only partial rebates. These are what “extras” insurance is designed for.

None of this is a reason for alarm, it’s a reason to plan. Families who know about ambulance and dental before they arrive absorb them easily. Families who find out via a bill do not.

If something goes wrong

Knowing where to go, before you need to

Worth reading once and remembering. Most newcomers’ first real interaction with the system is unplanned.

Emergency

Call 000

Triple zero reaches ambulance, fire and police. It’s the number to teach your children on day one. Emergency departments at public hospitals treat anyone who needs them, regardless of cover, care first, paperwork later.

Urgent, not an emergency

Urgent care & after-hours GPs

Between a GP appointment and an emergency department sit urgent care clinics and after-hours services, with growing bulk-billed provision. For a sprain, a fever or stitches, they’re usually the faster, kinder option.

Not sure

Healthdirect · free advice

A national free health advice line and website staffed by health professionals, available around the clock. When you’re new and unsure whether something warrants a hospital, it’s exactly the right first call.

Where you come in

Practising in Australian healthcare

For clinicians, the system offers broad experience and a collaborative, patient-centred culture, with national registration that lets you move between states without starting again.

You might work in a public health service, a private provider, or community and primary care, and many roles offer a mix of the two. Practice tends to be team-based and relatively flat: your professional opinion is expected, not merely permitted, and being asked “what do you think?” by a senior colleague is normal rather than a test. Clinicians arriving from more hierarchical systems tell us this takes a few weeks to trust and then becomes the thing they’d least want to give up. That said, it’s a tendency rather than a guarantee: culture varies from one workplace and team to the next, so it’s worth asking what a particular department is really like when you interview, and talking to people already doing the job.

There is a genuine emphasis on culturally safe care, including for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients, and you’ll meet it in induction, in documentation and in how teams talk about patients. It is treated as clinical quality, not as an add-on.

The honest counterweights: services are busy, some regions carry real workforce pressure, and distance is a clinical factor in a country this size, retrieval, telehealth and outreach are ordinary parts of practice rather than exceptions. Told straight

What’s worth knowing before you rely on it

Six things we’d rather you heard from us than discovered later.

1

Bulk billing is less universal than it sounds

“Free GP visits” is how Medicare gets described abroad. In practice many clinics charge a gap. Ask when you book.

2

Elective waits are real

Urgent care is prompt and excellent. Non-urgent surgery in the public system can mean a long wait, which is precisely why many families hold cover.

3

Ambulance cover varies by state

There is no single national answer, and an uncovered call-out is expensive. Check your state, not the internet’s general advice.

4

Your visa condition is yours to maintain

If cover is required by your visa, keeping it current is your responsibility for the whole stay, including through job changes.

5

Thresholds and rates change

The levy, the surcharge, PBS caps and insurance loadings all move. Anything you read anywhere, here included, should be checked against the current official figures.

6

We don’t give insurance or tax advice

We’ll tell you what to look into and flag what usually applies to clinicians in your position. The advice itself must come from the official sources or a licensed adviser.

Sophie’s insight

Two things reassure most families fastest: that healthcare here is good and universal, and that sorting their own access is simpler than they feared. Get your Medicare position clear before you accept the role, choose a family GP early, check your prescriptions before you fly, and find out how ambulance works in your state. That’s most of it.

The one I always raise unprompted is dental. It’s the cost people don’t plan for, and it’s much easier to absorb when you knew it was coming. For your work, the public–private mix is a real strength, it’s part of why clinicians find the experience here so broad.

Common questions

The things people ask us first

Will my family be covered by Medicare?

It depends on your visa and your passport. Citizens and permanent residents enrol in Medicare and so do their dependent children. Many temporary visa holders qualify through one of Australia’s eleven reciprocal health care agreements, the UK, Ireland, New Zealand, Belgium, Finland, Italy, Malta, the Netherlands, Norway, Slovenia and Sweden. If your passport isn’t on that list, you’ll hold private cover instead, often as a condition of the visa. We flag this early, because it affects your budget.

Do I need private health insurance?

If your visa requires it, yes, and you must maintain it for your whole stay. If you’re Medicare-eligible it’s a real choice: the public system will look after you, and cover buys shorter elective waits, choice of doctor, and extras toward dental, optical and physiotherapy. Two tax rules can tip the maths, a surcharge for higher earners without hospital cover, and a loading for taking out cover later in life, so it’s worth checking the current thresholds before deciding.

How soon can I enrol after arriving, and what do I bring?

You enrol once you’re here, not before. Bring passports for everyone, evidence of your visa grant, an Australian address, and, if you’re enrolling under a reciprocal agreement, evidence you’re in your home country’s system. Set up a myGov account in your first week and link Medicare to it. Hold interim cover across the gap between landing and your card arriving.

How do I see a doctor as a newcomer?

Register the family with a local GP practice, they’re the front door for most care and referrals. Do it before anyone is unwell, because new-patient appointments can take a fortnight or more in busy areas. Ask whether the clinic bulk-bills (nothing to pay) or charges a gap fee. You’re not tied to one practice, but continuity is worth having.

Is everything really free?

No, and we’d rather say so. Public hospital care as a public patient is free, and Medicare rebates cover much of everyday care. But many GPs charge a gap, specialists often charge a significant one, and adult dental, ambulance in some states, optical and most allied health sit largely outside Medicare. The big frightening costs are covered; the everyday ones need a modest budget.

What about ambulance cover?

This is the one that surprises people most. Ambulance is arranged state by state rather than nationally, in some places it’s provided, in others you subscribe or insure, and an uncovered call-out can be a serious bill. Find out how it works in your state in your first week; it’s usually a small annual cost to fix.

Will my regular medication be available?

Usually there’s an equivalent, but brand names and availability differ and not every medicine is listed on the PBS. Check your family’s prescriptions against the PBS before you move, bring a written summary of your history and current doses, and carry enough supply for the transition and your first GP appointment.

Are waiting lists long?

For anything urgent, no. Emergency and acute care are prompt and of a high standard. For non-urgent elective surgery, public waits can be long, and they vary by state and by procedure. That gap is the honest reason many families hold private hospital cover, and it’s worth weighing against your own circumstances rather than a national average.

How different will practising here feel?

Clinically familiar; culturally a little flatter. Teams are collaborative, your opinion is expected rather than tolerated, and culturally safe care is treated as clinical quality. Registration is national, so you can move between states without starting again. Services are busy, and distance shapes practice, retrieval, telehealth and outreach are ordinary rather than exceptional. Your profession guide covers the day-to-day properly.
