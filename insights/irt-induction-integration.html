Chapter 06

Induction, integration & building belonging

Sophie CareemFounder, Ethicare Resourcing · former NHS change leader
Toolkit · 06 of 08

The flight landing is not the finish line. The first year is.

Starting a new job is hard. Starting one in a new country, in an unfamiliar health system, with your furniture still on a ship somewhere, is something else entirely. Nobody is at their best in week one, and expecting it costs you people. Employers who plan for the dip, instead of being surprised by it, are the ones whose recruits find their feet fast and stay.

Since the start of 2022, more than 19,800 international practitioners have registered to practise in Australia for the first time, over 10,800 of them nurses and midwives.

AhpraAhpra media release, 28 April 2023: first-time registrations of international practitioners since January 2022

Induction is only the beginning

Most organisations have an induction: mandatory training, IT set-up, policies, paperwork. All important, but that’s organisational induction, not integration. Internationally educated professionals usually need more to understand how the system actually works:

  • Clinical pathways and models of care
  • Documentation standards
  • Local legislation and professional expectations
  • Communication styles and escalation processes
  • Workplace culture and patient expectations
  • The wider healthcare system

Helping someone understand how the health system works matters just as much as showing them where the staff room is.

Confidence isn’t competence

A temporary dip in confidence should never be mistaken for a lack of competence. Many internationally educated clinicians bring years of experience and specialist skill, yet even the most experienced can feel uncertain adapting to a different system. With steady supervision, protected learning time and honest feedback, confidence comes back fast. It grows through support, not pressure.

Supervision: a bridge, not a barrier

For many internationally educated professionals, a period of supervision is a regulatory requirement, but it’s a mistake to treat it as a hurdle to clear. Done well, supervision is one of the fastest routes to confidence: a structured, supportive way to learn how care is delivered here, ask questions safely, and demonstrate the competence someone already has in a new context. Left to chance, it becomes anxiety for the new clinician and pressure on the supervisor.

Supervision that builds confidence

Plan it

  • Enough supervisor capacity
  • Protected time, not goodwill
  • Clear scope & milestones

Support the supervisor

  • Recognise their time
  • Brief them on the transition
  • Share the load across the team

Focus on growth

  • Regular, honest feedback
  • Confidence, not compliance
  • Independence as the goal

The shift in mindset is simple but powerful: supervision isn’t about checking whether someone is good enough to be here. It’s about helping an already-capable professional become confident and independent in a new system as quickly as possible.

Seen as an arc rather than a hurdle, supervision has a clear beginning, middle and end, and a defined point where independent practice begins.

From supervised to independent practice

Orientation

  • Shadowing & observation
  • Learning local systems
  • Questions actively encouraged

Competence assessment

  • Structured, fair review
  • Evidence against local standards
  • Feedback, not judgement

Independent practice

  • Graduated responsibility
  • A clear, agreed sign-off point
  • Pastoral support continues

The hidden curriculum

One of the most interesting things I saw in the NHS was how much of integration happens outside clinical practice. Many NHS organisations run international inductions covering the unwritten things local staff take for granted, sometimes called the “hidden curriculum.”

The things nobody writes down

Language & etiquette

  • Everyday expressions
  • Local acronyms
  • Workplace etiquette

How things work

  • How meetings run
  • Escalation & speaking up
  • Professional communication

Belonging

  • Social customs
  • Humour & small talk
  • Building relationships

Something as small as knowing that “fancy a brew?” is an invitation to a cup of tea helps someone feel included rather than lost. Being able to join the conversation, get the humour and take part confidently does an enormous amount for a person’s sense of belonging.

A transition curriculum for Australia and New Zealand

There’s a real opportunity to build on induction with a structured international transition curriculum, combining clinical orientation with practical guidance on living and working locally. Topics might include:

  • How the local health system is structured
  • Professional expectations and clinical communication
  • Local terminology and abbreviations
  • Māori health, Te Tiriti o Waitangi and cultural safety in New Zealand
  • Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health and cultural safety in Australia
  • Everyday language and community life
  • The practical side of settling into a new country
  • Career development, wellbeing and resilience

I haven’t yet seen a programme this thorough consistently in place across Australia or New Zealand. I think both public services and private providers could develop their own, ideally supported by a nationally consistent framework that sets core standards while leaving room to tailor content to each service, community and set of values.

A genuine welcome, both ways

Integration is a two-way effort: the best programmes prepare the receiving team as much as the arriving clinician.

What good induction includes

For the clinician

System orientation
Prescribing & referral routes
Supervision & check-ins

For the team

A named buddy
Cultural awareness
Realistic ramp-up

This is exactly the kind of transition curriculum we decided to build ourselves.

Case study: our Kiwi Workplace Readiness Programme

This is where our personality really shows. Rather than leave integration to chance, we built our own programme, the Kiwi Workplace Readiness Programme, to prepare internationally trained clinicians for life and work in Aotearoa before they arrive. It started with anaesthetic technicians joining Health New Zealand / Te Whatu Ora, and we’re extending it to more professions.

It runs over four modules, and it’s offered to our candidates and clients at no extra cost, because we think it’s simply part of doing this properly:

The Kiwi Workplace Readiness Programme

Preparing overseas-trained clinicians to arrive ready: professionally, culturally and personally.

Module 1

Introduction to Aotearoa

The land, the cities, the history and what it means to be a Kiwi: right down to the accent, the slang and the sense of humour.

Module 2

Tikanga & Te Reo Māori

Māori customs and language (manaakitanga, whanaungatanga, tapu and noa) for genuinely culturally competent care.

Module 3

Communication in healthcare

Active listening and empathetic, clear communication for speakers of English as a second language, in real clinical scenarios.

Module 4

Health NZ & life admin

Workplace culture and expectations at Te Whatu Ora, plus bank accounts, IRD numbers, schools and finding somewhere to live.

The point isn’t the modules themselves. It’s the message they send a new arrival: we thought about you before you got here. That’s what turns a placement into a person who stays. She’ll be right, as they say.

Case study: a sustainable AT team in Palmerston North

A perioperative team within Health New Zealand / Te Whatu Ora in Palmerston North had Anaesthetic Technician vacancies it couldn’t fill locally, and a locum bill to match. Three internationally educated ATs were recruited for values and long-term commitment as much as clinical capability, prepared through the Kiwi Workplace Readiness Programme before departure, and met at the airport by their own service manager. All three posts are now permanent, locum reliance has fallen, and the families have settled. The full story, honest parts included, is in the case studies.

Best practice · Induction that actually integrates
  • Pair every arrival with a buddy for the first ninety days.
  • Cover the hidden curriculum, not just the mandatory training.
  • Brief the receiving team on the why and the how, before arrival.
  • Treat supervision as an arc with a defined end point, and say so out loud.

Prepare the existing team, too

Preparing the new arrival for the workplace is half the job. Preparing the workplace for them is the other half. A new international colleague can bring energy, curiosity and fresh learning to a team, but it can also create uncertainty if people don’t understand why the recruitment is happening or what to expect. Some worry it will add to their workload through supervision, reduce opportunities for domestic staff, or change the culture; others simply feel unsure about working alongside colleagues trained in a different system.

Those concerns shouldn’t be ignored. Name them in the open and answer them honestly: why we’re recruiting internationally, what it will mean for your workload, and what it won’t mean for your own career.

Preparing the team before arrival

Explain

  • The programme & its objectives
  • The role of supervision & mentorship
  • How it complements domestic hiring

Prepare

  • The transition challenges colleagues may see
  • Unconscious bias & cultural difference
  • What good support looks like

Welcome

  • Encourage curiosity & mutual learning
  • Celebrate the experience they bring
  • New colleagues, not outsiders

International recruitment should never be presented as replacing the domestic workforce; it works alongside the investment in the people already here. When a team understands the why, they stop being bystanders and start being the people who help new colleagues succeed. The best organisations don’t treat internationally educated professionals as outsiders joining the team; they’re simply new colleagues.

One of the simplest, most effective things a leader can ask their team before new colleagues arrive is this:

“How can we make this the best possible experience for our new colleagues, and what support do you need to help make that happen?”

That small shift changes everything. International recruitment stops being something happening to the team and becomes something the team helps to shape, and colleagues who feel involved rather than merely informed are the ones who become mentors, advocates and champions.

Integration is everyone’s job

Integration can’t be delivered by HR alone. Managers, clinical leaders, educators and colleagues all shape it, and the smallest actions have the biggest impact. Introducing a new colleague to the team. Checking in after the first week. Explaining a local custom. Inviting someone to lunch. Taking the time to pronounce a name correctly. Those moments create belonging; belonging creates confidence; confidence supports retention.

Careers, not just vacancies

International recruitment should never be a temporary fix. The same development, research and leadership paths as everyone else say, more clearly than any welcome pack ever could: you belong here. Building those long-term careers is Chapter 7.

The principles behind belonging

  • Induction should reach beyond compliance and admin.
  • Clinical confidence grows through structured support, supervision and psychological safety.
  • Integration includes culture, communication and the “hidden curriculum.”
  • Consider a structured international transition curriculum for overseas-educated staff.
  • Belonging is created by the whole organisation, not just HR.
  • Onboarding was never the goal. Long careers, confident professionals and thriving communities are.
