Four weeks, in the order that actually works. Some of these tasks depend on each other, and doing them out of sequence is what costs people time.

Kettle on. A longer read — about 7 min

Four things unlock everything else

They have to happen roughly in this order, because each one is asked for by the next. Get the chain right and the first fortnight is easy. Get it wrong and you’ll spend a month being told to come back with something you don’t have yet.

1 · A New Zealand address
Everything else is asked in relation to it. A short-term rental, serviced apartment or a friend’s spare room all count — you don’t need the permanent one yet.
2 · A bank account
Open it before you fly if you can; most banks let you start the process from overseas and finish it in branch with your passport and address.
3 · An IRD number
Apply in your first few days. It needs the address and the bank account, and your employer needs it before your first pay run.
4 · A phone number
Asked for by almost every form, appointment and rental application you’ll make. A prepay SIM at the airport is enough to start.
The one that costs real money
  • Your employer will ask you to complete an IR330 tax code declaration before your first pay run. If you have no IRD number yet, you’re taxed at the no-notification rate — considerably higher than your real rate — until you produce one.
  • It’s refunded eventually, but "eventually" can mean the end of the tax year. Open the bank account before you fly, apply for the IRD number in your first few days, and this never becomes a problem.

Four weeks, roughly

Adjust for who’s arriving with you — the list changes quite a lot if there are children in it.

Week one · Land and anchor
Sleep, a SIM card, the bank account finished in branch, the IRD application in. Walk your temporary neighbourhood. Register the household with a GP near where you’ll actually live. Feels: disorienting, and faster than you expected.
Week two · Wheels and paperwork
Start the licence conversion, rent or buy a car, get an a local public transport card. First shifts and orientation. If children are with you, contact schools and check the zone for any address before you sign. Feels: busy, productive, tiring.
Week three · Viewings and the dip
Serious house-hunting, references ready, applications in the same day. Somewhere in here the adrenaline runs out. Feels: harder than week one, and completely normal.
Week four · Settle and join
Lease signed, utilities and broadband connected, KiwiSaver decided. Join one thing that isn’t work. Feels: like the beginning of an ordinary life rather than a very long trip.

Around week three, most people crash a bit

The first fortnight runs on adrenaline and admin. There’s a list, the list gets shorter, and every day contains something new. Then the list runs out, the novelty thins, and you’re left in a house you didn’t choose carefully, in a city where you know six people, doing a job in a system that does things slightly differently from the one you spent your career learning.

It usually lands somewhere between day fifteen and day twenty-five. It’s extremely common, it says nothing whatsoever about whether you made the right decision, and it passes.

What helps is knowing it’s coming. What helps more is having something in the calendar for that week that isn’t admin.

Six things that won’t be done in a month

A month is enough to be functional. It isn’t enough to be settled, and expecting otherwise is the commonest way people make the first year harder than it needs to be.

Feeling at home in the department
Three to six months before the systems, the shorthand and the referral pathways stop taking conscious effort.
A settled circle of friends
Six to twelve months, and it depends almost entirely on what you joined in month one.
Knowing the city
A year, and a few wrong turns. You’ll pick the right beach for the conditions eventually.
The right house
Most people move once. Renting first and choosing properly second is the sensible plan, not a failure.
Residency, if that’s the plan
Measured in years rather than months, and worth starting the paperwork trail early.
Not converting every price
Six months or so before you stop doing mental exchange-rate arithmetic in the supermarket.

Each of these has a chapter of its own when you need the detail: Money, tax & banking for your IRD number, first payslip and KiwiSaver; Driving & licences for converting your licence and buying a car; and Schools & education for zoning and enrolment.

The shape of the first year

The week-three dip is the first bend in a longer curve. Most people describe the same four phases on roughly the same timetable, and knowing the shape in advance takes most of the alarm out of the middle of it.

First weeks
The honeymoon
Everything is new — the scenery, the accents, the supermarket — and the adrenaline of setting up a life carries most of it. It does not stay this heightened, and that is not a loss.
First months
The dip
Once the novelty thins, the distance from home, the small frustrations and the effort of starting over tend to arrive together. It is the part nobody warns people about, it is well documented, and it says nothing about whether the decision was right. Naming it takes away most of its power.
Around six months
Finding your feet
Routines form: a GP, a favourite beach, people you would call on a bad day. Work stops taking conscious effort, the children have friends, and the place stops feeling like somewhere you are visiting.
The first year
Home
You stop converting prices, you know the back roads, and you are the one giving the next arrival directions. It rarely happens in a moment. Somewhere in the first year, it has happened.

Looking after yourself through it

Admin and friendships are only part of it. Four things people who have been through the dip tend to say they wish they had known earlier.

Homesickness is normal, not failure
Missing home does not mean the decision was wrong; it means you valued what you left. It softens as the new life fills in around it, and saying it out loud shortens the process.
Stay connected, and be here
Regular calls home help. Living entirely online keeps one foot in the old life. Plan the trips back, then let yourself be fully here in between.
Judge it at a year, not at month three
The first year has seasons, literally and emotionally. A “was this a mistake?” decision taken in the dip is taken on the worst evidence you will ever have. Most people who waited are glad they did.
Ask for help early
A GP, a community group, a colleague, or us. Reaching out early is what the people who settled did, not a sign of struggling.

The people side has a chapter of its own, finding your community, and if a partner or children are moving with you, so does moving with your family. What we do in those months is set out once, on the About page.
