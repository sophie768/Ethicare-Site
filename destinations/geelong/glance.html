Geelong at a glance

Geelong Guide · Chapter 3 of 9

Geelong is easier to understand once you see it as a bay city that turned to face the water again. Almost everything about it — the waterfront, the wind, the way the suburbs spread — follows from Corio Bay.

The shape of the place

The city centre sits at the northern edge of Corio Bay, with the Barwon River running through the southern suburbs before reaching the sea at Barwon Heads. The Bellarine Peninsula extends south-east from the city, and the You Yangs — a granite range visible from most of Geelong — rise to the north-west.

What that means in practice: the inner suburbs and the waterfront are compact and walkable, the growth corridor runs south toward Armstrong Creek, and the Bellarine towns give you the coast with a genuine commute rather than a walk.

The seasons, honestly

Geelong’s climate is temperate and coastal, with more wind than Melbourne and slightly less rain than the hills further south. The difference between a still day and a blustery one can be the difference between a beach day and an indoor one.

Summer — Dec to Feb
Warm to hot, with hot inland northerlies sometimes followed by a sharp cool change off the bay within hours.
Autumn — Mar to May
Mild and settled — arguably the best swimming and the best light on the waterfront.
Winter — Jun to Aug
Cool and wet, with Bass Strait weather making itself felt. The wind is at its most noticeable.
Spring — Sep to Nov
Changeable and windy, with wildflowers on the You Yangs and the first warm days on the coast.

How the city got here

Long before
Wadawurrung Country
Corio Bay and the surrounding plains have been lived on and managed by the Wadawurrung people, part of the Kulin nation, for tens of thousands of years.
1830s
A port on the bay
European settlement followed the sheltered anchorage, exporting wool from the Western District’s sheep runs.
19th century
The wool boom
Geelong grew wealthy enough to build the grand civic architecture that still defines the city centre.
20th century
A manufacturing city
Car assembly, an aluminium smelter and an oil refinery all operated here in turn, making Geelong one of Victoria’s great industrial centres.
Today
A city that turned back to the water
All three heavy industries have closed within the last fifteen years. The waterfront has been rebuilt around people, two state agencies relocated their head offices here, and the local health and university sectors have grown to fill the gap.

What is here that you would not expect

  • Victoria’s only tertiary hospital outside Melbourne
  • Two Deakin University campuses, one on the waterfront
  • The National Wool Museum, in a former woolstore
  • A working seaport still handling grain and fuel
  • One of the AFL’s most fiercely supported clubs
  • A heritage carousel and painted bollards along the promenade
  • Cool-climate wine country twenty minutes from the CBD
  • The start of the Great Ocean Road at the edge of the municipality
Orientation, in one line

A bay city that built its wealth on wool, spent a century on heavy industry, and has spent the last fifteen years turning back to the water. Work out whether you want the inner city, the growth corridor or the Bellarine, and the rest of the map follows.
