Mental health & wellbeing

Only one of these three is a registered profession in Australia. That single fact reorganises everything else.

Where you fit

Psychology is registered, through the Psychology Board of Australia under Ahpra, with a defined route for internationally qualified applicants. Psychotherapy and social work are not registered professions in Australia at all.

Unregistered does not mean unrecruited — employers set their own requirements, and professional membership often carries the weight a register would. But it changes entirely who decides whether you are eligible, and what you can rely on before you arrive.

  • Psychologists→Registered with the Psychology Board of Australia. Provisional registration, a supervised program and a national exam sit between most overseas psychologists and general registration.
  • Psychotherapists→Not a registered profession in Australia. Eligibility is set by employers and professional bodies rather than by a board.
  • Social workers→Not registered either. The Australian Association of Social Workers assesses qualifications, including for migration purposes.
  • Another mental health role→Mental health nursing, counselling and specialist roles. Tell us what you hold and we will tell you which system you are in.

Where might that team work?

Mental health careers here do not all sit inside a “mental health department”, and public specialist services are only part of the picture. A psychologist might be working in rehabilitation, persistent pain, workplace health or private practice; the mix of professions changes with the setting and with who is paying for it.

Public services are run state by state rather than nationally, so two jobs with the same title in Perth and in Hobart genuinely differ — in structure, in caseload and in pay. What it is like to practise here more broadly — the funders, the priorities and cultural safety — sits in practising in Australia.

Adult community mental health

State-run community teams carrying assessment, treatment and continuity for a defined population — the backbone of the public system, and the setting where the multidisciplinary team is most visible. Caseloads, team structure and the balance between crisis and continuing care vary considerably between states and between metropolitan and regional services, which is worth asking about rather than assuming.

Often in the team: psychiatry · nursing · psychology · occupational therapy · social work · peer workers

Acute & inpatient

Inpatient units, emergency mental health and crisis teams, with an intensity closer to what most overseas clinicians already know.

Children & young people

Child and adolescent services alongside a well-developed national youth mental health sector, with family and school around every case.

Older persons & cognitive health

Later-life depression, anxiety and psychosis through to cognitive assessment, dementia and capacity — where physical health, cognition and mental health overlap.

Psychiatry · psychology · nursing · occupational therapy · social work · geriatric medicine

Forensic mental health

Court liaison, prison in-reach and secure services, at the interface of health and justice, and organised state by state.

Eating disorders

Specialist multidisciplinary work spanning psychological, psychiatric, dietetic and medical care.

Psychiatry · psychology · nursing · dietetics · medicine

Alcohol & other drug services

Community treatment, co-occurring mental health and substance use, harm reduction and recovery-focused work, much of it delivered by community-managed organisations.

Private practice

Independent or group practice on Medicare-subsidised referrals and private fees — a different model around caseload, referrals, autonomy and income, and the setting a large share of Australian psychologists work in.

Psychosocial & disability support

Work funded through the disability scheme rather than through health, focused on function, capacity building and daily living rather than episodic treatment.

Rural, remote & telehealth

Small teams over very large areas, generalist scope, and telehealth as core practice rather than an add-on. Often the most autonomous work available, and the most isolated.

Workplace wellbeing & EAP

Employee assistance, workplace mental health, occupational rehabilitation and organisational wellbeing — employer-funded, and a very different working day.

Aboriginal & Torres Strait Islander services

Community-controlled organisations and social and emotional wellbeing teams delivering culturally grounded care, usually with family and community at the centre of the model rather than at its edge.

Public specialist services

Large multidisciplinary teams, defined referral pathways, enterprise-agreement pay set by your state, and the full weight of the system around complex care.

Private, community-managed & independent

Smaller teams or full autonomy, different referral routes and funding, and often a caseload you shape yourself. Public or private is not the same job with a different employer — it changes your caseload, your team, your appointment structure and what a working week looks like.

Working as part of a wider system of care

Mental health rarely sits in one room. The clinical team is the centre of the picture, not the whole of it — and in Australia there is a fourth ring most systems do not have, because four different funders buy mental health care and each of them buys a different kind of job.

Imagine someone with a serious mental illness who is also struggling to keep their tenancy and their job. A psychiatrist manages diagnosis and treatment; a nurse coordinates ongoing care; a psychologist provides intervention; an occupational therapist works on daily functioning and return to meaningful activity; a social worker addresses housing, income and family circumstances. Around them sit a GP, a housing provider, an employment service, an alcohol and drug service, a peer worker, and the person’s family and carers.

The job isn’t only what happens in the consulting room.

The difference that reorganises the map

In Australia, the funder decides the door a person comes through — and therefore what your job is. Medicare-subsidised sessions in private practice, a state-run community mental health team, NDIS-funded psychosocial support and an employer-funded workplace service are four different working weeks under the same professional title.

It also means the same qualification can be worth a different amount depending on which funder you sit under, and that provider eligibility can matter as much as registration. Worth understanding before you choose between two offers, not after.

What applies across mental health and wellbeing

The things that are the same across these three — starting with the one that is not.

Who regulates this work?

One of the three, and only one.

Psychologists register with the Psychology Board of Australia through Ahpra. National registration, and you cannot use the title without it.

Psychotherapy and social work are not regulated in Australia. There is no board and no register; eligibility is a matter for employers and for professional bodies such as the Australian Association of Social Workers, which also assesses qualifications for migration.

How registration works in Australia · Check your pathway

What is the psychology route, in order?

Four steps, and they run in sequence rather than in parallel.

1. Provisional registration with the Board. 2. A Board-approved transitional program — three continuous months, at least 17.5 hours a week, under an approved supervision plan. 3. The national psychology exam, which may be sat during or after the program. 4. General registration.

Verified 26 August 2026 against psychologyboard.gov.au. Check your own case before you plan around it.

Is endorsement the same as being a specialist?

No. Australia has areas of practice endorsement rather than specialist registration — clinical, neuropsychology, forensic and the rest. An endorsement is added to general registration; it is not a separate register and it is not a specialist title in the way medicine uses the word.

Worth being precise about, because the wording on a job advert often is not.

Is the professional association the same as the regulator?

No, and this is the distinction that costs people time. Ahpra and the Psychology Board decide whether you may practise. The Australian Psychological Society assesses qualifications for migration purposes. Two organisations, two questions, and neither answer implies the other.

What does the pay look like?

Public-sector pay runs on state and territory enterprise agreements, so the same job genuinely pays differently in Perth and in Hobart — there is no single national scale the way there is in New Zealand. Private practice sits outside those agreements again.

Superannuation is paid on top of base salary and is a real part of the package rather than a footnote. Pay in Australia

Which visa would I need?

Most clinicians arrive on either an employer-sponsored visa or a skilled visa. Which route suits you depends less on your profession than on your age, your family and whether you want permanent residence from the start.

The visa can usually run alongside registration rather than after it. Visas and residence

What about the rest of the move?

Schools, housing, healthcare, banking, shipping, pets and the first few weeks are the same whichever profession you hold, so we explain them once rather than on each page.
