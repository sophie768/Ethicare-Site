Medical imaging · Australia

Imaging careers, with a country to explore.

Two regulators, not one.

Radiographers, radiation therapists and nuclear medicine technologists register nationally through Ahpra. Sonographers take a different route entirely — and knowing which is yours before you start saves months.

Find your imaging pathway →Talk to the team

Where you fit

Australia runs a large public system alongside one of the biggest private imaging markets in the world, and it does not train enough people to staff either. Internationally qualified clinicians are a permanent part of how imaging works here, not a gap-filler.

The important difference from New Zealand: three of these professions register with Ahpra, and one does not register at all — sonography is accredited instead. Start on the wrong route and you lose time you cannot get back.

  • Sonographers→General, obstetric and cardiac scanning, with autonomous reporting and a private market that pays for it. Accredited through ASMIRT and ASAR — not an Ahpra-registered profession.
  • Radiographers→Plain film, CT, MRI, mammography, theatre and interventional work across public and private services. Registered with the Medical Radiation Practice Board through Ahpra.
    Also searched asMammographerCT radiographerMRI radiographerInterventional radiographer
  • Radiation therapists→Planning and delivering treatment across public cancer centres and a growing private network. Same board, same Ahpra application as radiography.
  • Nuclear medicine technologists→SPECT/CT and PET/CT practice, with theranostics expanding fast. Ahpra registration plus an ANZSNM skills assessment.

Mammography, CT and MRI are not separate professions

They are scopes within radiography, and one Ahpra registration covers all of them — you do not apply again to move between them. Australian employers do advertise by modality, so we have named them above because that is how people search. The route in is the radiographer route.

Looking for radiology rather than imaging?

Consultant radiologists are doctors, with a different regulator and a different assessment again — specialist registration with the Medical Board of Australia, and a comparability assessment by RANZCR. We recruit them into Australia too: see consultant radiologists.

Where your work sits in someone’s diagnosis

Imaging is almost never the destination. It is the step where another specialty’s uncertainty turns into a decision — which is why an imaging department is the busiest crossroads in most hospitals, and why the professions in it work to somebody else’s clock as well as their own.

Most of the family is invisible in that sentence. One examination can involve a radiographer or sonographer, a radiologist, a medical physicist, a nurse, the person who booked it and the clinician who acts on it. The patient meets perhaps two of them.

Worth knowing before you search

In Australia the funder shapes the job as much as the employer does. A large share of imaging happens in private practice on Medicare-funded referrals rather than inside a public hospital — so “radiographer” can mean an acute, unselected hospital week or a booked outpatient list in a community clinic, at different pay under a different agreement.

Your modality matters just as much. Plain film, CT, MRI, mammography and theatre work all sit inside one scope of practice, and they are four or five genuinely different working days. When a vacancy names a title, the useful questions are which rooms, which case mix, who funds it and what the after-hours commitment is.

What could your career look like here?

Six routes cover most of what an internationally trained clinician actually does here. The state you land in shapes the agreement and the rota; the route you take shapes the week.

Sonography sits outside Ahpra, so for one of these professions the starting point is ASAR rather than a national board — worth settling before you plan anything else.

General diagnostic imaging

The widest door, and where most people start. In a public department that means plain film, fluoroscopy, ultrasound and cross-sectional work in the same week, with the emergency department and theatre lists pulling on the same rooms — run by state and territory health services, so your enterprise agreement, rota and progression are set where you land rather than federally. Private and community imaging is the other half of the picture and a very large employer here: referred outpatient work on Medicare-funded requests, booked lists, a great deal of musculoskeletal imaging, and often the newest equipment.

Often in the department: radiography · sonography · radiology · nursing · medical physics · assistants and administration

CT & MRI

Deeper work in one place rather than breadth across many, and the modalities employers ask about most often. Cross-sectional experience gained overseas usually transfers well; smaller sites may expect you to cover both rather than specialise in one.

Breast screening & mammography

National population screening is a defined, high-volume workflow with its own quality requirements and its own mobile services — a genuinely different day from diagnostic work. It carries its own training expectations, so check what will be recognised before you plan around it.

Nuclear medicine & PET

SPECT/CT, PET/CT and therapeutic work, concentrated in fewer centres. Radiation licensing is handled state by state on top of national registration, which is an extra step rather than a barrier — but one that takes time.

Interventional & theatre

Image-guided procedures, angiography and hybrid theatre work, where imaging is part of the intervention rather than a step before it. Closer to the patient and to the surgical team, with on-call attached in most services.

Radiation therapy

Its own profession and its own route, not a corner of diagnostic imaging. Treatment is delivered from designated centres, public and private, rather than from every hospital — so the job comes with a location decision attached — and the work is planned, longitudinal and spent with the same patients over weeks.

Radiation therapy · medical physics · radiation oncology · nursing

Rural, remote & regional

This one cuts across all six. Broader practice, more autonomy and often a closer-knit team — you are more likely to be the person on site than one of several, and it is frequently the best-paid version of the job. The trade-off can be distance, fly-in services, on-call and fewer specialist services nearby, which are things worth understanding before choosing where to live. Some of this work sits in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander community-controlled services, where imaging is one part of a wider model of care and cultural safety is part of the clinical standard rather than an induction module.

Public hospital services

Acute and unselected case mix, the widest range of equipment, an on-call commitment, and pay set by the enterprise agreement of the state you work in.

Private & Medicare-funded practice

Booked lists, referred outpatients, commercial throughput and often better equipment for a narrower range of work. Public or private is not the same job with a different employer — it changes your case mix, your hours, your autonomy and what a week feels like.

What applies across medical imaging & radiation therapy

The things that are the same whichever of these professions you hold — and the one thing that is not.

Who regulates this work?

Two different answers, which is the single most useful thing to know before you start.

Radiographers, radiation therapists and nuclear medicine technologists register with the Medical Radiation Practice Board of Australia through Ahpra — one board, one application, three divisions of practice.

Sonographers do not register with Ahpra at all. Sonography is not a regulated profession in Australia; you are accredited, through ASMIRT recognition of your qualification and then entry to the ASAR register. Different body, different evidence, different timeline.

What does the pay look like?

Public-sector pay runs on state and territory enterprise agreements, so the same job genuinely pays differently in Perth and in Hobart — there is no single national scale the way there is in New Zealand. Private practice sits outside those agreements again.

Superannuation is paid on top of base salary, and it is a real part of the package rather than a footnote.

Which visa would I need?

Most clinicians arrive on either an employer-sponsored visa or a skilled visa, and all four of these professions sit on the skilled occupation lists. Which route is better depends less on your profession than on your age, your family and whether you want permanent residence from the start.

The visa can usually run alongside registration or accreditation rather than after it. Visas and residence

Does state or territory matter?

For registration and accreditation, no — both are national and travel with you. For pay, rostering, and how quickly you get onto a modality, yes: those are set locally, and the difference between states is larger than most people expect.
