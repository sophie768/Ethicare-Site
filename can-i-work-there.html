Registration

Can I work there?

It is the question almost everyone asks first, and the one people put off answering — because it looks like paperwork and feels like judgement on a career you have already built. We understand that. The shape of it: a Board looks at your qualification and your practice and decides whether they count. That answer shapes the visa, and the visa shapes when you could realistically fly. Which means the rest of the move is waiting on this, and getting it answered early is a kindness to yourself.

Your answer

Ten minutes, and your route has a name

The checker asks what the Board asks — where you trained, what you are registered as now, how recently you have practised — and then tell you which pathway you would most likely be assessed under, what it costs and roughly how long it tends to take. Nothing is submitted. No fee is paid to find out. If the answer is not what you hoped, you will at least know that today rather than in four months.

Where people get stuck

Registration is more than your qualification

It is rarely the assessment of your training that holds people up. Every regulator also has to satisfy itself about your standing, your English, your character and your health — and it is these, the slow ordinary ones, that decide whether registration takes four months or nine. None is difficult. They simply take longer than anyone expects, so it helps to know now.

Certificates of good standing

You will need one from every regulator you have practised under — in New Zealand, from the last five years or since you last practised there, whichever is shorter. The authority sends it directly to the Council rather than to you, and it expires: the Medical Council wants every certificate dated within three months of the day you start work. Ask too early and it lapses; too late and everything else waits on it. There is a window, and your checklist marks it.

The English-language rule

This one surprises people who have practised in English for twenty years. It turns on where you trained and were assessed rather than on nationality, and each Board decides for itself what counts as evidence and for how long. In Australia it is one of the five registration standards every Board applies, with nursing and midwifery the exception. Worth settling before any fee is paid.

Character, and a criminal-history check

Ahpra requires an international criminal history check from one of two approved suppliers if you have been primarily based outside Australia for six consecutive months or longer as an adult. You pay for it, the supplier sends the result to Ahpra directly, and it is valid for three months. New Zealand works by declaration instead: any conviction carrying three months’ imprisonment or more, and any investigation or disciplinary proceeding anywhere in the world, current or past.

Health, and fitness to practise

Both countries ask whether anything about your physical or mental health could affect your ability to practise. It is a declaration, not an examination, and a condition that is managed is not a bar — what causes trouble is a gap between what you declare and what a referee or a previous regulator says. Declaring something early is almost always easier than explaining it later.

Recency, CPD and indemnity

Australia’s other three registration standards, set Board by Board rather than nationally: enough recent practice, continuing professional development you can evidence, and professional indemnity insurance in place. If you have had a career break, recency is the one to check first, because it is the one with no quick fix.

References that answer the question

A thin reference is the most common reason a Board comes back with questions, and it is almost never the referee’s fault — they simply were not told what was being looked for. A short conversation with them beforehand changes what they write.

And one your employer asks, not the regulator

Immunity and a pre-employment health declaration — measles, hepatitis B, the rest — are a condition of the job rather than of registration, handled by the employer’s occupational health service after an offer. People routinely confuse the two and chase records they do not yet need. Worth gathering anyway, because they are far easier to obtain before you leave than afterwards.

Which of these bites, and how hard, is our experience rather than a rule, and we try to keep the two clearly apart. The Board’s own document list, linked from every guide and checklist, is what governs your application. The specifics above were checked against the Medical Council of New Zealand and Ahpra on 28 September 2026 — both change them, so check the date before you rely on it.
