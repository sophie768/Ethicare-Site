Before anything else

It shouldn’t start with a vacancy

Better work-life balance, beautiful beaches, more space, an outdoor lifestyle. You have seen it on LinkedIn and in recruitment adverts, and much of it is true.

But when you are thinking about moving your life — and possibly your family’s — to the other side of the world, there is more you probably want to know. What do the healthcare professionals who made the move love? What surprised them? And what do they miss, six months or ten years later?

Not to persuade you to go, and not to put you off. Deciding whether to move shouldn’t start with a vacancy. It should start with whether you can see a life there.

You don’t need to have decided anything yet — not the country, not the timing, not whether you are going at all. This is exploration. Every other part of the site is open without it, and this page is here whenever the question comes back.

Section 01 · The reasons

Why people go

Sometimes it is work. More often, work is what makes the move possible. And for many people life at home is good — they are simply curious what another version of it could look like.

Time

More life around the working day

Finishing a shift with daylight left. Weekends that are not spent recovering. Wanting work to take up a smaller share of life.

Work

A different working life

Another health system, other ways of working, a specialist opening, or simply a change. Not easier by definition — different.

Family

Children who grow up outside

Space, schooling, and wanting children to know another country while they are young enough for it to become theirs.

Money

Earning potential

Real in some Australian professions and places. The headline salary is only part of it — Make the numbers work is where that gets tested.

Space

Room to live

A garden. A shorter drive to work. Coast or hills as part of an ordinary week rather than a holiday. Not everyone gets a house by the beach.

Adventure

Doing something extraordinary

Some people simply want to, while they can. Wonder about it for ten years and there comes a point where you either do it or accept you probably never will.

The system

Tired of the one you are in

Some people are: the workload, the waits, the pay conversation, a service that runs on goodwill. A real reason. A move changes a great deal and does not fix what would travel with you — and both these systems are under pressure too. Different pressure, not none.

Precedent

Someone you know has done it

A friend, a colleague, a cousin already there. Often the thing that turns an idea into something that feels possible.

Section 02 · Not one destination

Two countries, two different offers

Australia and New Zealand are not one place, and neither is one place inside itself.

New Zealand

Pace, nature, scale

Smaller communities, the outdoors close to ordinary life, and a different scale of living. People come for family life and adventure more than for earning potential.

Auckland, Wellington, Christchurch, Palmerston North and rural New Zealand are very different lives.

Explore New Zealand →
Australia

Warmth, size, opportunity

A warmer climate, larger cities, a bigger health system and job market, long-established migrant communities, and stronger earning potential in some professions.

Perth, Melbourne, Hobart, Bundaberg and regional Western Australia are not interchangeable.

Explore Australia →

Section 03 · After the move

What people say they love

It is rarely the dramatic things that make people stay. Mountains on the drive to work are wonderful. Everyday life matters more.

  • The children being outside — after school, not just on holiday.
  • The barbecue that isn’t saved for the one sunny Saturday in August.
  • A walk after work, in daylight.
  • A beach the tourists don’t know about.
  • Room for a dog.
  • Being known in a smaller community.
  • In parts of Australia, how much of life happens outdoors.
  • In New Zealand, how quickly you are out of the city and into coast, bush or hills.
  • A shorter, calmer commute — for some people, in some places.
  • The first Christmas on a beach. A child’s accent you weren’t expecting.

Eventually the extraordinary becomes ordinary. People tell us that is when somewhere starts to feel like home.

What is here is what the people we have placed tell us. Named stories appear as people agree to them — Megan’s, from Manchester to the Sunshine Coast, is the first.

Section 04 · The other side

The things nobody puts in the brochure

Moving across the world can give you an extraordinary new life. It also means leaving parts of your old one behind. Knowing that first is not negativity; it is how the decision gets made with your eyes open.

Real story · what you leave

“Of course, we miss our loved ones and the sunny South African weather, so we feel very fortunate that both of our parents were able to come and visit us here.”

Janita, nuclear medicine technologist, Auckland. The airport, January 2024 — both sets of parents have since been out to visit.

What the move was like →

Written first for people leaving the UK, because that is who most of our candidates are today. Coming from South Africa, Ireland, India or the Philippines? What you would gain and miss is different, and we would rather write it with people who have done it. Tell us what you would want here.

The ordinary choice

Food, and streets that change every few blocks

Chinese, Indian, Turkish, Caribbean or Vietnamese within a walk; the family-run place you have been going to for years. Both countries are multicultural, most of all in the big cities — a smaller city or regional town will feel the difference.

Old things

Terraces, stone walls, the pub

Victorian brick, villages where none of the houses line up, a pub that has been there for 300 years. And the pub itself — the local, the fire, the roast — is its own culture. Both countries have pubs; they are not that.

Europe

Another country two hours away

Lisbon for a long weekend, Paris by train, the Alps. Even if you rarely went, you could. From here the near world is the Pacific and Asia, and Britain is a day’s flying away.

Culture and choice

The exhibition, the theatre, the tour

Melbourne and Sydney are major cultural cities and New Zealand has real arts and music scenes — but moving from a global city to a smaller centre is still a trade. If this is part of what makes you happy, it is not superficial.

Seasons

A crisp October morning

The first cold day, autumn leaves, long light evenings. Christmas at 28 degrees delights some people and never feels right to others. New Zealand and southern Australia have real seasons; the tropical north does not. “Better weather” isn’t the question — what weather makes you happy is.

Familiarity

Knowing how things work

Which supermarket, which form, which queue. For the first while, nothing is instinctive, and that is tiring in a way people don’t expect. It passes; it is worth knowing it is coming.

Section 05 · The real trade-off

How does being this far from home feel to you?

Food can be found and you can travel for a concert. Family is harder. You will not be there for everything: parents get older, friends have babies, someone you love has a terrible week and you cannot drive round with a cup of tea.

Worth thinking through now rather than after:

  • A full day in the air each way, and what the flights cost for a family.
  • How much of your annual leave a visit home uses.
  • Long-haul with small children.
  • Ageing parents, and what an emergency would look like from there.
  • Keeping relationships alive across a time difference that turns your evening into their morning.
  • How often you could realistically afford to go back.

Video calls help enormously. They are not the same as being there. For some people the life they gain makes the distance worthwhile; for others it eventually brings them home. Neither means the move failed.

The cost of visits belongs with everything else that has a number — Make the numbers work.
