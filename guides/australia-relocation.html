The practical turn

From decision to departure

Reviewed July 2026 · general guidance only. Rules on visas, licences, pets and tax differ by state and change over time, check the official sources linked below for your own circumstances.

By now the big questions are answered: the life, the role, the registration, the visa. What is left is logistics, and logistics reward lead time — the slow items first, one category at a time.

This is the step where people either feel organised or overwhelmed, and the difference is almost never how capable they are. It’s whether the long-lead items. Pets, freight, school places, a rental, were started early enough that they weren’t all happening in the last fortnight.

Every region is a little different on housing, schools and the cost of getting set up, which is why we’ll point you to a relocation guide for your state as those are published. This step is the national overview that sits behind them.

Order of operations

A realistic countdown

Not a rigid schedule. Visas and registration set their own pace. But this is the sequence that works, and knowing what belongs where takes most of the panic out of it.

3–6 mo

Start the slow things

Registration and visa are already running. Alongside them: begin the pet import process if you’re bringing an animal, it is the longest lead time of anything on this list and cannot be rushed. Get quotes for freight. Order official copies of documents you’ll need: birth and marriage certificates, qualifications, children’s school reports and immunisation records, driving licences and history, references.

If you rent, check your notice period. If you own, start the conversation with an agent about selling or letting.

6–8 wk

Money, home and school

Open an Australian bank account, many banks allow this from overseas. Book short-term accommodation for landing (four to six weeks is a sensible window). Start contacting schools about places and enrolment. Book freight if you’re shipping. Confirm what health cover you need in place on arrival.

Tell utilities, insurers, banks and your GP at home what’s happening, and get copies of your medical and dental records for everyone in the family.

2–4 wk

Wind down and confirm

Book flights once your visa is granted, not before. Confirm your first day with your employer and what they need from you. Cancel or transfer subscriptions, memberships and insurance. Arrange mail redirection. Sort a plan for money in the first month: cleared funds in your Australian account before you land is the goal.

Final wk

Hand luggage matters more than you think

Passports and visa evidence, all original certificates, medical summaries and prescriptions, your children’s immunisation records, employer contact details, accommodation details, and enough medication for the transition. Everything else can be replaced; these can’t easily be.

Week 1

Land, then admin

Local SIM, activate the bank account, apply for a Tax File Number, enrol in Medicare if you’re eligible, register the family with a GP, and start the rental search in person. Also: find your nearest emergency department and check how ambulance works in your state. Then stop and go and look at something beautiful, you’ve earned it.

Get the admin moving

Money, banking & the essentials

A few accounts and numbers make everything else possible, and several can be started before you land.

Banking

Open an account early

Many Australian banks let you open an account from overseas weeks before you arrive, then activate it with your passport once you’re here. Your salary and rent then have somewhere to land from day one.

Tax

A Tax File Number

Apply for a TFN once you arrive. It’s needed for pay and tax. Without it you’re taxed at the top rate and have to claim the difference back, so it’s a first-week job, not a first-month one.

Super

Superannuation

Employers pay compulsory superannuation on top of your wage. Retirement savings that are yours. You’ll nominate a fund or be assigned one when you start. Worth understanding rather than ignoring; it’s real money.

Three things people wish they’d known

Moving money costs more than you expect. High-street bank transfer rates are rarely the best available, and on the sums involved in a relocation the difference is meaningful. Compare a specialist transfer service, and split the transfer rather than moving everything on one day’s exchange rate.

Your credit history doesn’t travel. However established you are at home, you arrive with no Australian credit file. That affects phone contracts, car finance and sometimes rental applications. It builds quickly, but the first few months can feel oddly like being twenty-two again.

Mind the gap before your first pay. Between landing costs and your first Australian pay cycle there can be four to six weeks of outgoings with nothing coming in. That gap, plus a rental bond and several weeks’ rent up front, is the single most common cause of relocation stress we see. Plan the buffer properly and almost everything else is manageable.

Health cover from the start

Medicare & health insurance

How you access healthcare depends on your visa and your passport, so it’s worth sorting before you need a GP, not after.

Medicare

Australia’s public scheme. Citizens and permanent residents enrol. Many temporary visa holders qualify through one of Australia’s eleven reciprocal health care agreements, enrolment happens after you arrive, with passports, visa evidence and an Australian address.

Private & visa health cover

Many temporary work visas require you to hold and maintain adequate cover for the whole stay. Medicare-eligible families often take cover anyway, for shorter elective waits and extras toward dental and optical. Check the waiting periods before you choose a policy.

Records & prescriptions

Bring a written summary of everyone’s medical and dental history and current prescriptions, plus immunisation records for the children (schools ask). Check your medicines against the PBS before you travel, brand names differ.

The full picture. Eligibility, bulk billing, the PBS, ambulance by state and what Medicare doesn’t cover, is in Understanding healthcare in Australia. It’s worth twenty minutes before you accept an offer.

Where you’ll land

Finding somewhere to live

The decision people most want to get right, and the one most likely to be rushed. Renting first is almost always the answer.

Sequence

Land, then choose

Book short-term accommodation for four to six weeks and house-hunt on the ground. Photographs flatter, commutes deceive, and a suburb that looks right on a map can feel wrong in person. Almost nobody regrets renting first; plenty regret signing early.

Applying

Come prepared to compete

Rental markets in the major cities are tight, and viewings can be brief and busy. Have a folder ready: identification, your employment contract or offer letter, references from previous landlords or agents, and bank statements. Applying the same day you view is normal.

Money up front

Bond plus rent in advance

Expect to pay a bond (held, and returned at the end if all is well) plus rent in advance. Rent here is usually quoted and paid weekly or fortnightly rather than monthly, which catches people out when they budget. Ask exactly what’s due before you sign.

The no-rental-history problem. With no Australian tenancy record and no local credit file, good applicants can still be passed over. Two things help more than anything: a letter from your employer confirming the role and salary, and written references from previous landlords or agents with contactable details. Have both before you land.

Furnished or not. Most long-term rentals are unfurnished, and often without white goods. If your freight is weeks behind you, plan for a period of camping in your own home, or budget for second-hand basics, a very well-served market here.

For the children

Schools & childcare

The order matters: for public schools, where you live usually determines where they go. So the suburb decision and the school decision are the same decision.

1

Enrolment zones come first

Public schools generally enrol from a defined local catchment. Confirm the zone before you commit to a lease, not after, and confirm it with the school or the state education department rather than a property listing.

2

Non-government schools apply differently

Independent and faith-based schools enrol on their own terms, often with waiting lists and fees. If that’s your preference, start the conversation months out.

3

Fees can apply on temporary visas

Public schooling is generally free for citizens and permanent residents. Some states charge fees for children of temporary visa holders, and the rules differ state by state. Check yours specifically, it can be a significant annual figure.

4

The school year runs late January to December

Four terms, with the long summer holiday over Christmas and January. If your children are mid-year in the northern hemisphere, they may join part-way through a year, or be placed by age rather than by year completed. Schools handle this constantly and handle it well.

5

Bring the paperwork

Immunisation records, recent school reports, any assessments or learning-support documentation, and birth certificates. Having these ready shortens enrolment from weeks to days.

6

Childcare needs booking early

Long day care and preschool places in popular areas have genuine waiting lists, and subsidy eligibility depends on your visa. If you need childcare to start work, treat it as a long-lead item like the pet, not a first-week task.

More detail on year levels, choosing between systems and how the school day feels is in Education & schools in Australia.

The physical move

Shipping, pets & what to leave behind

Two long-lead items and one liberating realisation: you probably need less than you think.

Freight takes months, not weeks

Sea freight to Australia is measured in weeks at sea plus port and customs time at both ends — plan for a long gap between your things leaving and your things arriving. Air freight a small “first month” box if the budget allows. Get several quotes and ask specifically what customs, quarantine inspection and delivery are included.

Australia’s biosecurity is strict

Strict, and enforced. Wooden items, cane and bamboo, garden tools, footwear with soil, seeds, food and plant material all attract attention and can be treated or destroyed at your cost. Clean everything thoroughly, declare honestly, and don’t try to bring food. This is not a formality.

Pets: start now, and get help

Bringing a cat or dog involves permits, vaccinations, blood tests with waiting periods, an approved route and post-arrival quarantine. It runs to many months and is expensive. It’s entirely doable and thousands of families do it, but it must be the first thing you start, and most people use a specialist agent.

What’s worth shipping. Almost everyone over-ships on the first move. Furniture rarely justifies the freight; the sentimental, the irreplaceable and the good quality do. Electrical goods are worth checking against Australian plugs and voltage before you pay to move them.

What to sell or store. Storing at home for a year sounds prudent and often turns into storing for five. If you’re unsure whether the move is permanent, store the small and sentimental and sell the bulky, it’s cheaper to buy a sofa twice than to store one for three years.

Getting around

Licences, cars & distance

Driving licences are handled by each state and territory, not nationally — so the answer to “can I drive on my licence?” depends on where you land.

Your licence

Usually usable, then converted

Most overseas licences can be used for a period after you arrive, and many can be converted without a full test. Timeframes and requirements vary by state, and some licences need a translation. Check your state’s transport authority early. The grace period is shorter than people assume.

Buying a car

Expect to need one

Public transport is good in the inner cities and thin beyond them. Most families end up with a car, sometimes two. With no local credit history, finance can be harder at first, many newcomers buy modestly for cash and upgrade in year two.

Distance

It’s bigger than the map suggests

Australia is vast, and a drive that looks like an afternoon can be a full day. It shapes weekends, commutes and how often you’ll realistically see friends in another city. Worth factoring into where you choose to live.

Landing well

What the first few weeks look like

Busy but exciting. A flurry of small set-up tasks against the backdrop of a brand-new place.

Expect the early days to go on the practical foundations: activating your bank account, applying for your Tax File Number, local SIM cards, enrolling in Medicare, registering with a GP, enrolling the children, starting the rental search, and finding your way around a neighbourhood where you don’t yet know which supermarket is the good one.

Two honest notes. First, the admin bunches: much of it can only be done once you’re here, so week one feels front-loaded. Second, the emotional shape of it surprises people. The first fortnight is often adrenaline; weeks four to eight are frequently the hardest, when the novelty thins and you’re tired and the people who know you well are asleep when you’re awake. That’s not a sign the decision was wrong. It’s the ordinary middle, and it lifts.

Told straight

The money to plan for

Costs differ enormously between a capital city and a regional town, so we do not print figures that would be out of date by the time you read them. The list itself is remarkably consistent.

Before you go: document certification and official copies, English testing if required, registration and assessment fees, visa application charges for the whole family, health and police checks, pet import, freight, and the flights themselves.

Landing: short-term accommodation for four to six weeks, rental bond plus rent in advance, basic furniture and white goods, a car, local SIMs and internet connection, school uniforms and supplies, and any state school fees that apply to temporary visa holders.

Ongoing, and easy to forget: health cover if your visa requires it, ambulance cover in some states, dental for the family, car insurance and registration, and the flights home you’ll want to take more often than you planned.

Ask your employer directly what relocation support is offered. Some roles include a contribution to flights, freight or initial accommodation, and some don’t, but it is a fair, normal question and it is far better asked before you accept than after. We’ll help you ask it well.

Told straight

What we’d rather you heard from us

Six things that reliably catch people out. None are dealbreakers; all are easier known early.

1

Don’t book flights before the visa is granted

Not the application, the grant. Changeable fares cost a little more and save a great deal of grief.

2

Don’t resign until both are real

Offer and visa. We say this on the interview page too, and we mean it just as much here.

3

Pets and childcare are the long poles

Months, not weeks. If either applies to you, start before anything else on this page.

4

The first-pay gap is the real budget risk

Landing costs, bond and rent in advance all land before your first Australian pay does. Build the buffer.

5

State rules differ, genuinely

Licences, ambulance, school fees for temporary visa holders. Advice from a friend in another state may not apply to you.

6

We’re not tax or migration advisers

On pensions, savings transfers, tax residency and visas, we’ll tell you what to ask and who to ask. The answers need a licensed professional.

Common questions

The things people ask us first

Should I buy or rent when I arrive?

Almost everyone rents first, and we’d encourage it. It lets you learn an area, settle the children and start work before committing to a suburb or a mortgage. Arrange short-term accommodation for four to six weeks, then take your time. Very few people regret renting first; plenty regret buying in month two.

How far ahead should I start all this?

Three to six months for the slow items, pets above all, then freight quotes, official document copies and school conversations. Six to eight weeks for banking, accommodation and confirming health cover. Flights only once the visa is granted. If you’re bringing an animal or need childcare to start work, start those before anything else on the list.

Can I drive on my current licence?

Usually for a period after you arrive, and many overseas licences convert without a full test. The grace period and the requirements vary by state and territory, and some licences need a certified translation. Check your destination’s transport authority early. People routinely assume they have longer than they do. Bring your licence history or an official driving record if you can get one.

How much should I budget to get set up?

It varies enormously. Landing in Sydney or Melbourne costs meaningfully more than a regional centre, so we won’t print a figure that would mislead you. Plan against the list instead: short-term accommodation, rental bond plus rent in advance, basic furniture, a car, connections and school costs, all before your first Australian pay arrives. That gap is the real risk. And ask your employer what relocation support is offered.

Can I bring my pet?

Yes, and many families do, but it’s the longest and most expensive item on this page. Permits, vaccinations, blood tests with mandatory waiting periods, an approved route and post-arrival quarantine add up to many months. Start it before anything else, use the Department of Agriculture’s own guidance as your source, and consider a specialist agent. Do not leave it until the visa is granted.

What can’t I bring into Australia?

Biosecurity here is strict and enforced. Food, seeds, plant material, soil on shoes or garden tools, wooden and cane items all attract inspection and may be treated or destroyed at your cost. Clean everything, declare everything, and check the biosecurity site before you pack. Declaring something is never penalised; failing to declare it is.

Will my children get a free school place?

Public schooling is generally free for citizens and permanent residents. Several states charge fees for children of temporary visa holders, and the amounts differ by state, so check yours specifically before you budget. Public places are usually tied to your address, so confirm the enrolment zone before signing a lease. Bring immunisation records, recent reports and birth certificates.

Should I ship my furniture?

Usually less of it than you think. Sea freight is slow and costly, and most rentals are unfurnished, so you’ll be buying basics anyway. Ship the irreplaceable, the sentimental and the high quality; sell the bulky. Check electricals against Australian plugs and voltage first. And if freight will land weeks after you do, plan for that gap.

What about my pension or savings back home?

That needs proper financial advice rather than guesswork. Rules on transfers, tax residency and timing differ by country and can be expensive to get wrong. We can suggest the questions to ask and when to ask them; a qualified adviser in both jurisdictions should handle the specifics. Moneysmart is a good independent starting point.
