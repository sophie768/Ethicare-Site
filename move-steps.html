01

Imagine the possibilities

What could your career and life look like on the other side of the world?

Most people who land here are carrying some version of the same thought — a conversation that keeps coming back, a colleague who made the move, the feeling that something could be different. Before registration forms and job applications, give yourself permission to imagine the life: the weekends, the house, what anyone coming with you would do with their days, what school looks like if there are children, how often you could get home.

Deciding whether to move shouldn’t start with a vacancy. It should start with whether you can see a life there — why people move, and what they say they miss.

Where are you in your thinking?

Eight stages describe the whole move. They are not eight steps you have to complete. Start wherever you are, skip anything you have already decided, and come back to the rest when it matters.

The two countries share a lot and sit close on a map. The differences that matter in daily life are more specific: what your profession earns in each, how long a visa takes, whether your partner can work freely, how far you are from a flight home, and what a week actually feels like where you land.

Neither is the more ambitious answer. Australia is bigger, pays more in most specialties and has one national registration covering everything. New Zealand is smaller, moves at a different pace and tends to suit people who want career and life to fit together more easily.

03

Find your place

Cities, coast, regional life, communities, climate, commuting and what different places actually feel like.

This is the question that usually gets decided by accident. A role comes up somewhere, so that becomes the answer — and only later does anyone ask whether it was the right place for the household. Housing, the commute, schools and how far you are from a big hospital vary far more between regions than between the two countries.

Tell us what you want and what you would miss from home. We will suggest three places worth reading about, with the trade-offs alongside the good bits.

On your own, the trade-off is usually distance: from specialist backup at work, and from the people at home. Every destination guide says how far each is.

Two careers, one move. Places with a broader job market for whoever is not the clinician — the main centres and state capitals — usually make the shortlist for that reason alone. Moving with a partner covers work rights.

With children, school zones and the school year shape the shortlist as much as the hospital does. Each destination guide has a schools section; the national schools guide explains zoning and terms once.

Two adults, children, one move. School zones and a job market for both of you narrow the list faster than climate does. The schools guide and moving with a partner or children between them own those two topics.

Bringing a parent changes the housing question — a second bedroom or a self-contained flat, a GP taking on patients, distance to a base hospital. Regional places tend to be generous on space and thinner on specialist cover; each destination guide’s healthcare section says which.

Can I work there?

Registration, eligibility, visas, health and character requirements.

Registration sets every timeline. It decides how long before you can work, whether your employer will wait, and which visa route is open to you. Some professions register in a few weeks. Others take the better part of a year — and some need additional exams or bridging programmes that change the whole picture.

Neither country works like the UK system you already know. The pathway checker asks about your profession, where you trained and what you hold already, then shows the route most likely to apply to you with a realistic timeline.

Two clinicians means two registrations, and your partner’s can take longer than yours — a different board, a different pathway, sometimes a different English requirement. Run Check my pathway for both of you, and read the registration master with their profession in mind as well as yours.

05

Make the numbers work

Salary, tax, housing, cost of living, moving costs and your personal budget.

Salary figures look good at first glance. What matters is what they look like after rent, the school run and one flight home a year — and that varies significantly by where you land. Auckland and Sydney are expensive. Dunedin and Adelaide are not. The same grade feels very different in each.

The cost calculator puts the whole move in one place: visa fees for everyone coming, flights, shipping, temporary accommodation and the first months of costs, alongside what an employer typically covers. The salary guide explains how pay actually works — steps, progression, penal rates and what sits on top of base — before you start comparing figures.

One income, one set of visa fees, one flight home. Most line items in the cost calculator shrink; rent does not.

Two incomes or one, and for how long? A partner’s right to work depends on their visa, and the gap before a second income starts is the figure couples most often leave out. The cost calculator has a line for it.

Add childcare or school costs to the sums. Whether school fees apply can depend on your visa — the schools guide explains when, and the cost calculator carries the figure.

Visa fees for everyone, childcare or school costs that can depend on the visa, and a possible gap before a second income. The cost calculator takes all three; the salary guide explains what one clinical salary carries.

At university age your visa route can decide whether they pay domestic or international fees — a gap wide enough to change the maths of the whole move. The visa guide explains which routes count, and the schools guide covers the tertiary side.

A dependent parent is the line most people never model: visa eligibility is narrower, health insurance may be a condition, and access to public healthcare differs by visa. Read the visa guide before you rely on any figure.

07

Plan your move

Turn the decision into a practical, personalised relocation plan.

The visa comes after the offer, not before it — your employer sponsors you. What matters is knowing which visa, what it costs for everyone coming, and how long it actually takes. Some routes take three months; some take seven. The visa guide walks through the options without the immigration jargon, and links to the official authorities for the decisions that are theirs, not ours.

Everything else — shipping, storage, temporary accommodation, the school catchment you need to be in before term starts — has a lead time that surprises people. The moving checklist is filtered to your household and keeps track of what is done.

The shortest critical path: registration, visa, somewhere to land for the first month. The moving checklist filtered to one person is a good deal shorter.

Your partner’s visa is applied for alongside yours, and their right to work depends on it. It is the item most likely to hold up a start date, so it goes early in the checklist; the visa guide explains the options.

Enrolment zones decide where you rent, and the school year decides when you arrive. Both are in the schools guide; put them on the checklist before shipping.

Dependant visas for everyone, enrolment zones, and the term dates that fix your arrival window. The visa guide and the schools guide own every one of those.

Childcare places, waiting lists and cost vary street by street, and the good ones fill early — worth starting before you fly rather than in your first week. Childcare, explained, and the official position at the Ministry of Education.

Doing this on your own is a different move, not a smaller one. Two things carry it: a school or childcare arrangement that fits a clinical roster, and the immigration authority’s requirements for children travelling with one parent — evidence about the other parent’s consent or custody is commonly asked for. Moving as a single parent.

Parent visas run on their own timetable and rules, separate from yours. The visa guide sets out what exists; the decision is the immigration authority’s, and this is where a licensed adviser earns their fee.

08

Settle into life

The first weeks and months — starting work, finding your feet and making somewhere new feel like home.

Landing is not the end of the hard part. It is the beginning of a different kind of hard — registration paperwork in a new system, your first days on the unit with people you do not know yet, ordinary things like driving on the left or finding a GP taking on patients, and the slow work of making a place feel like yours rather than somewhere you are staying.

The first-month guide runs through what needs doing in what order, what can wait, and what people say they wish they had done sooner. The community guide is the one that goes after the feelings rather than the admin.

The first months are yours to shape. The community guide is written for the quiet evening in a new flat as much as for anyone.

Your partner arrives without a workplace to walk into. The community guide is as much for them as for you, and the first-month guide covers their tax number, bank account and driving licence too.

School is the fastest route into a community — for the children and, through the school gate, for you. The first-month guide puts enrolment, the GP and the ordinary things in order.

Everyone lands at once and only one of you has a job to go to. The first-month guide puts the household admin in order; the community guide is for the rest.

A parent’s first months turn on a GP, getting around without driving, and company during the day. The community guide covers the groups that welcome newcomers; the first-month guide covers healthcare enrolment.
