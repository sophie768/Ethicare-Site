Where will your salary go further?

Higher pay is not the same as more money

Australia generally pays healthcare professionals more than New Zealand. That sounds as though it should make the financial decision straightforward. It doesn’t.

The wrong question

A higher salary is not the same as more money

Two clinicians earning similar amounts can have completely different standards of living, depending on where they live, whether they have children, how they commute and what is actually included in their employment package.

So if you’re weighing Australia against New Zealand, the useful question isn’t where will I earn more?

It’s: where will my salary give me the life I want?

This guide works through the things that actually decide that. Retirement contributions, housing, commuting, children, everyday costs, your profession, your career stage and the first-year costs nobody budgets for.

Section 01 · The headline

Australia generally wins on headline salary

Across many healthcare professions, Australian salaries tend to be higher than the equivalent New Zealand salaries. The gap can become particularly significant for some senior medical specialists, GPs and clinicians working within Australia’s private sector.

Australia

Higher, but not one market

There are substantial opportunities across diagnostic imaging and allied health too, particularly where employers are struggling to recruit experienced clinicians.

But Australia is an enormous and varied employment market. A salary in Sydney shouldn’t be treated as equivalent to the same salary in Adelaide, Hobart, Bunbury or a regional Queensland city.

New Zealand

Lower, and more transparent

For many professions the equivalent New Zealand salary will be lower. That doesn’t automatically make it a worse financial move.

The market is smaller, and many roles, particularly in the public system, sit within established salary scales or collective agreements. That makes remuneration relatively transparent, often with additional payments for overtime, weekends, nights or on-call.

The point

The number on the offer letter is only the beginning of the comparison. Everything below is the rest of it.

Section 02 · Retirement contributions

Superannuation & KiwiSaver

Both countries add employer-funded retirement savings on top of your pay. In Australia, how that’s worded in an offer can change the figure by thousands.

Australia

Check whether it’s plus super

Superannuation is Australia’s retirement savings system, and employers generally make compulsory contributions for eligible employees. That makes the wording of an offer important:

  • A$130,000 + super is not the same offer as…
  • A$130,000 including super.

When comparing two Australian jobs, or an Australian job against a New Zealand one. Establish exactly what the quoted figure represents before you do any arithmetic.

New Zealand

KiwiSaver, now at 3.5%

KiwiSaver is New Zealand’s workplace retirement savings scheme. From 1 April 2026, the default employee and matching employer contribution rate rose from 3% to 3.5%, subject to eligibility and your specific employment arrangement.

  • The default rate is scheduled to rise again to 4% from 1 April 2028.
  • Members who need to can apply to Inland Revenue for a temporary reduction back to 3%.
  • Eligibility matters for new arrivals. Joining generally requires the right to live in New Zealand indefinitely, so on a temporary work visa you may not be able to join yet.
Look wider than the base

Also look for relocation assistance, professional registration costs, CPD funding, salary packaging, overtime and on-call payments. A good package is much more than its base salary, and two offers with the same headline can be thousands apart once these are counted.

Section 03 · Housing

Housing can completely change the calculation

For most relocating healthcare professionals, housing is the largest household expense, and it’s where broad country comparisons become unreliable.

Sydney is not Australia. Auckland is not New Zealand.

Australia

The difference can vanish into rent

A clinician earning considerably more in a major Australian city may spend much of that difference on housing. Conversely, a role in a regional centre can pair a strong salary with more affordable housing than some capital-city markets.

New Zealand

Same principle, different map

Auckland’s housing costs can be challenging, whereas a role in Palmerston North, Christchurch or another regional centre may produce a completely different household budget on a similar salary.

How to compare properly

Compare job plus location, not Australia plus New Zealand. The country is far too blunt a unit for a decision this specific.

Section 04 · The commute

Then there’s the commute

This is easy to overlook when you’re comparing employment contracts from thousands of miles away. How are you actually going to get to work?

  • Will you need two cars?
  • Will you pay for parking?
  • Could you live ten minutes from the hospital or clinic?
  • Or are you likely to spend an hour each way?

A shorter commute has financial value. It also has something harder to quantify: time. Saving an hour every working day means hundreds of additional hours with your family over a year.

For a great many clinicians, that turns out to be the single biggest improvement after relocating, bigger than the salary.

Section 05 · Family

Moving with children changes everything

A single healthcare professional and a family with three children can reach completely different conclusions about exactly the same salary.

Families need to weigh housing size, school location, childcare, after-school care, transport and activities, and schooling often decides where you live rather than the other way round.

Australia

Varies by state and system

Arrangements differ between states and between school systems, so the relationship between where you live and where your children can enrol needs checking locally rather than nationally.

New Zealand

Enrolment zones matter

School enrolment zones can make the link between your address and your children’s schooling particularly direct, worth understanding before you sign a tenancy, not after.

Look at the household, not the house

The cheapest house isn’t necessarily the cheapest solution, if it creates additional transport, childcare or commuting costs. Add it up across the whole household.

Section 06 · Everyday costs

Two misconceptions worth losing

People relocating often focus heavily on property prices, but everyday costs matter too, and the intuitions people arrive with about each country tend to be wrong in opposite directions.

Misconception one

“Higher Australian salary means higher Australian costs”

It isn’t that simple. Australia’s cost of living varies substantially by location, and while housing remains an important contributor to household cost pressures, those pressures are not identical across cities or household types.

A regional healthcare position can therefore be attractive: a strong salary, a shorter commute and potentially lower housing costs than one of the most expensive metropolitan markets. “Regional” doesn’t automatically mean remote.

Misconception two

“New Zealand must be cheap”

Smaller cities and a relaxed lifestyle create an impression that living there must be inexpensive. That isn’t necessarily so. Housing in some areas is expensive, groceries can be costly and imported goods may cost more than new arrivals expect.

Choose New Zealand because the overall proposition works for you, not because you assume everything will be cheaper.

On groceries specifically: food can feel expensive in both countries, particularly at first, when you instinctively convert every price back into pounds. Over time most people stop shopping as though they’re still in Britain, different supermarkets, different brands, seasonal buying, local markets and suppliers. But food belongs in your initial relocation budget all the same.

Section 07 · Your profession

There isn’t one answer for “healthcare professionals”

The financial comparison changes completely depending on what you do, because the pay structures themselves are different.

  • An experienced radiologist considering Australian private practice is making a very different calculation from a radiation therapist comparing two public-sector positions.
  • A GP considering an Australian percentage-of-billings arrangement cannot sensibly compare the headline number with a salaried New Zealand GP position.
  • A sonographer in an area of significant workforce shortage may be able to command a different package again.
  • Radiographers, psychologists, physiotherapists, anaesthetic technicians and others all have their own salary structures and employment markets.

Which is why profession-specific advice beats country-level advice every time. Our profession guides go through each one, and the GP, radiologist and sonographer pages each carry a compared section.

Section 08 · Career stage

Your career stage matters too

Moving overseas isn’t always about maximising your income immediately.

Earlier in your career

What the role builds

An opportunity might offer experience in a new modality, greater autonomy, leadership responsibility or access to a specialist area that strengthens your career over the next decade. That can be worth more than the difference in salary.

Later in your career

What the role costs you

The priority may reverse: willingly exchanging some earning potential for fewer sessions, predictable hours, a shorter commute or more time with your family.

Money matters. But so does what you’re being paid to do.

Section 09 · The first year

Relocation assistance, and why year one isn’t year five

Two things that reshape the financial picture and rarely appear in a salary comparison.

Relocation support

It has real, countable value

Depending on the employer and profession, a package may contribute towards flights, temporary accommodation, shipping, registration, immigration expenses or other relocation costs. For a family moving halfway around the world, that can be thousands of dollars.

An employer offering a slightly lower salary but meaningful relocation assistance may well be the better financial proposition in your first year.

  • Establish exactly what is included.
  • Ask whether there are repayment conditions.
  • Ask what happens if you leave the role earlier than expected.
Year one

Relocating is expensive

You may initially pay for temporary accommodation, rental bonds, furniture, a car, registration, immigration costs and flights. You may also arrive before your partner starts working.

Your first six months can therefore feel considerably tighter than your eventual lifestyle suggests, and that doesn’t tell you whether the move was financially worthwhile. Once you’re established, household finances can look very different.

Section 10 · The verdict

So, where does your salary go further?

There isn’t a universal winner, but there is usually a clear answer for a specific person considering a specific job.

Australia often comes out ahead if…
  • Maximising earning potential is your priority
  • You’re in a profession or sector where private earnings are substantially higher
  • You’re open to regional as well as metropolitan roles
  • You’d rather have the higher number and manage the costs
New Zealand can still win if…
  • A manageable commute and a smaller city appeal
  • Predictable, transparent pay matters more than the ceiling
  • You want to reduce the costs, and stresses, of a major city
  • What you moved for isn’t primarily the money
The honest summary

A high income in an expensive metropolitan area can disappear surprisingly quickly. For some people Australia will leave substantially more money in the bank. For others, New Zealand will provide more of what they actually moved for.

Before you accept anything

Compare the life, not just the salary

When you’re weighing an offer, write down more than the salary: expected take-home pay, retirement contributions, housing, transport, childcare, working hours, annual leave, relocation assistance and likely household income.

Then write down something else entirely.

What does an ordinary Tuesday look like?

  • What time will you leave home?
  • How long will it take to get to work?
  • What time will you finish?
  • How far away is your child’s school?
  • Can you afford the kind of home you want?
  • And what will you be able to do when the working day is finished?

Those questions won’t appear on an employment contract. They may tell you far more about whether Australia or New Zealand offers the better deal.

Common questions

The money questions we’re asked most

Does Australia always pay more?

Generally yes on the headline figure, and sometimes substantially, particularly for senior medical specialists, GPs and clinicians in the private sector. But Australia is many employment markets rather than one, and a Sydney salary isn’t an Adelaide, Hobart or regional Queensland salary. What you keep depends far more on where you live than on which country you choose.

What does “plus super” actually mean?

That the employer’s compulsory retirement contribution sits on top of the quoted salary rather than inside it. “$130,000 + super” and “$130,000 including super” are materially different offers. Always establish which convention an advert or offer is using before comparing it with anything else.

How much does KiwiSaver add?

The default employee and matching employer rate rose to 3.5% from 1 April 2026, and is scheduled to reach 4% from 1 April 2028. Eligibility matters for new arrivals: joining generally requires the right to live in New Zealand indefinitely, so on a temporary work visa you may not be able to join immediately. Confirm your position with a provider or Inland Revenue.

Should I take a lower salary with relocation support?

Often it’s the better first-year proposition. Flights, shipping, temporary accommodation, registration and immigration costs add up fast for a family. Just establish what’s included, whether there are repayment conditions, and what happens if you leave sooner than planned.

Why won’t you just tell me which country pays more for my role?

Because the answer changes with your profession, your seniority, public or private, the state or region, and your household. We’d rather work through your actual numbers than hand you an average that fits nobody. Tell us your profession and where you’re considering, and we’ll do it properly.

I’m not from the UK, does this still apply to me?

Yes. We support clinicians trained across the UK, Ireland, South Africa, the UAE and beyond. The currency you’re converting from changes, but the structure of the comparison, package, housing, commute, household, doesn’t.
