Working as a Sonographer in Australia or New Zealand?

One title, two very different systems

Of all the professions we place, sonography is where the two countries differ most, starting with the fact that only one of them regulates the title.

Two very different systems

The profession is the same. The system around it isn’t.

Sonographers are in demand in both Australia and New Zealand, and both countries actively recruit internationally trained staff. If you scan well, you will find work in either.

But sonography is the profession where the two countries diverge most sharply. New Zealand regulates sonographers as a registered health profession with a protected scope. Australia does not regulate the title at all. Instead, a combination of professional recognition and an accreditation register governs who can scan and, crucially, whose scans attract funding.

That single difference shapes the paperwork before you arrive, who can employ you, and how much say you have over your own practice once you’re there.

Section 02 · The big one

The regulatory difference

This is the part that surprises almost every sonographer we speak to, and the part worth understanding before you compare a single salary.

Australia

Recognition, then accreditation, not registration

Sonography is not a registered profession in Australia. There is no Ahpra board for sonographers and the title is not protected, which is different from your radiographer and radiation therapist colleagues, who register with Ahpra through the Medical Radiation Practice Board.

Instead the pathway runs through two bodies:

  • ASMIRT assesses and recognises your overseas qualification.
  • ASAR. The Australian Sonographer Accreditation Registry, is the accreditation register. Being on it is what allows your scanning to attract Medicare funding.

In practice that means accreditation is commercially essential rather than legally mandatory: an employer whose income depends on Medicare rebates needs an accredited sonographer.

New Zealand

A registered profession with a scope

New Zealand regulates sonographers through the Medical Radiation Technologists Board. You hold registration within a defined scope of practice and renew an Annual Practising Certificate each year.

  • The Board assesses your qualification, recent clinical experience and competence against New Zealand standards.
  • Registration is a legal requirement, not a commercial one, you cannot practise without it.
  • Your scope is explicit, which cuts both ways: it protects the profession, and it defines its edges.
Why this matters more than it sounds

In New Zealand, a regulator stands between you and an employer who wants something outside your scope. In Australia, that protection comes from your professional body and your own judgment rather than from law. Some sonographers value the autonomy; others find the New Zealand framework reassuring, particularly when they’re new to a country and don’t yet know what normal looks like.

Section 03 · Scale

The two markets

Both countries are short of sonographers. What differs is the kind of employer doing the hiring.

Australia

Private, and very large

Australia has one of the world’s biggest private imaging markets, alongside public hospitals in every state and territory. For sonographers that means a great many employers, a great many locations, and genuine competition for experienced staff.

It also means most roles sit in a commercial setting where throughput matters to the business model.

New Zealand

Smaller, and short-staffed

A much smaller market, predominantly public, with a growing private sector. Sonography is one of New Zealand’s most persistently short-staffed professions, which gives experienced international sonographers unusually strong standing.

Fewer employers, but the ones there are tend to want you badly.

The honest trade-off

Australia offers choice: if a role doesn’t suit, there are others nearby. New Zealand offers use: in a country this short of sonographers, a good candidate has real room to ask for what they need. Different kinds of power.

Section 04 · Scope

Scope & specialisation

Most sonographers work across general, obstetric and gynaecological, vascular and musculoskeletal scanning, with cardiac sonography often a distinct pathway. How much you can concentrate depends on where you go.

Australia

Room to concentrate

The size of the market supports more specialised work, particularly in metropolitan practices where a single site may run enough volume in one area to keep a specialist busy. Cardiac and vascular sonography, MSK and obstetric work can all be pursued in depth.

New Zealand

Breadth, with depth possible

A smaller market often means a broader daily list, general, obstetric and vascular in the same week. Many sonographers enjoy that variety, and there is still room to develop genuine expertise, particularly in the larger centres.

Ask before you accept

Modality and case mix vary enormously between employers, more than between the two countries. Ask what proportion of your list would be in the area you care about, and whether that is guaranteed or aspirational.

Section 05 · The working day

Scanning volumes, and looking after your body

Sonography is the one imaging profession where workload is a physical question as well as a clinical one. Repetitive strain is a career-ending risk, and appointment length is the variable that decides it.

This is where the difference between a high-throughput private practice and a public department becomes very concrete. Neither is inherently better, but they are not the same job, and the honest questions are the same in both countries:

  • How long is each appointment, and how many patients per session?
  • Is there time between scans, or is the list back to back?
  • What ergonomic equipment is provided, and is it actually used?
  • Who covers when a colleague is sick or on leave?
  • What happens when the list overruns? Do you stay, or does it move?
  • Is there protected time for reporting and image review?
  • What sonographer-specific support exists if you develop symptoms?

A generous salary spent on physiotherapy isn’t a generous salary.

Ask these before you sign, in either country. An employer who answers them precisely is telling you something good; an employer who answers them vaguely is also telling you something.

Section 06 · Pay

How pay is structured

Sonographer pay is built differently in each country, which is why comparing two headline numbers rarely tells you much.

Australia

Award floor, private market above it

A national modern award sets a minimum for allied health professionals in the private sector, and public hospitals pay under state and territory enterprise agreements that sit above it. Most sonographer roles, though, are in private imaging. Where high volumes and short supply support competitive pay.

Because the market is commercial, there is real room to negotiate, and packages vary by employer as much as by state.

  • Establish whether a figure is base salary or a package including superannuation.
  • Ask what sits on top: overtime, on-call, CPD funding, accreditation costs.
New Zealand

Collective agreements, published steps

Public-sector pay sits on a published step within a national collective agreement, so the scale is transparent and the same across the country. Your job is to land on the correct step for your experience, which is where good representation earns its keep.

  • Overseas experience normally counts towards your step; document it fully.
  • Penal rates, allowances and KiwiSaver sit on top of base pay.

The question isn’t which country pays sonographers more. It’s what you’ll earn for the list you’ll actually be scanning.

Section 07 · Location

Where could you live?

Sonographers have more geographic freedom than almost any other imaging profession, because ultrasound is needed absolutely everywhere.

Australia

Regional shouldn’t mean remote

Private imaging groups operate across metropolitan and regional Australia, and shortages are often sharpest outside the capitals, which is where incentives, accommodation support and stronger packages tend to appear.

Cities such as Geelong, Newcastle, Wollongong, the Gold Coast, Bunbury and many regional centres offer substantial populations, schools, hospitals and established communities alongside easier access to the coast.

New Zealand

Demand almost everywhere

Because base pay is national in the public system, the same role pays the same in Auckland as in a smaller centre, so your money goes noticeably further outside the largest city.

Wellington, Christchurch, Hamilton, Tauranga, Palmerston North, Nelson, Hawke’s Bay, Dunedin and numerous smaller communities all need sonographers, and a ten-minute commute is normal rather than remarkable.

Section 08 · Family & lifestyle

Family & lifestyle

Both countries are safe, English-speaking and outdoors-oriented, with accessible schooling and universal healthcare for your family. They feel different to live in.

Australia

Warm, big and buzzy

A warmer climate across much of the country, larger cities with plenty going on, more employment options for a partner, and enormous variety of places to settle. Distances are big, which shapes travel and where you choose to live.

New Zealand

Green, compact and calm

Mountains, coast and lakes rarely far away, smaller and more relaxed cities, shorter commutes and the outdoors on your doorstep. For families arriving from a major city, even Auckland can feel considerably smaller in scale.

Worth being honest about

Some sonographers find New Zealand wonderfully manageable and never look back. Others eventually want the scale and options of a bigger country. Both reactions are common, and neither is a failure. Experience in one country is valued in the other.

Section 09 · Deciding

So, which should you choose?

Read both columns and notice which one you nod along to.

Australia may suit you if…
  • Earning potential is high on your list
  • You want a large private market and many employers
  • Concentrating in a specialty area matters to you
  • You’d like the widest geographic choice
  • You’re comfortable in a commercial, high-throughput setting
New Zealand may suit you if…
  • A regulated profession with a defined scope reassures you
  • Transparent, published pay steps appeal
  • You want a broad generalist list
  • A shorter commute and smaller city suit your family
  • You’d value being in demand
The thing to check first

Whichever way you’re leaning, start with the paperwork. ASMIRT recognition and ASAR accreditation, or MRT Board registration and an APC, are not interchangeable and take different amounts of time. Understanding both before you choose sometimes settles the decision on its own.

Sophie’s insight

Sonographers are the most sought-after people we work with, and I don’t think the profession always realises how much standing that gives you. You can ask hard questions. How long are the appointments? What happens when the list overruns? What ergonomic support is there, really? The employers worth working for answer those precisely, because they’ve thought about it. My honest advice is to judge the role on the shape of the working day rather than the number at the top, because in sonography, more than in any other imaging profession, the working day is what determines whether you’re still enjoying this in ten years.

SophieFounder, Ethicare Resourcing

Common questions

The things sonographers ask us first

If sonography isn’t regulated in Australia, do I still need accreditation?

In practice, yes. The title isn’t protected and there’s no Ahpra registration, but ASAR accreditation is what links your scanning to Medicare funding, so employers whose income depends on rebates need accredited sonographers. It’s commercially essential rather than legally mandatory, which amounts to the same thing when you’re job-hunting.

Does registration in one country help in the other?

Not directly. New Zealand’s MRT Board registration and Australia’s ASMIRT recognition and ASAR accreditation are separate processes with different requirements and timelines. Experience gained in either country is well regarded in the other, but you would start the other country’s process from the beginning.

Where will I earn more?

Australia’s private market generally supports higher pay, particularly where volumes are high and supply is short. New Zealand’s public pay is transparent and predictable, and the shortage gives good candidates real use. The more useful comparison is what you’d earn for the list you’d actually be scanning, and what it costs to live where the job is.

Will I be scanning back-to-back all day?

That depends far more on the employer than the country. High-throughput private practices exist in both; so do departments with sensible appointment lengths. It’s the single most important thing to establish before accepting a role, because it’s the difference between a sustainable career and an injury.

Can I keep working in my specialty area?

In Australia’s larger centres, often yes, the volume supports it. In New Zealand, and outside the biggest Australian centres, expect a broader list alongside your specialty. Ask what proportion of the work would be in your area, and whether that’s contractual or hopeful.

I’m not from the UK, does this still apply to me?

Yes. We support sonographers trained across the UK, Ireland, South Africa, the UAE and beyond. Both countries recognise a wide range of international qualifications. The specifics of your assessment are exactly what we help you check.
