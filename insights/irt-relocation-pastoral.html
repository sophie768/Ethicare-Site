Chapter 05

Relocation, pastoral support & family integration

Sophie CareemFounder, Ethicare Resourcing · former NHS change leader
Toolkit · 05 of 08
OverviewThe workforce challengeWhy it matters01 · Building a sustainable workforce02 · Ethical & responsible sourcing03 · Planning & preparation04 · Creating a recruitment programme05 · Relocation & family integration06 · Induction & belonging07 · Retention & belonging08 · Measuring success

People don’t move countries for a job. They move for a life, and that’s the part most employers underprepare for.

I’ve sat with clinicians the week before they fly. The contract is signed, the visa has come through, and what they want to talk about is whether their daughter will make friends, or how a partner will feel about giving up their own career for a year. That conversation tells you what relocation actually is. It isn’t the flights and the rental car, the easy things an agency can book in an afternoon. It’s a whole family trying to land on its feet in a country it has never lived in. Employers who understand that keep the people they hire. The ones who treat it as logistics usually lose them inside a year.

You’re never hiring just one person

Recruit a clinician and you’re really recruiting a household. There’s often a partner leaving a job they liked, who will want one of their own before the move feels like a good idea. There are children starting again at a new school, in a new accent. Sometimes there are ageing parents staying behind, and the quiet guilt that travels with that. None of it shows up on a CV, and all of it decides more offers than salary ever does. Look after the family and you protect the hire. Leave them to cope alone and no pay packet will hold it together.

The small stuff that sinks a move

Most of what derails a relocation is small and dull. A bank that won’t open an account without a local address, and an address you can’t secure without a bank account. A school enrolment deadline nobody mentioned. A tax number that quietly takes three weeks. You don’t have to solve every piece of it yourself. You do have to make sure someone is walking alongside people, rather than leaving them to work it out on their phone at eleven at night.

The questions no relocation pack answers

Underneath the logistics, people are quietly asking themselves things no welcome pack ever covers:

  • Will I fit in here?
  • Will the children settle?
  • Will I miss home too much?
  • Have we made the right call?

You can’t promise the answers will all be yes. But asking the questions out loud, and being honest about the hard parts, is usually what separates someone who feels supported from someone who feels sold to.

Belonging begins before the plane

The best moves start weeks before the flight. A short video call with the team they’re about to join. An introduction to another family who made the same jump last year and can say which suburb to look at and which school still has space. An honest account of what the first month really feels like. None of it costs much, and all of it means someone steps off the plane already half-belonging, instead of starting from nothing.

Cultural transition is real, even in English

The surprise, for a lot of people, is that a shared language is not a shared workplace. A clinician arrives from the UK or South Africa completely fluent and still spends months working out how decisions get made here: when it’s your turn to speak in a meeting, how consent is handled, what “she’ll be right” means on a busy ward. That’s a context problem, not a competence one, and it fades quickly with a bit of preparation. It runs both ways, too. The existing team needs a nudge to meet a new colleague halfway, and not to mistake unfamiliarity for a lack of ability.

The cheapest retention you’ll ever buy

Pastoral support has an image problem. It sounds soft, and it’s first in line when budgets tighten. Yet it’s one of the clearest signals of who will still be here in three years. Someone who felt genuinely looked after when they were at their most exposed (new, far from home, quietly overwhelmed) does not forget it. They settle faster, they stay longer, and they’re the ones who tell a friend back home to apply. That isn’t sentiment. It’s the cheapest retention you will ever buy.

Best practice · The week-one basics

What good looks like in the first seven days:

  • Someone at the airport, and a stocked fridge in the accommodation.
  • Bank account, tax number and phone sorted within the week.
  • School enrolments confirmed before the flight, not after.
  • One person whose job is answering the silly questions.

The first six months matter most

The first six months are where a move is won or lost. Everything is new at once: the hospital’s systems and the supermarket’s layout, driving on the other side of the road, the school run in a town you don’t know yet, a social life rebuilt from scratch on the weekends. A fortnightly check-in sounds almost too small to matter. It’s how you catch the wobble while it’s still a wobble, and not a resignation letter.

What “done well” looks like

You can’t measure pastoral support with a checklist. The real measure is a life taking root somewhere new. A few moments from people we’ve supported (shared anonymously):

Radiographer · from South Africa

What she talks about isn’t the job. It’s that her children can finally cycle to school and play outside until dinner, the everyday freedom that made the whole move worth it.

Anaesthetic technician · from Iran

Two years in, he’s walked several of New Zealand’s Great Walks and found a passion for the outdoors he never expected, a life that now feels entirely his own.

Sonographer · from the UK

She swapped a long daily commute for evenings at the beach in Auckland. The role was similar; the life around it was transformed.

The principles behind great pastoral support

  • Relocation is about rebuilding a life, not simply changing jobs.
  • Supporting families is essential, not optional.
  • Practical support has a huge impact on confidence and wellbeing.
  • Belonging begins before arrival.
  • Pastoral support is one of the strongest investments in long-term retention.
