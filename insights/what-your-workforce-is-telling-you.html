Workforce & leadership

What your workforce is already telling you

Sophie CareemFounder, Ethicare Resourcing · former NHS change leader
7 min read

Why exit interviews, stay interviews and staff surveys may be your most underused workforce-planning tools.

Healthcare organisations collect vast amounts of workforce data: vacancy rates, turnover, agency spend, sickness absence, recruitment metrics. Yet one of the most important questions is often overlooked: what are our people actually telling us?

Throughout my time in NHS transformation, some of the most valuable insights did not come from dashboards. They came from conversations: with people who were leaving, with people who were staying, and with teams who wanted to deliver excellent care but were facing mounting pressure. The challenge is rarely a lack of information. More often, it is whether organisations are listening.

The limitations of workforce metrics

Metrics matter. They identify trends and flag concern. But they tend to tell us what is happening rather than why. A turnover rate tells us people are leaving; it cannot tell us why. A rise in sickness absence signals pressure; it cannot tell us where that pressure comes from. If we want to improve retention, we have to move beyond measurement towards understanding.

Exit interviews: the missed opportunity

Most organisations conduct exit interviews. Many do very little with what they hear. In my experience the value does not sit in any single response: it sits in the themes across them. Why are people leaving? What influenced the decision? Would they recommend the organisation? Would they ever return? What could have been done differently? The objective is not to defend the organisation. It is to learn.

The power of stay interviews

Here is something I have observed repeatedly: organisations often spend more time talking to people who are leaving than people who are staying. Stay interviews reverse that. Instead of asking why did you leave?, we ask why do you stay? What you enjoy most, what keeps you engaged, what might cause you to go, what we could improve. That information is often more valuable than any exit interview, because it gives you the chance to act before someone resigns.

What staff surveys can really tell us

Most organisations run staff surveys; the challenge is rarely collecting responses, but turning them into action. Themes tend to cluster around leadership, recognition, workload, career development, communication, team culture and psychological safety. The NHS Staff Survey and the NHS People Promise exist precisely because that feedback predicts retention. The organisations that improve outcomes are the ones that act visibly on what they hear.

The friends-and-family question

One of the simplest questions is often the most revealing: would you recommend this organisation as a place to work? It cuts through complexity. People may not articulate every challenge, but they almost always know whether they would encourage someone they care about to join. If the answer is no, leaders should be curious, not defensive.

Looking for hotspots

Not every team experiences the same pressure. One of the most useful exercises is identifying workforce hotspots: teams with higher turnover, higher sickness, recruitment difficulty, or heavy reliance on temporary staffing. The question should never be who is performing badly? It should be what can we learn?

From listening to action

Collecting feedback is only the first step. The organisations that make progress are those that listen, understand, act, measure and repeat. Workforce improvement is not a one-off project: it is an ongoing cycle.

The workforce improvement cycle
Listen→ Understand→ Act→ Measure→ Listen again
Before we can improve retention, engagement or stability, we need to understand what our workforce is already trying to tell us.

Final thoughts

Healthcare organisations spend a great deal of time discussing recruitment, and recruitment matters. But some of the greatest workforce opportunities already exist within the teams we have. The challenge is not always finding new people: sometimes it is understanding the people who are already there.
