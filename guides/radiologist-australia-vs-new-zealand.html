Two strong options

One college, two quite different working lives

For radiologists considering an international move, Australia and New Zealand offer two of the most attractive options in the world. Both have sophisticated imaging services, modern equipment, significant demand for experienced specialists and a shared training tradition through the Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Radiologists (RANZCR).

But the experience of working in each can be quite different. Australia offers a much larger radiology market, significant private-sector opportunities and potentially very high earning potential. New Zealand offers a smaller and more connected healthcare system, interesting public and private roles, and a lifestyle that suits radiologists looking for a long-term change rather than a different job.

So how do you choose?

Section 02 · Scale

The radiology market

Perhaps the biggest difference is scale, and scale changes what kind of career is available to you.

Australia

Choice, in every direction

A population of more than 27 million spread across a vast geographical area. The imaging sector includes large public hospital departments, major private radiology groups, smaller independent practices and increasingly sophisticated remote reporting networks.

You might work in a tertiary hospital in Melbourne, join a private imaging group in Perth, specialise in MSK imaging in a regional coastal city, or combine on-site clinical work with teleradiology.

New Zealand

Smaller, and much more connected

Considerably smaller, but that does not mean the work is less sophisticated. Major centres including Auckland, Wellington and Christchurch provide complex tertiary imaging, while private providers operate advanced CT, MRI, PET/CT, nuclear medicine and interventional services.

The difference is that the radiology community feels connected. You are working within a country of around five million people rather than a continent-sized healthcare market.

The honest trade-off

For some radiologists, the size of the New Zealand community is precisely the attraction. You can know your colleagues nationally and see your contribution land. For others, Australia’s scale is what makes a specialised career possible at all.

Section 03 · Setting

Public or private?

An important question in both countries, and one worth answering before you compare any two offers, because it changes the shape of the whole job.

Australia

A substantial private sector

Private radiology can offer excellent remuneration and considerable flexibility. Depending on the organisation and your seniority, arrangements may include salary, sessional payments, contractor models or more commercially structured agreements. The potential earnings can be significant.

It is important to understand exactly what sits behind an attractive headline figure. Ask about:

  • Expected reporting volumes and modality mix
  • After-hours, weekend and on-call requirements
  • Procedures within your scope
  • Leave and professional development
  • Whether remuneration is salary, sessional or productivity based

For some radiologists the private sector provides exactly the combination they want: interesting clinical work, autonomy and strong financial reward. Others prefer the teaching, multidisciplinary environment and complex case mix of a public hospital.

New Zealand

Public, private, or a mix of both

New Zealand has an established private imaging sector alongside the public system. Radiologists may work entirely within a public hospital, entirely in private practice, or combine elements of both.

  • Public roles offer exposure to complex pathology, multidisciplinary meetings, teaching and a broad clinical caseload.
  • Private practice can offer attractive remuneration and access to excellent imaging facilities.
  • Because the overall workforce is smaller, experienced radiologists can make a visible contribution to a service relatively quickly.
Section 04 · Earnings

What can a radiologist earn?

If maximum earning potential is the priority, Australia generally has the advantage. But the headline figure is only ever part of the story.

Australia

A high ceiling, especially in private

Private-sector radiology can be extremely well remunerated, particularly for experienced specialists and radiologists with sought-after skills. MSK, breast imaging, interventional radiology and other areas of specialist expertise can be particularly valuable to employers, although demand varies by location and organisation.

New Zealand

Well remunerated, lower ceiling

New Zealand radiologists are also well remunerated, particularly by New Zealand standards, but the ceiling is generally lower than the upper end of Australian private practice.

A package may include professional development allowances, relocation assistance, additional sessions, on-call payments, indemnity arrangements and other benefits. The structure of the working week matters just as much: a radiologist earning less but working eight sessions with predictable hours may well consider that the better proposition than earning substantially more in a high-volume environment.

The question isn’t which country pays radiologists more. It’s what you’ll earn for the way you actually want to work.

Section 05 · Scope

Will your subspecialty make a difference?

Absolutely. A radiologist is not a radiologist when an employer is recruiting. An organisation may specifically need an MSK, breast, interventional or neuroradiologist, or someone comfortable reporting across a broad general workload.

Australia

Depth is possible

A larger population naturally supports more highly subspecialised roles. In major metropolitan centres it may be possible to concentrate a significant proportion of your work within your subspecialty.

New Zealand

Breadth is often valued

Even with a particular area of expertise, an employer may value your ability to contribute to general radiology alongside your subspecialty. This is particularly relevant outside the largest centres.

Establish this before you accept

Some radiologists love that variety. Others have spent years developing a highly specialised practice and do not want to move away from it. Neither position is wrong, but finding out afterwards is expensive.

Section 06 · Registration

Registration: one of the most important parts of the decision

For an internationally trained radiologist, registration should be considered before deciding which country offers the better opportunity. Australia and New Zealand share RANZCR as their specialist college, but they have different medical regulators and different registration pathways.

Australia

Specialist assessment through RANZCR

Internationally qualified specialist radiologists who are not eligible for Australia’s Expedited Specialist pathway will generally need their specialist training and experience assessed through the appropriate specialist pathway.

RANZCR assesses qualifications, specialist training, examinations, experience, recent practice and continuing professional development against the Australian-trained radiologist standard. The outcome, and any additional requirements, therefore depend on your individual background.

  • An Expedited Specialist pathway was introduced for certain diagnostic radiologists in July 2026.
  • Eligibility is qualification-specific. Being a recognised specialist in the UK, Ireland, Canada or elsewhere does not automatically make you eligible for the expedited route.
New Zealand

Assessment against the vocational standard

Internationally trained radiologists seeking specialist recognition are assessed against the standard expected of a New Zealand vocationally trained radiologist.

Depending on qualifications and experience, you may enter through a provisional vocational pathway before progressing to full vocational registration. That can involve a period of supervised practice and, depending on the assessment outcome, additional requirements.

  • New Zealand has also introduced a fast-track provisional vocational pathway for doctors holding certain approved overseas postgraduate qualifications.
Registration is personal

Two radiologists from the same country can receive different requirements, because their training, examinations, subspecialty experience and recent scope of practice differ. Before making a decision based on salary or location, understand your likely route in both countries.

Section 07 · The working week

What about workload?

Radiology has changed enormously. Remote reporting has created flexibility that would have been unimaginable a generation ago, but it has also made workload less visible.

A beautiful imaging centre near the beach tells you very little about what your working day will actually feel like. So ask:

  • How many sessions will I work, and what reporting volumes are expected?
  • What is the modality mix, and how much of my work will be within my subspecialty?
  • Will I perform procedures? Is there an on-call rota?
  • Can reporting be completed remotely?
  • What administrative or MDT commitments are expected?
  • How is discrepancy or peer review managed?
  • What happens when the reporting list becomes unusually busy?

These questions are often more revealing than the salary.

Section 08 · Location

Where could you live?

In both countries, the received wisdom about capital cities leaves some of the best opportunities unexamined.

Australia

Regional shouldn’t mean remote

Some international doctors focus exclusively on Sydney, Melbourne or Brisbane. That can mean overlooking exceptional opportunities.

Regional cities can have major hospitals, sophisticated private imaging centres, excellent schools, airports and populations in the tens or hundreds of thousands. Bunbury in Western Australia, for example, offers a very different proposition from central Perth while still providing established healthcare services and a coastal lifestyle. Similar opportunities exist across Queensland, Victoria, Tasmania, South Australia and New South Wales.

For radiologists who do not need to live in a major capital, Australia’s geography opens up considerably.

New Zealand

The smaller cities are part of the appeal

Auckland has the country’s largest healthcare market, but it is far from the only option. Wellington, Christchurch, Hamilton, Tauranga, Palmerston North, Nelson, Hawke’s Bay and other centres can offer compelling specialist opportunities.

The scale can take adjustment. A radiologist relocating from London, Johannesburg or Dubai may look at a smaller New Zealand city and wonder whether there will be enough there. For many people, that becomes the reason they stay: a short journey to work, good schools, space at home, and reaching the coast or countryside without planning a weekend around the traffic.

For others, it will feel too small. Both reactions are valid.

Section 09 · Family & lifestyle

Moving with a family

Radiologists are often relocating at a later stage of their careers than other healthcare professionals, and that changes the conversation entirely.

There may be a partner with an established career, children approaching important school years, a property to sell or rent, and significant financial commitments at home.

Australia

More options around the job

Larger cities can offer more employment opportunities for partners and a wider choice of schools and universities, alongside extraordinary weather and outdoor living.

New Zealand

Smaller scale, closer outdoors

New Zealand may appeal to families specifically because of its smaller scale and access to outdoor life, mountains, coastlines and national parks on a scale that becomes part of normal life.

Housing costs vary enormously in both countries, so comparing a salary in isolation is rarely useful. A package in regional Australia or a smaller New Zealand city can look very different once housing, commuting and everyday living are considered.

On “work-life balance”

Don’t choose either country because somebody told you it has a great one. Find out what your actual working week looks like. If you finish at 5pm, have a short commute and can have dinner outside with your family, the lifestyle proposition is real. If you are regularly reporting into the evening because the list has not been cleared, living beside the ocean will not magically create balance.

Section 10 · Deciding

So, which should you choose?

Read both columns and notice which one you nod along to.

Australia may suit you if…
  • You want maximum earning potential
  • A large, sophisticated private radiology market appeals
  • You want greater geographical choice
  • Highly subspecialised work matters to you
  • You’d like the option of a much larger healthcare system
New Zealand may suit you if…
  • You want a smaller, more connected medical community
  • Interesting public and private work appeals
  • A potentially broader clinical role suits you
  • Shorter distances matter to your family
  • Access to the outdoors is part of the point
The answer usually arrives late

For some radiologists, Australia is the obvious choice. For others, New Zealand offers exactly the change they are looking for. And for many, it only becomes clear when they stop comparing countries and start comparing actual jobs.

Sophie’s insight

Our role isn’t to persuade you that one country is better than the other. It’s to help you understand what a particular move would mean for you, the role itself, your likely registration pathway, remuneration, location, subspecialty opportunities, working pattern and the practical realities of relocating your family. A radiologist considering an MSK position in regional Western Australia may have completely different priorities from someone considering a public hospital role in Wellington. The right answer should be different for them too. Because you aren’t choosing between two countries. You’re choosing the job, and the life that comes with it.

Common questions

The things radiologists ask us first

Will I earn more in Australia?

Generally yes, particularly at the top end of private practice, and particularly with sought-after subspecialty skills. But remuneration structures differ. Salary, sessional, contractor and productivity-based arrangements are not comparable on a headline number. The useful question is what you’d earn for the working pattern you actually want, which we work through per role.

Does RANZCR being shared make registration transferable?

No. Australia and New Zealand share the college but have different medical regulators and different registration pathways. Being assessed in one country does not register you in the other, and the requirements you’re given can differ. It’s worth understanding your likely route in both before you decide.

Am I eligible for Australia’s Expedited Specialist pathway?

Possibly, but don’t assume it. The pathway was introduced for certain diagnostic radiologists in July 2026 and eligibility is qualification-specific, recognised specialist status in the UK, Ireland, Canada or elsewhere doesn’t automatically qualify you. We’ll help you check your specific position before you plan around it.

Will I be able to keep working in my subspecialty?

In Australia’s larger centres, often yes. You may concentrate much of your work within it. In New Zealand, and outside the largest Australian centres, employers frequently value your ability to contribute to general radiology alongside your subspecialty. Establish this explicitly before accepting a position; it’s one of the biggest sources of later regret.

Is remote reporting an option?

Increasingly, yes — Australia in particular has sophisticated remote reporting networks, and many roles blend on-site and remote work. It’s worth asking specifically how much of the reporting can be done remotely, and what that means for your on-call and MDT commitments.

I’m not from the UK, does this still apply to me?

Yes. We support radiologists trained across the UK, Ireland, South Africa, the UAE and beyond. Both countries recognise a wide range of international qualifications. The specifics are what we help you check.
