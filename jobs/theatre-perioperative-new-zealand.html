If you trained overseas

Your job title may not translate. Your scope usually does.

New Zealand regulates anaesthetic technology through the Medical Sciences Council. If you have worked as an operating department practitioner, an anaesthetic practitioner, an anaesthesia technologist or under another theatre title, the question that decides your route is not what the job was called — it is whether your training and your clinical scope line up with the New Zealand profession.

That cuts both ways, and it is worth knowing which way before you spend anything. A UK ODP diploma and a decade of lists is a strong position. A job title that sounds close but covered a narrower scope is a harder one. We have taken people through this in both directions and would rather tell you which you are early.

In New Zealand

A registered profession

Anaesthetic technicians register with the Medical Sciences Council of New Zealand against a defined scope of practice. Registration is what allows you to work, and it runs on its own timeline.

The practical upside: the role carries formal standing here, and your experience is assessed and recognised rather than taken on trust.

Where you may have trained

Often no register at all

In several countries the people doing this work are unregulated. If that is you, expect the evidence requirements to focus on demonstrating scope rather than on matching a qualification.

In practice that means gathering the detail of what you do — the lists, the techniques, the equipment, the supervision arrangements — and it is worth starting that long before you apply.

The shape of the day

Where do you fit around the patient?

Perioperative work is defined by handover — one patient through five settings in a few hours, with a different combination of the team around them at each. Find the stages you own now, and you can see quickly where an overseas role lands in a New Zealand theatre.

Stage 1

Pre-assessment

The patient is worked up before the day — history, medications, airway, comorbidities — so that surprises happen here rather than in the anaesthetic room.

Anaesthetists · pre-assessment nursing · the referring team
Stage 2

The anaesthetic room

Where anaesthetic technicians and anaesthetists work most closely: equipment checked, drugs drawn, monitoring on, airway secured.

Anaesthetists · anaesthetic technicians
Stage 3

Theatre

The list itself. Position, monitoring, drugs and equipment managed continuously while the surgical team works.

Anaesthetists · anaesthetic technicians · surgical and nursing teams
Stage 4

Recovery

Emergence, airway, pain and observation until the patient is safe to move — a handover that has to be complete rather than quick.

Recovery nursing · anaesthetists
Stage 5

Ward or discharge

Back to the ward, or home the same day for day-surgery lists, with the plan handed on to whoever picks up the care.

Ward and day-surgery teams · the patient and their whānau

The list only runs if the whole team is there. That is why theatre recruitment is a team problem, not a vacancy.

Where the work happens

Where might that list be?

A theatre is not a theatre. The size of the hospital decides your case mix, how specialised your lists are, how often you are on call and how much of the equipment you are expected to know cold.

Tertiary theatres

Cardiac, neuro, paediatric, transplant and major trauma, concentrated in a handful of cities. Deep specialisation, and the complex end of the equipment list.

Regional & secondary hospitals

General, orthopaedic and obstetric lists with a much broader personal case mix, a smaller team, and acute work that reaches you rather than somebody more senior.

Private surgical hospitals

Elective lists, predictable finishing times, different team structures and terms outside the collective agreement. Many people hold both a public and a private commitment.

Day surgery

High throughput, short cases, fast turnarounds — and a working week that fits around a family more reliably than an acute roster does.

Rural & remote lists

Small theatres a long way from a tertiary centre, where the team is three or four people and the transfer plan matters as much as the anaesthetic.

Obstetric & emergency cover

Out-of-hours work for maternity, the emergency department and critical care — in smaller hospitals this is a substantial part of the job rather than an occasional call.

Auckland, Wellington, Palmerston North and Whangārei are not the same job

Two theatre posts with the same title, four hours apart, can differ more than two posts in different specialties. Before you shortlist a city, ask what is on the list, how many of you there are, and how far the nearest tertiary centre is.

The practical side

Registration, pay and your move

Registration

Anaesthetic technicians register with the Medical Sciences Council; anaesthetists with the Medical Council. Either way it is your scope that is assessed, not only your certificate. Registration is the critical path, and for an unregulated overseas title the evidence-gathering is the slow part, so gather it before you look at jobs.

Check your pathway →

Pay

Public-sector theatre pay is set by a national collective agreement, so your base salary comes from a published scale. Your step, your hours and your on-call commitment move the total. Private and ACC-funded work sits outside the agreement.

What you could earn →

Immigration

Most clinicians move on a work visa tied to an accredited employer. Anaesthetists, registered nurses and anaesthetic technicians all sit in Tier 1 of the Green List (as at 9 March 2026) — but the anaesthetic technician entry names a specific New Zealand qualification, so an overseas ODP should read the current entry before assuming it applies. The visa can usually run alongside registration.
