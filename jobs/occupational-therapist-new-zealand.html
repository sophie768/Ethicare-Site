In depth

The detail, when you want it

Registration, pay, the work itself and life outside it. Everything is here, one section at a time.

Why New ZealandA system that backs rehabilitation

For occupational therapists, New Zealand offers something rare: manageable caseloads, real autonomy, and ACC-model funding that lets you focus on helping people recover and return to life.

  • Manageable, person-centred caseloads
  • Genuine clinical autonomy
  • A system built around rehabilitation
"The system here backs rehabilitation."

Add shorter commutes and the outdoors close by, and the move tends to give back time and headspace as well as a different way of working.

What the work is actually likeACC pays for rehabilitation directly, which shapes the whole caseload

Occupational therapy travels well as a clinical skill. What is different here is who pays for the plan, and where the work happens.

ACC funds rehabilitation, not just treatment. New Zealand's no-fault accident scheme pays for OT assessment and intervention directly — equipment, home modifications, vocational rehabilitation, return-to-work planning — for anyone injured here, however it happened. In practice that means funding decisions move faster than you are probably used to, injury-related work makes up a bigger share of most caseloads, and a great deal of OT happens outside hospital walls because someone is paying for it to.

Community and home-based practice is the centre of the profession here, not the overflow. You will spend real time in people's houses, workplaces and schools, and you will usually carry your own caseload with the authority to make the call. That is more autonomy than many OTs arrive with. It also means more driving, more lone working, and more of your clinical reasoning happening in someone's kitchen rather than in a team room.

Assessment is whānau-centred, and that is a clinical difference. Te Tiriti o Waitangi obligations and Māori models of health — Te Whare Tapa Whā most commonly — sit inside the competencies you are assessed against rather than alongside them as training. You will formulate around a household, not only an individual. For most OTs arriving from overseas this is the new part of the job, and it changes the plan, not just the paperwork.

"The funding follows the person, so the plan can actually happen."

That is the part OTs tell us they had stopped expecting: recommending what someone needs and watching it arrive. It is not frictionless, and the caseloads are real. But the system is pointed in the same direction you are.

Getting registeredRegistration overview

Reviewed July 2026 · checked against the Occupational Therapy Board of New Zealand’s current published guidance

To practise in New Zealand you'll register with the Occupational Therapy Board of New Zealand (OTBNZ). For most internationally trained OTs the process is well-trodden, and we’ll help you through it.

The Board assesses your qualification, experience and competency against New Zealand standards. As part of the process you'll typically need:

  • Verification of your qualification
  • Evidence of recent clinical practice
  • A police / criminal-history clearance
  • Confirmation of English-language proficiency, if required

Once registered, you’ll hold an Annual Practising Certificate (APC). We support you from pathway to placement.

Registration checklist

What to gather before you apply to the OTBNZ:

  • Certified copies of your qualifications
  • Evidence of recent, relevant clinical practice
  • A certificate of good standing from every authority you’ve registered with
  • A police / criminal-history check
  • Proof of identity, plus any name-change documents
  • English-language evidence (IELTS or OET), if required
  • One personal and one professional referee
  • Your application fee, ready at submission

Advice & notes

Start early

Gathering documents before you apply is the single best way to avoid delays. Missing paperwork is the most common hold-up.

Mind the dates

Certificates of good standing and certified copies have a short validity window, so time them so they’re still current when you submit.

Then your visa

Apply for your visa once you have a job offer. We’ll guide the order so nothing happens out of sequence.

You’re not on your own

We’ll review your documents with you before you submit, and stay alongside you through every step.

Scope of practiceA broad, flexible scope with real decision-making authority

New Zealand OTs work across physical and mental health, rehabilitation, community, paediatric and ACC-funded settings, and usually with more independence than in a heavily tiered service.

You will typically hold your own caseload, own the outcomes, and make independent clinical decisions about assessment, intervention and discharge. Most OTs build depth in an area over time while keeping a generalist base underneath it.

Equipment and home modification work carries more weight here. Prescribing equipment and recommending housing modifications is a core part of the job rather than a referral you make to someone else, funded through ACC or through Ministry of Health pathways depending on the person. There is still assessment and accountability, but the decision is far more often yours.

Vocational rehabilitation and workplace assessment are a bigger share of practice than in systems where nobody funds them — return-to-work planning, workplace ergonomic assessment and graduated return are routine ACC-funded work rather than a specialist niche.

Sensory-based practice is well embedded, particularly in mental health and paediatrics, and often more so than in the services OTs arrive from. If sensory regulation is an interest, there is a genuine community of practice here.

Hand therapy, neuro rehabilitation, paediatrics, mental health and older-person services all have established specialist routes. Tell us the work you want and we will be honest about where it exists.

Your role varies by setting, but most OTs enjoy a varied caseload and a high degree of autonomy.

OTs often aren’t sure how their experience will translate. In reality the transition is usually smooth: the standard of practice aligns closely, and ACC makes the rehab focus even clearer.

There can be a short adjustment while you get familiar with ACC and a new system, but it’s usually brief, and OTs tend to move quickly into highly autonomous work.

Common questions

Frequently asked

Do I need to be an ACC-approved provider?

It depends entirely on how you are working. If you are employed by a public service or an established provider, their contracting with ACC covers the work and you do not arrange anything yourself — you will simply notice that funding for equipment and rehab arrives from a different place than you are used to.

If you are planning to work privately and invoice ACC directly, that does mean meeting their provider requirements, and it is worth understanding properly before you set anything up. Tell us which you are considering and we will explain what actually applies.

Does my UK or South African experience count?

Yes. The Board assesses your qualification, your recent practice and your competency against New Zealand standards, and training from the UK, South Africa, Ireland, Australia and comparable systems is well recognised. Most internationally qualified OTs come through this route without difficulty.

The adjustment when you arrive is rarely clinical. It is the funding system, the amount of community work, and whānau-centred assessment — not your ability to do the job.

What does Te Tiriti practice actually mean day to day?

Practical things, mostly. Involving whānau in assessment and planning as a matter of course rather than on request. Working with Te Whare Tapa Whā as a model that includes spiritual and family wellbeing alongside the physical. Getting names and pronunciation right. Asking about a household rather than assuming it.

Employers provide induction and ongoing support, and nobody expects you to arrive fluent. They do expect you to arrive willing, and it will come up at interview.

Public or private — which gives the better caseload?

Different rather than better. Public gives you structured teams, supervision on tap, breadth of presentation and a clear progression path. ACC-funded private practice gives you more autonomy, more focused rehabilitation work, faster access to funding, and often more control over your diary.

A lot of OTs here do some of both over time, and moving between them is normal rather than a career reset.

How does equipment and home modification funding work?

Through two main routes: ACC for anything injury-related, and Ministry of Health pathways for need arising from disability or illness. Both are assessment-driven, and as the assessing OT you carry considerably more of the decision than in a panel-based system.

It is not unlimited and it is not instant. But the gap between what you recommend and what your client receives is much smaller than most OTs are used to.

Is there specialist work — hand therapy, sensory, neuro?

Yes, all three, with established routes into them. Hand therapy has its own strong professional community. Sensory practice is well embedded in mental health and paediatrics. Neuro rehabilitation runs across inpatient and community services.

Specialisation here tends to be built on a generalist base rather than entered straight from training, so if you are already specialised, tell us early — those roles are fewer and worth timing properly.
