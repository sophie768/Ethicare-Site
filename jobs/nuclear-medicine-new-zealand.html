What the work is likeThe physics travels. The scope boundary does not.

Nuclear medicine practice here will feel familiar from your first list. What differs is that the scope you register in defines what you may do — and in hybrid imaging that boundary sits in an unusually specific place.

Departments

Small teams, and you are not one of forty

Most services run a small technologist group across a gamma camera and a PET/CT, so you would know the physicists, the radiologists and the radiopharmacy staff by name. Protocol decisions are a conversation rather than a directive from a central team.

The scope

Your scope is the job

The Board registers you into a defined scope and that scope sets what you may do. If a role asks for work outside it, that is a registration question, not something a manager can sign off. It is a real cultural difference from systems where local sign-off is enough.

Breadth

Therapy alongside diagnostics

The scope covers imaging with or without sealed sources, so therapeutic work sits inside it rather than beside it. In smaller services you would see the whole caseload rather than a slice of it.

The CT question is the one to settle before you accept anything. The Board’s competence standards are explicit: nuclear medicine practitioners operate gamma camera SPECT/CT and PET/CT systems, and the CT within that is for attenuation correction, anatomical fusion and transmission imaging. That is what the scope covers.

Diagnostic CT is separate, and needs one of three things. A practising certificate in the medical imaging technologist scope; completion of a Board-approved education programme in CT; or evidence of CT competency obtained elsewhere that the Board deems satisfactory and approves. If a job description implies you would report diagnostic CT off the hybrid scanner, that is a registration conversation and we have it in writing first.

Trans-Tasman recognition is on your side here. Mutual recognition covers the medical imaging technologist, radiation therapist and nuclear medicine technologist scopes. MRI and sonography are excluded — so if you hold more than one of these, your scopes may arrive on different timelines. Worth planning for rather than discovering.

Getting registeredRegistration is with the MRTB, in the nuclear medicine scope

Reviewed August 2026 · checked against the Medical Radiation Technologists Board’s own published guidance

You register with the Medical Radiation Technologists Board in the Nuclear Medicine Technologist scope. The Board assesses your overseas qualification alongside your clinical experience, for the scope you name on the application.

If you are registered in Australia, say so first. Trans-Tasman mutual recognition covers this scope, which changes what is assessed and usually what it costs you in time. It is the first question we ask.

Otherwise, allow up to 12 weeks where a full international qualification assessment is required, counted from a complete application with the fee paid. Incomplete applications are the usual reason a timeline slips.

An examination is not automatic. Where the Board decides a qualification is not equivalent, it may in some circumstances offer an examination — but whether one is offered is the Board’s decision on your application, not a right you can plan around.

There is an experience-based route, and we will not print a threshold for it. The Gazette notice we work from contains no verbatim full-time-equivalent figure, so any number here would be invented. We ask the Board the question for your actual history instead.

Board fees are published and verified: NZ$922 to register, NZ$510 for the initial annual practising certificate, and NZ$2,704 for the online examination where the Board requires it.

What the Board will want to see:

  • A qualification the Board has assessed for the nuclear medicine scope, or Australian registration under trans-Tasman recognition
  • Evidence of nuclear medicine clinical practice meeting the Board’s requirements
  • Separate evidence for diagnostic CT, if the role requires it
  • Certificates of good standing from every authority you have registered with
  • Identity and English-language evidence where required
  • A practising certificate application once you are resident, or can evidence the move

The order matters more than the speed. The scope decision comes first, because it determines what is assessed. The qualification assessment, or the recognition route, runs next. The practising certificate is last by design, since it depends on your move — you must be resident in New Zealand, or able to evidence it, and you cannot practise in a protected scope without both registration and a current certificate. The visa runs alongside all of it rather than after it.

Where this comes from. The scope boundary, the CT routes and the hybrid-imaging wording are the Board’s Competence Standards for Medical Imaging and Radiation Therapy Practitioners in Aotearoa New Zealand (19 July 2024), Domain 5. Scopes, trans-Tasman coverage, the examination policy, the internationally qualified processing time and the practising-certificate timing are the Board’s own published pages and its Gazette scope notice, read in August 2026. Fees are the Board’s published schedule. Check your own case with the Board before you make a decision on it.

Life in New ZealandA small department, and a country you can reach on a Saturday.

A short commute, and the water or the hills at the end of it.

Nuclear medicine is a small profession here, and that cuts both ways: fewer departments to move between, but a working life where you are known rather than numbered. Services are busy without being industrial, and the day ends when the list does.

Most hospitals sit within half an hour of open water, a river or a mountain, so a weekend does not need planning weeks ahead. For families that is usually the argument that settles it.

A small profession, in a country where that means being known.

Where you could workWhere the nuclear medicine posts are

Nuclear medicine is more concentrated than general imaging, because hybrid scanners and radiopharmacy need scale, so the geography is narrower and worth understanding before you set your heart on a town.

Tertiary centres

PET/CT, therapeutic work and a larger technologist group to share the roster and the on-call between.

Base hospitals

Gamma camera and SPECT/CT services covering a regional referral base, often with a small team and broad exposure.

Dual scope

Where you also hold the medical imaging technologist scope, CT and general imaging can sit alongside — two scopes, not one.

Private imaging

PET/CT in the larger centres, generally with a more predictable day than a public roster.

Common questions

Frequently asked

Does my Australian registration carry across?

Yes, for this scope. Trans-Tasman mutual recognition covers the nuclear medicine technologist scope, alongside medical imaging technologist and radiation therapist — so an Australian-registered NMT has a quicker route than a colleague in MRI or sonography, which are excluded.

You still register and hold a current practising certificate before you practise. Recognition changes what is assessed, not whether you apply.

Can I report the CT on my SPECT/CT and PET/CT?

Not as diagnostic CT, not automatically. The Board’s competence standards put attenuation correction, anatomical fusion and transmission imaging inside the nuclear medicine scope — the CT that makes the hybrid study work.

Diagnostic CT is a separate matter and needs one of three things: a practising certificate in the medical imaging technologist scope, completion of a Board-approved CT education programme, or evidence of CT competency obtained elsewhere that the Board deems satisfactory. This is the single thing hybrid-imaging candidates most often get wrong.

I came to nuclear medicine through experience rather than a dedicated degree. Is there a route?

There is an experience-based route, and we deliberately do not print a threshold for it — the Gazette notice we work from contains no verbatim FTE figure, so any number we gave you would be invented.

What we do instead is ask the Board the question for your particular history before you spend anything. That is a short email and it beats a guess.

When can I apply for my practising certificate?

Not until you are resident in New Zealand, or can provide evidence of your move. And you cannot practise in a protected scope without both registration and a current practising certificate — two things, not one.

It is the sequencing detail that most often costs a start date, so we build the timeline backwards from it.
