Where you fit

Not whether you can register — which pathway you register on

For doctors, the question is rarely whether you can register — it is which pathway you register on, and that depends on where you trained and which College assesses your specialty.

That assessment is the part worth understanding before anything else, because it sets the timeline for everything that follows. We write a guide for a specialty once we are recruiting into it properly; the others are a conversation rather than a page.

The shape of the system

One patient, five levels of care

Doctors moving here usually ask about registration first and structure second. It is worth doing it the other way round — because the shape of New Zealand medicine, and how few layers sit between you and the patient, is the part that actually changes your working life.

The country is small enough that the same consultant may see a patient in clinic, operate on them, take the call about them at two in the morning and write to their GP afterwards. Sub-specialty depth is thinner than in a large system. Generalist breadth is expected, and valued.

Level 1

Primary care

General practice is the front door and, for most people, the long-term relationship. Referrals arrive from someone you may know by name.

General practice · practice nursing · community pharmacy
Level 2

Acute & emergency

Smaller departments, consultants on the floor, and retrieval across long distances as a routine part of acute care rather than an exception.

Emergency medicine · anaesthesia · retrieval · nursing
Level 3

Specialist inpatient care

Generalist breadth inside a specialty, fewer layers of juniors, and decisions that reach you sooner than you may be used to.

Senior medical officers · registrars and RMOs · nursing · allied health · imaging

A great deal of the work happens after discharge — community teams, district nursing, aged residential care and accident-funded rehabilitation.

Rehabilitation medicine · allied health · community nursing · aged care

What surprises most consultants

You will do more of your own work. Fewer registrars, thinner sub-specialty cover, and a service that expects you to be a generalist inside your specialty. Most doctors describe that as the best part of the job and the hardest month of the first year.

The second surprise is the size of the professional community. You are likely to meet most of the other consultants in your specialty nationally inside a year, which makes the multidisciplinary meeting less formal and considerably more useful.

Where the work happens

Where might that work happen?

Not everything is a tertiary centre, and the distance between a tertiary post, a regional hospital and a rural generalist role is much larger here than the job titles suggest.

Location is not a lifestyle footnote in New Zealand medicine. It sets your case mix, your on-call, your sub-specialty support and how far away the nearest scanner is.

Group one

Hospital medicine

Where most New Zealand hospital medicine actually happens — and it is not where most people assume.

Regional & secondary hospitals

Broader case mix, less sub-specialty support, a team small enough that everyone knows each other, and clinical decisions you make rather than escalate. A good number of consultants arrive intending to take a tertiary post and stay in one of these instead.

Often around you: SMO colleagues across specialties · registrars and RMOs · nursing · allied health · imaging · the local GPs

Tertiary centres

Sub-specialty work, complex referrals, teaching and research, concentrated in a handful of cities — and with the national service responsibility that goes with it.

Private practice

Elective work, often alongside a public appointment. Different pace, different income, and a caseload you have more say over.

Group two

Outside the hospital

Careers where the working week looks nothing like a ward round.

General practice & community

Longer consultations and genuine continuity of care, with a business model attached to the clinical one. A different career, not a lesser one.

Rural generalist

Broad scope, real autonomy, and distance as a clinical factor in every decision. Deeply rewarding, and worth planning the professional isolation for rather than discovering it.

Retrieval, transport & telehealth

Geography makes this core rather than niche — air and road retrieval, and telehealth that genuinely substitutes for a clinic.

Aged residential care

An increasing share of medicine, mostly outside hospital walls, and one of the quieter routes into a portfolio career.

Group three

Beyond direct care

Clinical careers where the patient is a population, a cohort or a student.

Māori & Pacific health providers

Kaupapa Māori and Pacific organisations delivering care on their own terms, with whānau at the centre of the model rather than at its edge.

Public health & population health

Prevention, screening, outbreak and policy work — a clinical career with the patient defined as a population.

Academic, teaching & research

Joint appointments with the medical schools, trials work and supervision, usually alongside clinical sessions rather than instead of them.

Locum & fractional work

A legitimate way to try the country, and a specialty, before committing your family to either. We will tell you honestly when it is the better first step.

Public

Public hospital services

Unselected acute work, the full multidisciplinary system, collective-agreement pay that is the same wherever in the country you do the job, and the on-call commitment that comes with it.

Private & mixed

Private & mixed practice

Elective lists, more control over your week, and income outside the agreement. Many consultants hold both. Public or private is not the same job with a different employer — it changes your case mix, your autonomy and what you are responsible for.

Location is a clinical decision

Two hospitals four hours apart can offer the same job title and a completely different week. Before you shortlist a city, it is worth asking what the on-call looks like and how far the nearest tertiary centre is.

Across the family

What applies across medicine

The things that are the same whichever of these specialties you hold.

Who regulates this work?+

The Medical Council of New Zealand registers all doctors. For specialist recognition, the relevant College assesses whether your training is comparable — that finding, not the Council, is usually what determines your pathway.

Registration is the first gate, and it runs on its own timeline.

What does the pay look like?+

Public-system pay is set by collective agreement rather than negotiated role by role, so the same job pays the same wherever in the country you do it. Private and ACC-funded work sits outside that and varies.

Indicative bands sit on each profession’s own guide above.

Which visa would I need?+

Most clinicians move on a work visa tied to an accredited employer, and several of these specialties sit on the Green List, which changes the route to residence considerably.

The visa can usually run in parallel with registration rather than after it.

What about the rest of the move?+

Schools, housing, healthcare, banking, shipping and the first few weeks are the same for every profession, so we explain them once rather than on each page.
