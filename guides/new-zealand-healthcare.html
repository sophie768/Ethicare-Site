The big picture

A publicly funded system, built on care for everyone

New Zealand’s health system is largely public, funded through taxation, and free or low-cost for residents and many people on longer work visas. The principle behind it is deeply held: good healthcare should not depend on your bank balance.

For you, this matters in two ways. As a new resident, it shapes how you and your family will access care. And as a healthcare professional, it shapes the system you’ll practise within: its values, its structure and its priorities. A little orientation now makes both feel far less unfamiliar.

One reassurance up front: the fundamentals will feel recognisable to anyone from a comparable system. The differences are mostly in the details, a no-fault accident scheme, the way primary care works, and a strong, explicit commitment to equity.

How it’s structured

The parts that make up the system

A handful of pieces fit together to deliver care. Understanding who does what makes the whole thing much easier to navigate, for your family and in your work.

The public system

Health New Zealand | Te Whatu Ora

The national organisation that runs publicly funded health services, hospitals, specialist and community care, across the country. Recent reforms brought services under one national body to make care more consistent wherever you live.

Primary care

GPs & community services

General practitioners are the front door to most healthcare. You enrol with a local practice, and your GP handles everyday care and referrals onward to hospital or specialist services when needed.

Accident cover

ACC, the no-fault scheme

A uniquely New Zealand feature: a national scheme that covers treatment for injuries from accidents, for everyone, regardless of fault, including visitors. More on this below.

The private system

Private hospitals & insurance

A private sector runs alongside the public one. Many people hold private health insurance for faster access to elective procedures and more choice, while relying on the public system for emergencies and complex care.

The one that surprises people

ACC: accident cover for everyone

ACC is a no-fault scheme covering treatment and rehabilitation for accidental injury, for residents and visitors alike. In return you generally cannot sue for personal injury — one of the things newcomers find most unfamiliar.

No-fault

If you’re injured in an accident, you’re covered regardless of who was to blame, at work, on the road, on the ski field or at home.

For everyone

Cover extends to everyone in New Zealand, including international visitors and people on any visa, not only residents.

Treatment & recovery

It helps with treatment costs, and can contribute to rehabilitation and lost income while you recover from a covered injury.

Everyday healthcare

Getting care, day to day

For most things, your journey starts with your GP. Here’s how the everyday side works, the practical bits you’ll want sorted in your first weeks.

Enrol with a GP practice

One of the first things to do on arrival. Enrolling makes everyday visits cheaper and gives you a regular doctor who knows your family’s history.

GP visits carry a part-charge

Primary care is subsidised rather than free for adults, so you pay a part-charge per visit. Care for children is heavily subsidised, and often free.

Prescriptions are subsidised

Pharmac, a government agency, decides which medicines are publicly funded, keeping the cost of most prescriptions low at the pharmacy counter.

Hospital & emergency care

Public hospital and emergency treatment is free for eligible residents. In a genuine emergency, call 111 for an ambulance.

Healthline, any time

A free 24/7 phone line staffed by nurses for health advice when you’re unsure whether to see a doctor, useful in the early days before you have a regular GP.

After-hours & urgent care

Urgent care clinics handle the things that can’t wait for a GP appointment but aren’t emergencies, handy to know where your nearest one is.

Told straight

What healthcare costs

The honest summary: the big, frightening costs are covered, while everyday primary care carries modest charges. Here’s the shape of it for an eligible resident.

Public hospital & emergency
Free for eligible residents and citizens, including surgery, specialist and emergency care.
Accident-related treatment
Largely covered by ACC for everyone, including visitors, with some part-charges depending on the care.
GP visits
A subsidised part-charge per visit for adults; care for children is heavily subsidised and frequently free.
Prescriptions
Low cost for funded medicines, thanks to Pharmac subsidies at the pharmacy.
Dental & optical (adults)
Mostly private and paid for out of pocket, though dental care is free for children and teenagers.
Private healthcare
Optional: private insurance buys faster access to elective procedures and more choice, on top of public cover.

Exact charges vary by practice, region and your eligibility, and the rules can change. Always confirm current detail with the practice or the official sources below. Ethicare are recruitment specialists, not a health authority.

Two questions everyone asks

Dental care, and whether you need private insurance

Two of the most common questions, because both work differently here. The short version: dental is mostly private for adults, and private health insurance is a choice rather than a must.

Dental · children

Free for under-18s

Basic dental care is free for children and teenagers up to their 18th birthday, through school dental services and enrolled community providers. It’s one of the quieter things families appreciate here.

Dental · adults

Mostly private & out of pocket

Adult dental isn’t generally publicly funded, so you pay privately, check-ups, fillings and the rest. Costs vary between practices, so it’s worth comparing and booking a first check-up once you’ve settled.

Dental · the exceptions

Accidents, emergencies & hardship

Dental treatment for an accidental injury is covered by ACC, public hospitals handle some acute and emergency dental, and limited grants exist for people on low incomes. Outside those, plan for adult dental as a private cost.

Private insurance

Optional, not a necessity

You don’t need private health insurance to be cared for: the public system covers emergency, hospital and essential care for eligible residents, and ACC covers accidents for everyone. Many people still take out cover for faster elective surgery, more choice of specialist, and sometimes dental and optical extras, and some employers offer it as a benefit. It sits on top of the public system, it doesn’t replace it.

Eligibility, funding and grants change over time and depend on your visa and circumstances, confirm the current detail with a practice or the official sources below. Ethicare are recruitment specialists, not a health authority.

“Optional” depends on what you are used to

Everything above is written as though not holding cover were the obvious default. For a clinician arriving from the UK or Ireland, it is. For a great many of the people we place, it is not, and this is the part worth saying plainly.

If you are coming from South Africa, a medical aid scheme is not a luxury upgrade at home. It is close to how a professional household gets care at all, the premium is a fixed and substantial line in the monthly budget, and stopping it would be unthinkable. The same is broadly true arriving from the United States, and from parts of the Gulf and Asia where employer-provided cover is the mechanism rather than a benefit.

So we watch people arrive here and immediately buy a comprehensive family policy, because that is what a responsible parent does where they come from. Often they did not need to, and nobody told them.

The reason it is different here is not that New Zealand insurance is better. It is that the two things insurance protects you against elsewhere are already covered: acute and emergency hospital care is provided by the public system for eligible residents, and anything caused by an accident is covered by ACC for everyone in the country, including visitors and people on any visa. The catastrophic scenarios. The crash, the sudden admission, the injury on a ski field, are the ones you are already covered for.

And one difference from Australia that matters if you are weighing both countries. Australia gives higher earners a tax reason to hold private hospital cover; New Zealand gives none. How the Australian system works sets out that mechanism, and Australia versus New Zealand compares the two properly.

The practical consequence is this: advice that is sound in Sydney is wrong in Wellington. Here the decision is purely about what you want, not about what you will otherwise be charged.

None of which makes cover pointless. Waiting times for elective procedures in the public system are real and can be long, and that is precisely what a policy buys you, a knee done in weeks rather than months, your choice of specialist, a private room. It buys time and choice. It does not buy safety, because you already have that.

Our honest suggestion: arrive, register with a GP, and give it three months before you decide. You will be in a far better position to judge what you actually want to insure once you have used the system rather than imagined it. If your employer offers cover as a benefit, take it. That is different, and it is free.

Sophie’s insight

The clinicians we place often tell me the system here lets them practise the way they always wanted to, with time to actually know their patients and a team that pulls together. The bits that feel foreign at first, like ACC or enrolling with a GP, become second nature within weeks. My advice is to get your family enrolled with a local practice early, and to come curious about the emphasis on equity and Māori health. It’s one of the things that makes practising here feel meaningful.

One honest caveat, though: “a team that pulls together” is true of many of the places our clinicians land, not a promise about every one. Team culture varies as much between workplaces here as it does anywhere — so it’s worth asking what a particular team and department are really like when you interview, and talking to people already doing the job.

SophieCEO & Founder, Ethicare Resourcing
